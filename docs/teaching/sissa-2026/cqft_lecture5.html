Abstract

The last two lectures construct the massive \(\Phi^4_3\) measure on the three-dimensional torus with the forward–backward equations of Lecture 4. In this lecture we set up the problem. For the mass regulator the Polchinski process of Lecture 4 is the linear estimator \(C_q P_q\) built from noisy observations of the field, and its forecast is their posterior mean; this gives an exact FBSDE at every lattice cutoff. Its backward half is governed by the Wetterich equation. We cannot solve that equation, so we construct an explicit approximate effective average action and write the exact FBSDE in the coordinates it defines: the approximate forecast \(Y\) and the error \(Z\). The error is driven only by the failure of the approximate action to solve the Wetterich equation. We explain why the approximate action needs two renormalisations, in two senses: a linearly and a logarithmically divergent mass counterterm, and two correction steps beyond one loop, which make the defect integrable in the backward equation. Finally we identify the large-field problem: the construction only controls fields of size \(r_q = (q / \gamma)^{1 / 2}\), and must be extended to all fields without losing coercivity. The price is an error that is not small but lives on large fields. Lecture 6 controls it by a priori estimates on the FBSDE and removes the cutoffs.

Introduction

Lecture 4 formulated a programme (Figure 7.1 and the remark following it): solve the FBSDE

\(\displaystyle \Phi_t = \int_0^t \dot{Q}_s F_s \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s + \int_0^t \dot{Q}_s^{1 / 2} \mathrm{d} W_s, \qquad F_t =\mathbb{E}_t [- DV_T (\Phi_T)],\)

with a renormalised interaction \(V_T\), with estimates uniform in the ultraviolet cutoff, and take the limit. For \(\Phi^4_3\) the force \(F_t\) is not expected to converge to an integrable terminal value, and the naive estimates of the force diverge (Appendix A.2 of Lecture 4). In these two lectures we carry out a version of the programme on the unit torus \(\mathbb{T}^3\). The guiding principle is the one of Section 8: do not estimate \(\Phi\) and \(F\) directly, but pass to the forecast coordinate, in which the forward equation has no drift and the tree diagrams have been integrated exactly. Section 12.4 shows on one site what goes wrong with a frozen cube, and what integrating it gives.

The argument has three parts, over two lectures.

  1. Deterministic renormalisation (this lecture). Construct an explicit approximate effective average action \(\bar{\Gamma}_q\) and a counterterm, by a finite number of steps of the Wetterich flow, and estimate its defect.

  2. A priori estimates (Lecture 6). Write the exact FBSDE in the coordinates of \(\bar{\Gamma}_q\), turn the forward equation into the field equation of Lecture 4 with its sub-Gaussian noise, and estimate it together with the backward equation, uniformly in the lattice.

  3. Removing the cutoffs (Lecture 6). Remove the upper scale at fixed lattice, then the lattice, and identify the limiting FBSDE and its terminal law.

The emphasis is on the structure of the argument. We give complete proofs of the identities and of the scalar models of the estimates, and only describe the tensor estimates on the lattice. The lectures follow the course notes [FBSDE26], where the estimates of this lecture are proved; Lecture 6 takes a shorter forward route than those notes, which use the memory form of its Exercise 13.3. The point of view is that of [BCG26]; for other constructions of \(\Phi^4_3\) see [GJ73, FO76, BFS83, BG20, GH21].

Origin of the results. The results of Lectures 5–6 are new. They were obtained recently, with the use of AI, in collaboration with Sarah-Jean Meyer. Detailed proofs of what these lectures cite or condense are in the Supplement.

What is proved. For every coupling \(\lambda > 0\) and every mass \(m^2 = q_0 > 0\), on a fixed torus, with no smallness, and for an explicit counterterm whose finite part \(r \in \mathbb{R}\) is arbitrary: the whole lattice sequence converges to a measure \(\nu\), which is written as an explicit density over an explicit SDE; the forecast processes converge to the solution of an FBSDE started from \(Y_{q_0} = 0\), driven by a Brownian motion obtained as the limit of the lattice innovations; the forecast is a continuous function of the observation; \(\nu\) has a renormalised variational formula and quartic exponential tails. It does not prove uniqueness for the vector FBSDE without its scalar form, strong solutions, the Osterwalder–Schrader axioms in full, or an infinite-volume limit.

One auxiliary scale. The number \(q_0 > 0\) fixes the Gaussian reference \(C = C_{q_0}\); it may be small, for instance \(q_0 = 1\). The expansion of the action needs \(g_q\) small, and this happens above an auxiliary scale \(q_{\ast}\), depending on \(\lambda\), \(q_0\) and on the finite part; we always take \(q_{\ast} \geqslant 1\). No statement about \(\nu\) depends on it. Below it the coefficients are frozen (Section 12.5), so the action, the system and the results hold at all scales. Since the measure \(\nu_N\) depends on \(q_0\) and \(\mu_N\) only through \(q_0 + \mu_N\), the finite part of the mass stays free.

10The problem and the exact FBSDE

10.1Lattice, scales and notation

Let \(N\) be an odd integer and \(\Lambda_N = (N^{- 1} \mathbb{Z}/\mathbb{Z})^3\) the periodic lattice of mesh \(N^{- 1}\) in the unit torus. Let \(H_N =\mathbb{R}^{\Lambda_N}\) with

\(\displaystyle \langle u, v \rangle = \int uv, \qquad \int f := N^{- 3} \sum_{x \in \Lambda_N} f (x),\)

so that the volume is \(1\) and integrals approximate continuum integrals. The nearest-neighbour Laplacian is \(\Delta_N u (x) = N^2 \sum_{i = 1}^3 [u (x + e_i / N) + u (x - e_i / N) - 2 u (x)]\); we usually drop the index \(N\). A kernel \(C (x, z)\) acts by \(Cu (x) = \int C (x, z) u (z) \mathrm{d} z\), so the identity has kernel \(N^3 \mathbb{1}_{x = z}\), and

\(\displaystyle \text{Tr} C = \int C (x, x) \mathrm{d} x, \qquad (\text{diag} C) (x) = C (x, x), \qquad \|C\|_{\text{row}} = \sup_x \int |C (x, z) | \mathrm{d} z.\)

\(M_f\) is multiplication by \(f\), and \(K + \gamma y^2\) means \(K + \gamma M_{y^2}\). For a functional \(F\) on \(H_N\), \(DF\), \(D^2 F\), \(D^k F\) are its gradient and derivatives, as in Lecture 3. \(\|u\|_p\) is the \(L^p\) norm for the measure \(\int\).

The resolution parameter.
Lecture 3 used a general covariance profile. Here we use the mass-regulator profile of Exercise 8.9 of Lecture 4: with \(m^2 = q_0\) and \(t \geqslant 0\),

\(\displaystyle \hat{Q}_t (k) = \frac{1}{|k|^2 + q_0} - \frac{1}{|k|^2 + q_0 + t^2}, \qquad \mathcal{R}_t = t^2 .\)

It is convenient to parametrise the resolution by the squared regulator mass

\(\displaystyle q := q_0 + t^2 \in [q_0, \infty) .\)

Set

\(\displaystyle K_q := q - \Delta_N, \qquad C_q := K_q^{- 1}, \qquad C := C_{q_0} = (m^2 - \Delta_N)^{- 1} .\) (10.1)

In the notation of Lecture 3, with \(T = \infty\) and using \(q\) instead of \(t\) as the resolution variable (so \(\dot{Q}\) now means \(\partial_q Q_q\)),

\(\displaystyle Q_q = C - C_q, \qquad \partial_q Q_q = C_q^2, \qquad \dot{Q}^{1 / 2} = C_q, \qquad S_q = C_q, \qquad \mathcal{R}_q = q - q_0, \qquad \partial_q \mathcal{R}_q = 1.\) (10.2)

So \(C_q\) is the covariance of the scales not yet resolved, and \(K_q = S_q^{- 1}\) is the precision of the posterior at resolution \(q\) under the free prior \(\mu^C\). The main properties of \(C_q\) are elementary.

Lemma 10.1. (Free resolvents) For \(q \geqslant q_0\) (constants depend on the lower bound only for scales below one), uniformly in \(N\): \(C_q\) has a nonnegative kernel, \(C_q \mathbb{1} = q^{- 1} \mathbb{1}\), \(\|C_q \|_{p \to p} = q^{- 1}\) for \(1 \leqslant p \leqslant \infty\), and

\(\displaystyle a_2 (q) := (C_q^2) (x, x) = \text{Tr} C_q^2 \lesssim q^{- 1 / 2} \wedge N^3 q^{- 2}, \qquad \text{Tr} C_q \lesssim N \wedge N^3 q^{- 1} .\) (10.3)

For \(y \in H_N\) and \(\gamma > 0\), the killed resolvent \(G_q (y) = (K_q + \gamma y^2)^{- 1}\) satisfies \(0 \leqslant G_q (y) (x, z) \leqslant C_q (x, z)\).

Proof. \(C_q = \int_0^{\infty} \mathrm{e}^{- qs} \mathrm{e}^{s \Delta} \mathrm{d} s\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{s \Delta}\) is the transition kernel of the lattice random walk. This gives positivity and the row sums. Killing the walk at rate \(\gamma y^2\) gives the comparison for \(G_q\). The symbol of \(- \Delta_N\) is comparable to \(|k|^2\) on the centred frequency cube \(n \in \{- \frac{N - 1}{2}, \ldots, \frac{N - 1}{2} \}^3\) (symbol \(4 N^2 \sum_i \sin^2 (\pi n_i / N)\), with \(k = 2 \pi n\)), and \(\sum_k (q + |k|^2)^{- 2} \leqslant C \int_{\mathbb{R}^3} (q + |k|^2)^{- 2} \mathrm{d} k = Cq^{- 1 / 2}\). Counting the \(N^3\) modes gives the other bounds.\(\Box\)

The bubble \(a_2 (q)\) is the coincident-point variance of the shell at resolution \(q\): \(\partial_q \text{Var} (X_q (x)) = a_2 (q)\) for the free field at resolution \(q\), and \(\int_{q_0}^{\infty} a_2 = \text{Tr} C_{q_0} \asymp N\) is the divergent variance of the lattice field.

10.2The cutoff measure and the goal

Fix \(\lambda > 0\) and put \(\gamma := 3 \lambda\) (not to be confused with the counterterm \(\gamma_T\) of Lecture 4). The interaction and the lattice measure are

\(\displaystyle U (\varphi) = \frac{\lambda}{4} \int \varphi^4, \qquad V_N (\varphi) = U (\varphi) + \frac{\mu_N}{2} \int \varphi^2, \qquad \nu_N (\mathrm{d} \varphi) = \frac{\mathrm{e}^{- V_N (\varphi)}}{\mathcal{Z}_N} \hspace{0.17em} \mu^C (\mathrm{d} \varphi),\) (10.4)

where \(\mu^C\) is the centred Gaussian measure with covariance \(C = C_{q_0}\) on \(H_N\), and \(\mu_N \in \mathbb{R}\) is a mass counterterm to be chosen. With this normalisation \(D^2 U (y) = \gamma M_{y^2}\). In Lecture 4 we wrote the interaction as \(\lambda \int H_4 (\varphi ; c_T) + \gamma_T \int H_2 (\varphi ; c_T)\); the Wick ordering is now part of \(\mu_N\), and the coupling is rescaled by \(4\). For every real \(\mu_N\), \(\nu_N\) is a probability measure with all moments, since the quartic term dominates.

Theorem 10.2. (Main result, informal) Let \(\lambda > 0\), \(q_0 > 0\), \(r \in \mathbb{R}\), and \(\mu_N = r - 3 \lambda \text{Tr} C + 6 \lambda^2 \int C^3\). Then:

  1. the laws \(\nu_N\), embedded in \(H^{- s} (\mathbb{T}^3)\), \(s > \frac{1}{2}\), converge along the whole sequence to a measure \(\nu\), and \(\nu\) is an explicit density over the explicit flow from \(Y_{q_0} = 0\) (Theorem 18.5);

  2. the forecast processes converge in law, along the whole sequence, tested at each scale and jointly with the terminal field, to a solution of a forward–backward system on \([q_0, \infty)\), \(Y_{q_0} = 0\), driven by a cylindrical Brownian motion, whose terminal value has the law \(\nu\); the forecast is \(\psi_q = m_q (\Phi_q)\) with \(m_q\) continuous (Theorem 18.6);

  3. \(\nu\) has a renormalised variational formula (Theorem 18.13), and it satisfies the integration by parts formula of Theorem 18.8, in which the renormalised cube appears only tested in expectation, and it has quartic exponential tails for tested fields.

The bounds that prove (a) and (b) are uniform in \(N\): this is UV stability. They are bounds on the error \(Z\) of an approximate forecast, defined below. We now set up the exact FBSDE at fixed \(N\).

10.3The Polchinski process as a posterior

At a fixed lattice everything can be realised by a simple statistical experiment. Let \(\Phi_{\infty} \sim \nu_N\), and let \(B\) be an independent Brownian motion on \(H_N\), \(\mathbb{E} \langle B_t, u \rangle \langle B_s, v \rangle = (t \wedge s) \langle u, v \rangle\). We observe

\(\displaystyle P_q = (q - q_0) \Phi_{\infty} + B_{q - q_0}, \qquad q \geqslant q_0,\) (10.5)

with the filtration \(\mathcal{F}_q\) generated by \((P_s)_{s \leqslant q}\) (completed), and \(\mathbb{E}_q =\mathbb{E}[\hspace{0.17em} \cdot \hspace{0.17em} |\mathcal{F}_q]\). Let

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{K}_q (p) := \log \int \mathrm{e}^{\langle p, \varphi \rangle - \frac{q - q_0}{2} \| \varphi \|_2^2} \nu_N (\mathrm{d} \varphi), \qquad \nu_{q, p} (\mathrm{d} \varphi) := \mathrm{e}^{\langle p, \varphi \rangle - \frac{q - q_0}{2} \| \varphi \|_2^2 -\mathcal{K}_q (p)} \nu_N (\mathrm{d} \varphi) .\) (10.6)

Proposition 10.3. (Posterior, forecast and innovation) At every fixed \(N\):

  1. the conditional law of \(\Phi_{\infty}\) given \(\mathcal{F}_q\) is \(\nu_{q, P_q}\). In particular the forecast and the conditional covariance are

    \(\displaystyle \psi_q := \mathbb{E}_q \Phi_{\infty} = D\mathcal{K}_q (P_q), \qquad \text{Cov}_q := \text{Cov} (\Phi_{\infty} |\mathcal{F}_q) = D^2 \mathcal{K}_q (P_q) > 0 ;\) (10.7)

  2. the innovation \(W_q := P_q - \int_{q_0}^q \psi_s \mathrm{d} s\) is an \((\mathcal{F}_q)\)-Brownian motion on \(H_N\), and

    \(\displaystyle \mathrm{d} P_q = \psi_q \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} q + \mathrm{d} W_q, \qquad \mathrm{d} \psi_q = \text{Cov}_q \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W_q ;\) (10.8)

  3. \(\mathcal{K}\) solves \(\partial_q \mathcal{K}_q + \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} D^2 \mathcal{K}_q + \frac{1}{2} \|D\mathcal{K}_q \|_2^2 = 0\).

Proof. (a) Conditionally on \(\Phi_{\infty} = \varphi\), the density of the observed path on \([q_0, q]\) with respect to the law of \(B\) is \(\exp \{\langle P_q, \varphi \rangle - \frac{q - q_0}{2} \| \varphi \|^2 \}\) (Girsanov, or a direct computation on finitely many increments). Bayes' formula gives \(\nu_{q, P_q}\), and differentiating \(\mathcal{K}_q\) in \(p\) gives the conditional moments. Positivity of \(\text{Cov}_q\) holds because \(\nu_{q, p}\) has a positive density. (b) For \(s < q\), \(\mathbb{E}_s [P_q - P_s] = (q - s) \psi_s\) since \(B\) has independent increments, and \(\mathbb{E}_s \int_s^q \psi_u \mathrm{d} u = (q - s) \psi_s\) since \(\psi\) is a martingale. So \(W\) is a continuous martingale with \(\mathrm{d} \langle W, W \rangle_q = I \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} q\), and Lévy's theorem applies. (c) \(\partial_q \mathcal{K}= - \frac{1}{2} \mathbb{E}_{\nu_{q, p}} \| \varphi \|^2\) and \(\text{Tr} D^2 \mathcal{K}=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{q, p}} \| \varphi \|^2 - \|D\mathcal{K}\|^2\). Differentiating (c) in \(p\) and applying Itô's formula along \(\mathrm{d} P = D\mathcal{K} (P) \mathrm{d} q + \mathrm{d} W\), the drift of \(D\mathcal{K}_q (P_q)\) cancels, which gives \(\mathrm{d} \psi = \text{Cov} \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W\).\(\Box\)

The observation \(P_q\) is not yet the field of Lecture 4. The posterior (10.6) is \(\nu_N\) tilted by a Gaussian factor of precision \(q - q_0\): the remaining randomness has precision \(K_{q_0} + (q - q_0) = K_q\). This suggests the following.

Proposition 10.4. (The Polchinski process) Let \(\Phi_q := C_q P_q\) and \(F_q := K_q (\psi_q - \Phi_q)\). Then

\(\displaystyle \mathrm{d} \Phi_q = C_q^2 F_q \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} q + C_q \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W_q, \qquad F_q =\mathbb{E}_q [- DV_N (\Phi_{\infty})], \qquad \psi_q = \Phi_q + C_q F_q .\) (10.9)

This is the FBSDE (7.7) of Lecture 4 for the profile (10.2): \(\Phi\) is the interacting field at resolution \(q\), \(F\) the effective force, \(\psi\) the forecast of Proposition 8.1, and \(\text{Cov}_q = H^{\text{ex}}_q (\psi_q)\) (Proposition 10.5) is the response of Proposition 8.3. Under \(\nu_N = \mu^C\) one has \(F = 0\), \(\Phi_q = \psi_q\), and \(\Phi_q\) is the Gaussian field \(X_q\) of Proposition 5.2 of Lecture 3, with law \(\mu^{Q_q}\).

Proof. \(\mathrm{d} \Phi_q = - C_q^2 P_q \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} q + C_q (\psi_q \mathrm{d} q + \mathrm{d} W_q) = C_q (\psi_q - \Phi_q) \mathrm{d} q + C_q \mathrm{d} W_q\), which is the first identity. For the second, integrate by parts in the density of \(\nu_{q, p}\) with respect to Lebesgue measure, \(\exp \{\langle p, \varphi \rangle - \frac{1}{2} \langle \varphi, K_q \varphi \rangle - V_N (\varphi)\}\): there is no boundary term, and

\(\displaystyle p = K_q \hspace{0.17em} D\mathcal{K}_q (p) + \int DV_N \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} \nu_{q, p} .\) (10.10)

At \(p = P_q = K_q \Phi_q\) this reads \(K_q (\psi_q - \Phi_q) =\mathbb{E}_q [- DV_N (\Phi_{\infty})]\). In the Gaussian case the posterior is \(\mu^{C_q} (\cdot - C_q P_q)\), so \(\psi = \Phi\) and \(\Phi_{\infty} - \Phi_q\) is independent of \(\mathcal{F}_q\) with covariance \(C_q\). Hence \(\Phi_q\) is a Gaussian martingale with covariance \(C - C_q = Q_q\). In general both the law of \((\Phi_{\infty}, (\Phi_q)_q)\) here and the law of \((X_{\infty}, (X_q)_q)\) under the measure \(\mathbb{P}\) of Proposition 7.2 are the Gaussian law tilted by the same function \(\mathrm{e}^{- V_N} /\mathcal{Z}_N\) of the terminal field, so they coincide. Since \(P_q = K_q \Phi_q\), the filtration \((\mathcal{F}_q)\) is also the one generated by \(\Phi\).\(\Box\)

So at a fixed lattice the FBSDE of Lecture 4 is a theorem, with a Brownian motion \(W\) which is explicitly the innovation of a filtering problem. All objects of Section 8 have a statistical meaning:

Lecture 4 filtering at resolution \(q\)
field \(\Phi_q\) \(C_q P_q\): posterior mean under the Gaussian prior \(\mu^C\)
forecast \(\psi_q\) posterior mean \(\mathbb{E}_q \Phi_{\infty}\)
response \(H^{\text{ex}}_q (\psi_q) = \text{Cov}_q\) posterior covariance
force \(F_q = K_q (\psi_q - \Phi_q)\) posterior mean of the terminal force \(- DV_N (\Phi_{\infty})\)
Brownian motion \(W\) innovation \(P_q - \int \psi\)
unresolved covariance \(S_q = C_q\) prior precision plus observation precision, inverted

The observations need no ultraviolet regularity: \(P_q\) is defined for every \(q\), with terminal resolution \(\infty\) at fixed \(N\). The lattice is the only ultraviolet cutoff, and \(\Phi_q \to \Phi_{\infty}\) as \(q \to \infty\) (Lemma 16.1).

10.4The Wetterich formulation

Let \(\Psi_q := \mathcal{K}_q^{\ast}\) be the Legendre transform of the log-partition function and

\(\displaystyle \Gamma_q (y) := \Psi_q (y) - \tfrac{1}{2} \langle y, K_q y \rangle, \qquad H_q^{\text{ex}} (y) := (K_q + D^2 \Gamma_q (y))^{- 1} .\) (10.11)

Comparing with (8.11), and using \(S_q^{- 1} = K_q\), \(\Gamma_q\) is, up to a function of \(q\) alone, the \(\Gamma_t\) of Lecture 4: \(\Gamma_q + \frac{1}{2} \langle y, K_{q_0} y \rangle\) is the effective average action with the regulator \(\mathcal{R}_q = q - q_0\) (Exercise 10.1). The coordinate identity \(P_q = D \Psi_q (\psi_q)\) reads

\(\displaystyle K_q \Phi_q = K_q \psi_q + D \Gamma_q (\psi_q), \qquad \text{i.e.} \qquad \Phi_q = \psi_q + C_q D \Gamma_q (\psi_q),\) (10.12)

which is (8.5) with \(\mathcal{F}_q = D \Gamma_q\).

Proposition 10.5. (Wetterich equation with a mass regulator) At fixed \(N\), \(\Gamma_q\) is smooth and strictly convex after adding \(\frac{1}{2} \langle y, K_q y \rangle\), and

\(\displaystyle \partial_q \Gamma_q (y) = \tfrac{1}{2} \text{Tr} H^{\text{ex}}_q (y), \qquad \text{Cov}_q = H^{\text{ex}}_q (\psi_q) .\) (10.13)

Proof. \(\mathcal{K}_q\) is smooth, strictly convex (its Hessian is a covariance) and superlinear (the posterior has full support), so \(\Psi_q\) is smooth and \(D^2 \Psi_q (D\mathcal{K}(p)) = (D^2 \mathcal{K}(p))^{- 1}\). By the envelope theorem at fixed \(y\) and Proposition 10.3(c), \(\partial_q \Psi_q (y) = - \partial_q \mathcal{K}_q (p) = \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} D^2 \mathcal{K}_q (p) + \frac{1}{2} \|y\|^2\), with \(p = D \Psi_q (y)\). Subtract \(\partial_q \frac{1}{2} \langle y, K_q y \rangle = \frac{1}{2} \|y\|^2\).\(\Box\)

This is (8.12) with \(\partial_q \mathcal{R}= 1\), up to the constant \(- \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} C_q\) (which is infinite in the continuum and irrelevant here). It is an identity about the unknown measure \(\nu_N\), not a construction: to use it we would need estimates on \(\Gamma_q\), which is what we want to prove. Lecture 4 suggests the strategy: since the FBSDE in forecast coordinates is

\(\displaystyle \mathrm{d} \psi_q = \text{Cov}_q \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W_q, \qquad \text{Cov}_q = H^{\text{ex}}_q (\psi_q),\)

an approximate effective action gives approximate forecast coordinates, and the error should be governed by how badly the approximate action fails to solve (10.13).

10.5Approximate forecast and error

Let \(\bar{\Gamma}_q\) be, for \(q > 0\), an even functional on \(H_N\), continuous and piecewise smooth in \(q\) and \(y\), such that \(\bar{\Psi}_q (y) = \frac{1}{2} \langle y, K_q y \rangle + \bar{\Gamma}_q (y)\) is uniformly convex, \(D^2 \bar{\Psi}_q \geqslant \underline{c}_q I\) with \(\underline{c}_q > 0\). Let \(\bar{\mathcal{K}}_q = \bar{\Psi}_q^{\ast}\) and define the approximate response, the defect and its drift

\(\displaystyle \) \(\displaystyle H_q (y) := (K_q + D^2 \bar{\Gamma}_q (y))^{- 1},\) \(\displaystyle \)
\(\displaystyle \) \(\displaystyle e_q (y) := \partial_q \bar{\Gamma}_q (y) - \tfrac{1}{2} \text{Tr} [H_q (y) - H_q (0)], \qquad b_q (y) := - H_q (y) De_q (y) .\) \(\displaystyle \) (10.14)

The subtraction of \(H_q (0)\) only removes a function of \(q\). Define the approximate forecast and the error

\(\displaystyle Y_q := D \bar{\mathcal{K}}_q (P_q), \qquad Z_q := \psi_q - Y_q, \qquad \Xi_q := \text{Cov}_q - H_q (Y_q) .\) (10.15)

Equivalently \(Y_q\) solves the approximate coordinate equation \(K_q \Phi_q = K_q Y_q + D \bar{\Gamma}_q (Y_q)\), i.e. (10.12) with \(\bar{\Gamma}\) in place of \(\Gamma\). If \(\bar{\Gamma} = \Gamma\) then \(Y = \psi\), \(Z = 0\), and \(e\) depends only on \(q\), so \(b = 0\).

Theorem 10.6. (The FBSDE in approximate forecast coordinates) At every fixed \(N\),

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} \mathrm{d} Y_q & = [b_q (Y_q) + H_q (Y_q) Z_q] \mathrm{d} q + H_q (Y_q) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W_q,\\ \mathrm{d} Z_q & = - [b_q (Y_q) + H_q (Y_q) Z_q] \mathrm{d} q + \Xi_q \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W_q, \hspace{3cm} \text{(10.16)} \end{array}\)

for \(q \geqslant q_0\), and \(Y + Z = \psi\) is the martingale \(\int \text{Cov} \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W\).

Proof. By the Legendre identities \(\partial_q \bar{\mathcal{K}}_q (p) = - \partial_q \bar{\Psi}_q (y) = - \frac{1}{2} \|y\|^2 - \partial_q \bar{\Gamma}_q (y)\) and \(D^2 \bar{\mathcal{K}}_q (p) = H_q (y)\) at \(y = D \bar{\mathcal{K}}_q (p)\). Hence

\(\displaystyle \partial_q \bar{\mathcal{K}}_q + \tfrac{1}{2} \text{Tr} D^2 \bar{\mathcal{K}}_q + \tfrac{1}{2} \|D \bar{\mathcal{K}}_q \|^2 = - \left( \partial_q \bar{\Gamma}_q - \tfrac{1}{2} \text{Tr} H_q \right) \circ D \bar{\mathcal{K}}_q = - e_q \circ D \bar{\mathcal{K}}_q + \tfrac{1}{2} \text{Tr} H_q (0) .\)

Differentiate in \(p\): \(\partial_q D \bar{\mathcal{K}} + \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} D^2 (D \bar{\mathcal{K}}) + D^2 \bar{\mathcal{K}} \hspace{0.17em} D \bar{\mathcal{K}} = - H \hspace{0.17em} De = b\). \(\displaystyle \mathrm{d} Y_q = \left[ \partial_q D \bar{\mathcal{K}} + \tfrac{1}{2} \text{Tr} D^2 (D \bar{\mathcal{K}}) \right] \mathrm{d} q + D^2 \bar{\mathcal{K}} \hspace{0.17em} (\psi_q \mathrm{d} q + \mathrm{d} W_q) = [b_q - H_q Y_q + H_q \psi_q] \mathrm{d} q + H_q \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W_q,\)

11Renormalisation: why two of them

We now construct \(\bar{\Gamma}_q\) on bounded fields. This section is deterministic: it uses no information on \(\nu_N\).

11.1Scales and power counting

Two scales govern everything:

\(\displaystyle g_q := \gamma q^{- 1 / 2}, \qquad r_q := (q / \gamma)^{1 / 2}, \qquad \gamma r_q^2 = q.\) (11.1)

\(g_q\) is the dimensionless coupling at resolution \(q\): in \(d = 3\) the coupling \(\lambda\) has dimension of mass, and \(q^{1 / 2}\) is the mass of the regulator. It is small at all scales \(q \geqslant q_{\ast}\) if \(g_{q_{\ast}} = \gamma q_{\ast}^{- 1 / 2}\) is small; this is the only large number in the construction, and it is not a physical mass. \(r_q\) is the field size at which the nonlinearity is comparable to the regulator: \(D^2 U (y) = \gamma y^2 \approx q\) when \(|y| \approx r_q\). Compare with the size of the free field at resolution \(q\): \(\text{Var} X_q (x) = \int_{q_0}^q a_2 \leqslant Cq^{1 / 2}\), so typical fields have size \(q^{1 / 4} = g_q^{1 / 2} r_q \ll r_q\).

We measure a \(k\)-tensor \(T (x_1, \ldots, x_k)\) (a kernel of a \(k\)th field derivative, a vertex) by its rooted norm

\(\displaystyle s (T) = \max_i \sup_{x_i} \int |T (x_1, \ldots, x_k) | \prod_{j \ne i} \mathrm{d} x_j,\)

and a functional \(f\) by \(\textsf{S}_k (f) = \sup_{y \in \mathcal{B}_q} s (D^k f (y))\) on the field ball

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{B}_q := \{y \in H_N : \|y\|_{\infty} < 2 r_q \} .\)

The natural size of the \(k\)th vertex of the loop corrections \(\Gamma_q - U - \frac{1}{2} \langle y, M_q y \rangle\) to the bare action is

\(\displaystyle \textsf{S}_k \lesssim g_q \hspace{0.17em} q \hspace{0.17em} r_q^{2 - k} .\) (11.2)

For \(k = 2\) this says the Hessian of the corrections is \(g_q\) times the regulator \(q\) (while \(D^2 U \approx q\) at \(|y| \approx r_q\)); each extra field derivative costs \(r_q^{- 1}\). For \(k = 4\), \(g_q qr_q^{- 2} = \gamma g_q\): the quartic vertex of the corrections is \(\gamma\) times the small coupling. The whole construction is a bookkeeping of (11.2).

The basic loop estimate.
Derivatives of \(\frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} (K + D^2 \Gamma)^{- 1}\) are traces of closed chains \(H \hspace{0.17em} D^{j_1 + 2} \Gamma \hspace{0.17em} H \cdots D^{j_p + 2} \Gamma \hspace{0.17em} H\). A closed loop with \(p\) vertices, each carrying at least one external leg (vertices without external legs are resummed into \(H\)), and \(p + 1\) propagators \(H\) (two of them adjacent, \(H^2\), once the trace is closed) is estimated by rooting one external slot, using two propagators in \(L^2\) (each row has \(L^2\) norm \(\leqslant (a_2)^{1 / 2}\)) and the others in operator norm (\(\leqslant C / q\)):

\(\displaystyle s (\text{loop}) \leqslant C \hspace{0.17em} a_2 (q) \hspace{0.17em} q^{- (p - 1)} \prod_{i = 1}^p s (T_i)\) (11.3)

[FBSDE26]. The vertices of the bare action have \(s (D^{j + 2} U) \leqslant Cqr_q^{- j}\) on the ball (\(D^3 U = 2 \gamma y\), \(D^4 U = 2 \gamma\)). With \(a_2 \leqslant Cq^{- 1 / 2}\), a loop with \(k\) external legs built from them is \(\leqslant Cq^{1 / 2} r_q^{- k}\), and after the integration in \(q\) that produces \(\Gamma\) (which gains a factor \(q\)) it has size \(q^{3 / 2} r_q^{- k} = g_q qr_q^{2 - k}\): this is the origin of (11.2), the size of one loop. Replacing a bare vertex by a vertex of a correction of relative size \(\varepsilon\) multiplies the loop by \(\varepsilon\) (Exercise 11.1). Every further loop gains one power of \(g_q\).

11.2The seed: one loop

The first approximation is the one-loop formula of Exercise 8.7(b): replace \(D^2 \Gamma\) by \(D^2 U = \gamma y^2\) in the Wetterich equation and integrate from \(q = \infty\). With the quadratic part subtracted this gives the seed

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} \mathcal{L}_q (y) & = \tfrac{1}{2} \text{Tr} [\log (I + C_q^{1 / 2} \gamma y^2 C_q^{1 / 2}) - C_q^{1 / 2} \gamma y^2 C_q^{1 / 2}],\\ \partial_q \mathcal{L}_q (y) & = \tfrac{1}{2} \text{Tr} [G_q (y) - C_q] + \tfrac{\gamma}{2} a_2 (q) \|y\|_2^2, \hspace{3cm} \text{(11.4)} \end{array}\)

with \(G_q (y) = (K_q + \gamma y^2)^{- 1}\). \(\mathcal{L}_q \leqslant 0\), and its constant and quadratic Taylor coefficients at \(y = 0\) vanish. Its expansion in powers of the field,

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{L}_q (y) = \sum_{j \geqslant 2} \frac{(- 1)^{j + 1}}{2 j} \text{Tr} (C_q \gamma y^2)^j,\)

is the sum of one-loop diagrams with \(2 j\) external legs. The quartic term is the bubble \(- \frac{\gamma^2}{4} \int \int C_q (x, z)^2 y (x)^2 y (z)^2 \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} x \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} z\), with \(s (D^4 \mathcal{L}_q (0)) = 6 \gamma^2 a_2 (q) \leqslant Cg_q qr_q^{- 2}\), as predicted by (11.2).

Figure 11.1. The seed (11.4) as one-loop graphs: lines \(C_q\), and a vertex \(\gamma y^2\) with its two external fields at each corner. The \(j\)-th ring has \(2 j\) legs and coefficient \((- 1)^{j + 1} / (2 j)\). The series sums to a logarithm and has radius of convergence \(r_q\). The quartic ring is the bubble.

11.3Corrections and the mass

The seed solves the Wetterich equation only with \(D^2 U\) inside the trace. Its defect is of relative size \(g_q\) compared with the one-loop flow \(q^{1 / 2} r_q^{- k}\) (two loops). To improve it, let \(\Pi f (y) = f (y) - f (0) - \frac{1}{2} D^2 f (0) [y, y]\) remove the constant and quadratic Taylor coefficients (the low jets) of an even functional, let \(M_q = \mu_q I + \Theta_q\) be a quadratic operator path (a running mass \(\mu_q\) and an even, translation-invariant, centred kernel \(\Theta_q\), \(\Theta_q \mathbb{1} = 0\)), and set

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{T}_q [M, h] (y) := \tfrac{1}{2} \text{Tr} [(K_q + M_q + D^2 (U + h) (y))^{- 1} - (K_q + M_q)^{- 1}] .\) (11.5)

The approximate action on the field ball is

\(\displaystyle \Gamma^{\text{o}}_q (y) = U (y) + \tfrac{1}{2} \langle y, M_q y \rangle + h^{[2]}_q (y),\) (11.6)

with the nonlinear part built by two integrated Wetterich steps,

\(\displaystyle h^{[0]} =\mathcal{L}, \qquad h^{[j + 1]}_q =\mathcal{L}_q - \int_q^{\infty} \Pi (\mathcal{T}_s [M, h^{[j]}] - \partial_s \mathcal{L}_s) \mathrm{d} s, \qquad j = 0, 1,\) (11.7)

so that \(\partial_q h^{[j + 1]} = \Pi \hspace{0.17em} \mathcal{T}_q [M, h^{[j]}]\): each step inserts the previous approximation into the trace. The integrals run from \(\infty\): the nonlinear part is fixed by the requirement that it vanishes at infinite resolution, where \(\Gamma_q\) must become the bare action (Theorem 8.4).

Figure 11.2. The iteration (11.7), schematically, with only two external legs drawn per vertex. Each step inserts the previous correction into the trace and gains one loop and one factor \(g_q\), as in (11.11). After two steps the defect is the trace difference of order \(g_q^3\), which gives (11.12) and makes the source integrable in the backward equation.

The quadratic part.
The low jets are not iterated: they are handled exactly by the path \(M\). The quadratic part of the flow at \(y = 0\) is, by evenness, \(\frac{1}{2} D^2 \text{Tr} H (0) = - \frac{1}{2} \text{Ctr}_{R_0^2} D^4 \Gamma^{\text{o}} (0)\), where \(R_{0, q} = (K_q + M_q)^{- 1}\) and \(\text{Ctr}_R T\) contracts two slots of \(T\) with the kernel \(R\). Requiring that \(M\) solves this part of the flow exactly gives the mass equation

\(\displaystyle \partial_q M_q = B_q [M] := - \gamma \text{Tr} (R_{0, q}^2) I - \tfrac{1}{2} \text{Ctr}_{R_{0, q}^2} D^4 h^{[2]}_q (0),\) (11.8)

(the source \(B_q [M]\) is not the sampling Brownian motion \(B\)), with the mixed boundary conditions

\(\displaystyle \mu_{q_{\ast}} = b, \qquad \Theta_q \to 0 \text{as } q \to \infty\)

(the latter in first spatial moment). The running mass is pinned at the auxiliary scale \(q_{\ast}\), at a value \(b\) with \(|b| \leqslant B \gamma q_{\ast}^{1 / 2}\), while the nonlocal kernel is pinned at infinity. The bare mass is then

\(\displaystyle \mu_N = \lim_{q \to \infty} \mu_q = b + \int_{q_{\ast}}^{\infty} \beta_q \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} q, \qquad B_q \mathbb{1} = \beta_q \mathbb{1},\) (11.9)

and it is this number that enters the Gibbs measure (10.4). Note the order: the counterterm is an output of the deterministic flow, not an input.

Proposition 11.1. (The coefficient construction [FBSDE26]) For \(q_{\ast}\) large enough (depending on \(\gamma\), not on \(N\)), the system (11.7)–(11.8) has a unique solution in a ball, with

\(\displaystyle | \mu_q | \leqslant C \gamma \sqrt{q}, \qquad v_1 (\Theta_q) \leqslant C \gamma^2 q^{- 1 / 2}, \qquad \| \partial_q M_q \|_{\text{row}} \leqslant Cg_q,\) (11.10)

where \(v_1 (\Theta) = \int d (0, z) | \Theta (0, z) | \mathrm{d} z\). The seed obeys \(\mathcal{N}_{k, q} (\mathcal{L}) \leqslant C_k g_q qr_q^{2 - k}\), \(4 \leqslant k \leqslant 10\), and the corrections obey

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{N}_{k, q} (h^{[j]} - h^{[j - 1]}) \leqslant C_k \hspace{0.17em} g_q^{j + 1} \hspace{0.17em} q \hspace{0.17em} r_q^{2 - k}, \qquad 4 \leqslant k \leqslant 10 - 2 j, \quad j = 1, 2,\) (11.11)

where \(\mathcal{N}_{k, q} (f) = \textsf{S}_k (f) + \sqrt{q} \hspace{0.17em} v_1 (D^k f (0))\), and for a \(k\)-tensor

\(\displaystyle v_1 (T) = \max_{i, a < b} \sup_{x_i} \int d (x_a, x_b) |T| \prod_{j \ne i} \mathrm{d} x_j .\)

The complete defect of \(\Gamma^{\text{o}}\) on the ball is \(e^{\text{o}}_q = \Pi (\mathcal{T}_q [M, h^{[1]}] -\mathcal{T}_q [M, h^{[2]}])\), and

\(\displaystyle \textsf{S}_4 (e^{\text{o}}_q) \leqslant C \gamma^5 q^{- 3} .\) (11.12)

At fixed \(N\) the bare mass exists, \(| \mu_N - b| \lesssim \gamma \text{Tr} C_{q_{\ast}} \lesssim \gamma N\), and \(| \mu_N - \mu_q | \leqslant C \gamma N^3 / q\), \(\| \Theta_q \|_{\text{row}} \leqslant CN \gamma^2 q^{- 1 / 2} \to 0\) as \(q \to \infty\).

Idea of the proof. (a) Trace Lipschitz estimate. If two corrections \(h, \hat{h}\) obey the individual bounds (11.2) up to order \(k + 2\) and their difference obeys them with \(g_q\) replaced by \(\varepsilon_q\), then \(\mathcal{N}_k (\mathcal{T}[M, h] -\mathcal{T}[M, \hat{h}]) \leqslant C \varepsilon_q q^{1 / 2} r_q^{- k}\). Expand \(D^k\) of the trace into loops; in each, telescope the difference onto one marked vertex or propagator and apply (11.3). (b) Integration. Each step gains one \(g\); the integrand of (11.7) is \(O (\gamma^{1 + k / 2} s^{- k / 2})\) in the \(k\)th derivative, and that of the increment \(h^{[j + 1]} - h^{[j]}\) is \(O (\gamma^{1 + k / 2} s^{- k / 2} g_s^j)\), integrable at \(s = \infty\) for \(k \geqslant 4\). The low jets have been removed, so the lower derivatives are controlled by Taylor's formula from the fourth. (c) Mass path. Given \(M\) in the ball, the right side of (11.8) is computed from the corrections, and the map \(M \mapsto (\mu, \Theta)\) is a contraction for large \(q_{\ast}\): differences gain a factor \(g_{q_{\ast}}\) (for \(\mu\) after integrating from \(q_{\ast}\), using \(\sup_x \log x / \sqrt{x} < \infty\)). (d) Defect. The mass equation supplies exactly the quadratic jet of the flow, and \(\partial_q h^{[2]} = \Pi \mathcal{T} [M, h^{[1]}]\), so the defect is the trace difference between the last two approximations, of order \(g^3\): by (a) with \(\varepsilon_q = g_q^3\), \(\textsf{S}_4 (e^{\text{o}}) \leqslant Cg_q^3 q^{1 / 2} r_q^{- 4} = C \gamma^5 q^{- 3}\).\(\Box\)

Two technical points are hidden in this sketch. First, the trace estimate (a) needs more derivatives than it controls: the \(k\)th derivative of the trace involves vertices of order \(k + 2\). Ten derivatives of the seed leave eight after one step and six after two; this is why the number of steps must be finite and fixed in advance (an infinite iteration loses all derivatives). Second, the quadratic kernel \(\Theta_q\) must be measured in a first spatial moment \(v_1\), not in a second one: the estimate \(\| \delta_z^2 C_q \|_{\text{row}} \lesssim d (0, z)^2\), which a second moment would require, fails by a logarithm, and one uses \(\| \delta_z^2 C_q \|_{\text{row}} \lesssim q^{- 1 / 2} d (0, z)\) instead [FBSDE26]. This is also why \(\mathcal{N}_k\) contains a spatial moment at \(y = 0\).

11.4Two mass renormalisations

The bare mass \(\mu_N\) diverges as \(N \to \infty\). Its two divergent parts are the two divergent self-energy diagrams of Appendix A.2 of Lecture 4.

  • The tadpole. The first term of (11.8) integrates to \(- \gamma \int_{q_{\ast}}^{\infty} \text{Tr} R_{0, q}^2 \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} q \approx - \gamma \text{Tr} C = - 3 \lambda \text{Tr} C \sim - N\) (up to a term independent of \(N\)). This is Wick ordering: \(\frac{\lambda}{4} \varphi^4 - \frac{3}{2} \lambda c \varphi^2\) agrees, up to a constant, with \(\frac{\lambda}{4} H_4 (\varphi ; c)\), with \(c = C (x, x)\) ((A.5) of Lecture 4).

  • The sunset. The second term contracts the bubble contained in \(D^4 h^{[2]} (0)\) (the quartic coefficient of the seed) with \(R_0^2\), and integrating in \(q\) produces the two-loop self-energy, which diverges like \(\lambda^2 \int_{|z| > 1 / N} |z|^{- 3} \mathrm{d} z \sim \lambda^2 \log N\). This is the sunset counterterm \(\gamma_T \sim \log T\) of (A.7) of Lecture 4: with \(\lambda_{\text{L3}} = \lambda / 4\) and \(\gamma_T \int H_2 = \gamma_T \int \varphi^2 +\)const, the sunset part of \(\mu_N\) is \(2 \gamma_T = 6 \lambda^2 \int C^3 \approx \frac{3 \lambda^2}{8 \pi^2} \log N\), positive. (The double-tadpole logarithms in the two terms of (11.8), the bubble with a tadpole insertion and the mass insertion in \(\text{Tr} R_{0, q}^2\), cancel each other.)

Figure 11.3. The mass equation (11.8) and the two divergent parts of the bare mass. The cross is the insertion \(R_{0, q}^2\). Since \(D^4 h^{[2]} (0)\) contains the bubble of Figure 11.1, its contraction contains the sunset. Integrating in \(q\) turns the crossed line into a line \(C\): the tadpole is linear in \(N\) and the sunset logarithmic.

Power counting (Exercise A.3 of Lecture 4) says that no other counterterm is needed. In the flow this is the statement that the integrals (11.7) of all vertices with \(k \geqslant 4\) legs converge at \(q = \infty\): only the quadratic jet must be fixed by a boundary condition.

There is a subtlety: the running mass \(\mu_q\) at fixed \(q\) has a limit as \(N \to \infty\), but \(\mu_N = \mu_{\infty}\) does not. One never interchanges these two limits. Also, the whole running kernel \(\Theta_q\) is kept: replacing the sunset by its local part would leave a nonlocal remainder of the wrong size.

11.5Two correction steps

Why two corrections, and not one or three? The answer is in the backward equation of (10.16). Freeze the noise and the large fields, and estimate the size of \(Z\) produced by a defect on the ball. The propagator of \(\mathrm{d} Z = - HZ \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} q + \ldots\) from \(q\) to \(s\) is about \(s / q\) (since \(H \approx C_q\) has norm \(q^{- 1}\)), so

\(\displaystyle Z_q \approx \int_q^{\infty} \frac{s}{q} \hspace{0.17em} b_s \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s, \qquad |b_s | \lesssim \|H_s \| \hspace{0.17em} \textsf{S}_4 (e_s) \hspace{0.17em} r_s^3 \lesssim s^{- 1} \textsf{S}_4 (e_s) \hspace{0.17em} r_s^3,\) (11.13)

where we used that the low jets of \(e\) vanish, so on the ball \(|De| \leqslant \textsf{S}_4 (e) |y|^3 / 6\). After \(n\) correction steps the defect is the trace difference of the last two approximations, of relative size \(g^{n + 1}\): by the trace estimate,

\(\displaystyle \textsf{S}_4 (e_s) \lesssim g_s^{n + 1} s^{1 / 2} r_s^{- 4} = \gamma^{n + 3} s^{- (n + 4) / 2}, \qquad |b_s | \lesssim \gamma^{n + 3 / 2} s^{- (n + 3) / 2},\)

and therefore

\(\displaystyle |Z_q | \lesssim \frac{\gamma^{n + 3 / 2}}{q} \int_q^{\infty} s^{- (n + 1) / 2} \mathrm{d} s.\)

steps \(n\) \(\textsf{S}_4 (e_s)\) \(|b_s |\) \(\int_q^{\infty} \frac{s}{q} |b_s | \mathrm{d} s\)
\(0\) (seed only) \(\gamma^3 s^{- 2}\) \(\gamma^{3 / 2} s^{- 3 / 2}\) \(\infty\) (power)
\(1\) \(\gamma^4 s^{- 5 / 2}\) \(\gamma^{5 / 2} s^{- 2}\) \(\infty\) (logarithm)
\(2\) \(\gamma^5 s^{- 3}\) \(\gamma^{7 / 2} s^{- 5 / 2}\) \(2 \gamma^{7 / 2} q^{- 3 / 2}\)

With one step the bounded-field source produces a logarithmically divergent error; two steps are the minimum, and they suffice: \(Z^{\text{b}}_q = O (q^{- 3 / 2})\), and the backward energy \(\gamma^{- 1} \int q^{1 / 2} |Z^{\text{b}} |^2 \mathrm{d} q\) is finite (Lecture 6). The general (fractional) theory gives the depth criterion \((n + 2) \varkappa > (3 + \varkappa) / 2\), with subcriticality \(\varkappa = \frac{1}{2}\) for \(\Phi^4_3\): \((2 + 2) \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 2 > \frac{7}{4}\), while \(n = 1\) gives \(\frac{3}{2} < \frac{7}{4}\). This is a sufficient condition, which loses \(\varkappa / 2\); for \(\Phi^4_3\) the heuristic above shows that \(n > 1\) suffices, with \(n = 1\) borderline.

The two senses of “two renormalisations” are related. The mass counterterm must contain the sunset because the quartic vertex of the corrections, already of the seed, contains the bubble, and contracting it produces the sunset; and the second step is needed because the defect of the first one, the three-loop remainder, is borderline in the backward equation, exactly as the sunset is borderline in the mass.

11.6The explicit counterterm

Formula (11.9) gives \(\mu_N\) as an output of the flow, from the boundary value \(b\). The input can be made explicit, and this also fixes what the finite part is.

Theorem 11.2. (The explicit counterterm) Let \(a_2 = \text{Tr} C_q^2\) and \(C_q^{(2)}\) be the kernel of \(C_q^2\). For \(|b| \leqslant B \gamma q_{\ast}^{1 / 2}\) and \(q_{\ast}\) large,

\(\displaystyle \beta_q = - \gamma a_2 - \gamma \mu_q a_2' + \gamma^2 a_2^2 + 2 \gamma^2 \int C_q^2 C_q^{(2)} +\mathcal{R}_q, \quad |\mathcal{R}_q | \leqslant C \gamma^3 q^{- 3 / 2}, \) (11.14)

and so, for \(f_N (b) = \mu_N (b) + 3 \lambda \text{Tr} C - 6 \lambda^2 \int C^3\),

\(\displaystyle f_N (b) = b (1 + \gamma a_2 (q_{\ast})) + \gamma (\text{Tr} C - \text{Tr} C_{q_{\ast}}) - 6 \lambda^2 \int (C^3 - C_{q_{\ast}}^3) + \int_{q_{\ast}}^{\infty} (\mathcal{R}_q + \gamma (\beta_q + \gamma a_2) a_2) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} q. \) (11.15)

Moreover \(|f_N (b_1) - f_N (b_2) - (b_1 - b_2) | \leqslant Cg_{q_{\ast}} |b_1 - b_2 |\), and \(f_N \to f\) pointwise along the whole sequence. Consequently, for \(B\) above a universal constant, every \(r \in \mathbb{R}\) and \(q_{\ast}\) large depending on \(\lambda, q_0, B, r\), there is a unique \(b_N\) with

\(\displaystyle \mu_N = r - 3 \lambda \text{Tr} C + 6 \lambda^2 \int C^3, \) (11.16)

and \(b_N\) converges along the whole sequence.

Proof. Expansion. Expand \(R_0 = C - CMC + CMCMR_0\) with \(C = C_q\). Since \(a_2' = - 2 \text{Tr} C^3\), the first term of (11.8) is \(- \gamma a_2 - \gamma \mu a_2' + 2 \gamma \text{Tr} (C^3 \Theta)\), up to \(O (\gamma a_2 g_q^2)\), and \(| \text{Tr} (C^3 \Theta) | \leqslant Cq^{- 1} v_1 (\Theta)\) because \(| \hat{\Theta} (k) | \leqslant 2| k|v_1\). The corrections in \(h^{[2]}\) give \(O (\gamma^3 q^{- 3 / 2})\) after contraction, by (11.11). The quartic part of the seed is \(- \frac{\gamma^2}{4} \int \int C^2 (x, z) y (x)^2 y (z)^2\), and contracting its Hessian with \(R_0^2\) gives the operator \(\gamma^2 a_2 \text{diag} (R_0^2) + 2 \gamma^2 C^2 \cdot R_0^2\), where \(\cdot\) is the pointwise product of kernels. Its constant-mode eigenvalue, with \(R_0^2\) replaced by \(C^2\) at a relative cost \(g_q\), gives the remaining two terms.

Integration. Use \(\partial_q \text{Tr} C_q = - a_2\), \(\partial_q \int C_q^3 = - 3 \int C_q^2 C_q^{(2)}\), and \(- \gamma \mu a_2' + \gamma^2 a_2^2 = - \gamma \partial_q (\mu a_2) + \gamma (\beta + \gamma a_2) a_2\). This is the cancellation of the double-tadpole logarithms: \(\beta + \gamma a_2 = O (\gamma^2 / q)\). At fixed \(N\) the boundary terms at \(q = \infty\) vanish, which gives (11.15).

Response. For two boundary values with difference \(\delta b\), put \(D = \sup_q | \delta \mu_q |\) and \(X = \sup_q q^{3 / 2} v_1 (\delta \Theta_q)\), which are finite at fixed \(N\). The trace comparison gives \(| \delta \beta_q | \leqslant C \gamma (Dq^{- 3 / 2} + Xq^{- 5 / 2})\) and \(v_1 (\delta B_q - \delta \beta_q I) \leqslant C \gamma^2 (Dq^{- 5 / 2} + Xq^{- 7 / 2})\), so \(X \leqslant C \gamma^2 D + C \gamma^2 q_{\ast}^{- 1} X\) and \(D \leqslant | \delta b| + C \gamma q_{\ast}^{- 1 / 2} D + C \gamma q_{\ast}^{- 3 / 2} X\). Hence \(D \leqslant 2| \delta b|\), and integrating \(\delta \beta\) gives the claim.

Limit and inversion. In (11.15), \(\text{Tr} C - \text{Tr} C_{q_{\ast}}\) has summable terms \(\lesssim (1 + |k|)^{- 4}\); \(C - C_{q_{\ast}}\) converges uniformly and \(C^2 + CC_{q_{\ast}} + C_{q_{\ast}}^2\) in \(L^1\); and the integrand converges at fixed \(q\) by consistency of the coefficients, dominated by \(C \gamma^3 q^{- 3 / 2}\). As \(\text{Tr} C - \text{Tr} C_{q_{\ast}} \leqslant C + q_{\ast}^{1 / 2} / 10\) uniformly in \(N\), the range of \(f_N\) contains \(r\) for \(B\) above a universal constant and \(q_{\ast}\) large. Monotonicity and pointwise convergence give the convergence of \(b_N\).\(\Box\)

The measure depends on \(\mu_N\) only, so (11.16) does not depend on the choice of \(q_{\ast}\). The boundary value \(b\) is not the finite part: by (11.15), \(f_N (b) \approx b + \gamma q_{\ast}^{1 / 2} / (4 \pi)\) at leading order, so at fixed \(b\), raising \(q_{\ast}\) changes the theory.

12The flow at large fields

12.1The large-field problem

All the estimates of Proposition 11.1 hold on the field ball \(\|y\|_{\infty} < 2 r_q\), and only there. But the forecast \(Y_q\) is a random field on \(N^3\) sites, and nothing prevents it from being larger than \(r_q\) somewhere. The free field has typical size \(q^{1 / 4} \ll r_q\), but it exceeds \(r_q\) with small, nonzero probability, and nothing a priori controls the interacting forecast. We need an action \(\bar{\Gamma}_q\) defined and uniformly convex for all fields, which agrees with \(\Gamma^{\text{o}}_q\) on the ball.

A scalar model.
The difficulty is already visible in the one-site seed, \(\mathcal{L}_q (y) = \frac{1}{2} [\log (1 + \gamma y^2 / q) - \gamma y^2 / q]\). Its power series \(\sum_{j \geqslant 2} \frac{(- 1)^{j + 1}}{2 j} (\gamma y^2 / q)^j\) has radius of convergence exactly \(r_q\): the singularities are at \(y = \pm \mathrm{i} r_q\). Truncating it at any order produces a polynomial whose top coefficient has sign \((- 1)^{j + 1}\): for even \(j \geqslant 4\) the truncation is unbounded below and destroys the convexity of \(U +\mathcal{L}\) at \(|y| \gg r_q\). The exact function is harmless: its logarithmic part grows only logarithmically, and \(|\mathcal{L}_q'' (y) | \leqslant \frac{9}{8} \gamma / q \leqslant 2 \gamma / q\) for all \(y\). In field theory we do not have the exact function, only bounds on the ball, obtained through an expansion of the trace in vertices (the corrections are not polynomials, but their estimates are expansions in the field). This is the large-field problem: perturbative control holds for \(|y| \lesssim r_q\), and outside one needs a non-perturbative replacement that preserves the structure the estimates use.

The structure to preserve is coercivity, and it is carried by the bare quartic term. In the scalar model the coordinate equation \(qy + \lambda y^3 = p\) has noise coefficient

\(\displaystyle H_q (y) = \frac{1}{q + \gamma y^2} \leqslant \frac{1}{q} \wedge \frac{1}{\gamma y^2} :\)

large fields are more elliptic and less noisy. A good large-field extension keeps \(U\) untouched and freezes only the correction.

12.2Clipping

Let \(\tau : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) be smooth and odd, \(\tau (u) = u\) on \([- 1, 1]\), constant outside \([- 2, 2]\), with \(0 \leqslant \tau' \leqslant 1\), and set

\(\displaystyle \kappa_q (y) (x) := r_q \hspace{0.17em} \tau (y (x) / r_q), \qquad \tilde{h}_q := h^{[2]}_q \circ \kappa_q, \qquad \bar{\Gamma}_q := U + \tfrac{1}{2} \langle y, M_q y \rangle + \tilde{h}_q .\) (12.1)

The clip acts pointwise in space and maps every field into the ball. The quartic and quadratic parts are not clipped. By the chain rule, with factors \(| \kappa_q^{(b)} | \leqslant Cr_q^{1 - b}\), the clipped correction obeys the same bounds (11.2) globally:

\(\displaystyle s (D^j \tilde{h}_q) \leqslant Cg_q qr_q^{2 - j} (j = 2, 3, 4), \qquad s (D^2 \partial_q \tilde{h}_q) \leqslant Cg_q,\) (12.2)

for all \(y \in H_N\); the scale derivative includes the motion of the clip, \(\partial_q \kappa_q = O (r_q / q)\).

Lemma 12.1. (Global coercivity) For \(q_{\ast}\) large, for all \(y \in H_N\) and \(q \geqslant q_{\ast}\),

\(\displaystyle \) \(\displaystyle c \hspace{0.17em} (K_q + \gamma y^2) \leqslant H_q (y)^{- 1} \leqslant C \hspace{0.17em} (K_q + \gamma y^2),\) \(\displaystyle \)
\(\displaystyle \) \(\displaystyle \|H_q (y)\|_{p \to p} \leqslant \frac{1 + C (g_q + g_q^2)}{q} \quad (1 \leqslant p \leqslant \infty), \qquad \text{diag} H_q (y)^2 \leqslant Ca_2 (q) .\) \(\displaystyle \) (12.3)

Idea. \(H^{- 1} = K_q + \gamma y^2 + M_q + D^2 \tilde{h}_q\), and \(M_q + D^2 \tilde{h}_q\) is small relative to \(K_q\): \(\|D^2 \tilde{h}_q \| \leqslant Cg_q q\), and \(M_q\) is small after multiplication by \(C_q\) (the centred kernel is controlled by its first moment, Exercise 12.1). Write \(H = (I + G (M + D^2 \tilde{h}))^{- 1} G\) with \(G = (K_q + \gamma y^2)^{- 1}\) and use the Neumann series and Lemma 10.1.\(\Box\)

So \(\bar{\Gamma}_q\) is a globally convex action, and Theorem 10.6 applies.

12.3The complete defect

On the set \(\{\|y\|_{\infty} \leqslant r_q \}\) the clip is the identity and the defect is \(e^{\text{o}}\), of size (11.12). Outside it, the clipped action does not solve the Wetterich equation even approximately in relative terms. For \(q \geqslant q_{\ast}\) split the drift into its bounded-field and large-field parts,

\(\displaystyle b^{\text{b}}_q (y) := - H_q (y) \hspace{0.17em} D \kappa_q (y) \hspace{0.17em} De^{\text{o}}_q (\kappa_q y), \qquad b^{\text{l}}_q := b_q - b^{\text{b}}_q,\)

and let \(\chi_q (y) = \mathbb{1}_{|y| > r_q}\) (pointwise) and \(t_q (y) = y \hspace{0.17em} \chi_q (y)\), the field restricted to the large-field set.

Proposition 12.2. (Complete source bounds [FBSDE26]) For \(q \geqslant q_{\ast}\), uniformly in \(y \in H_N\), \(N\) and \(p \in [1, \infty]\),

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} \|b^{\text{b}}_q (y)\|_p & \leqslant C \gamma^{7 / 2} q^{- 5 / 2},\\ \|b^{\text{l}}_q (y)\|_p & \leqslant C \gamma q^{- 3 / 2} (\|t_q \|_p + r_q^{- 1} \|y^2 \chi_q \|_p),\\ \|b^{\text{l}}_q (y)\|_2^2 & \leqslant C \gamma^4 q^{- 5} \|t_q \|_6^6 . \hspace{3cm} \text{(12.4)} \end{array}\)

Moreover \(\|b_q (y)\|_p \leqslant C \gamma q^{- 3 / 2} \|y\|_p\).

The first bound is (11.13) with \(n = 2\): on the ball \(|De^{\text{o}} | \leqslant \textsf{S}_4 (e^{\text{o}}) (2 r)^3 / 6\), and \(q^{- 1} \gamma^5 q^{- 3} r_q^3 = \gamma^{7 / 2} q^{- 5 / 2}\). The large-field part is as large as the bound \(\gamma q^{- 3 / 2} |y| = (g_q / q) |y|\) on the whole drift (no smallness beyond the factor \(g_q\) already present in \(b\)), but it is supported on the large-field set (up to nonlocal tails controlled by operator bounds). The last bound follows from the second since on \(\{|y| > r\}\), \(|t| + r^{- 1} y^2 \leqslant 2 r^{- 2} |t|^3\), and \((\gamma q^{- 3 / 2} r^{- 2})^2 = \gamma^4 q^{- 5}\).

Where the large-field error comes from.
Compute the gradient of the defect of the clipped action and compare it with \(D \kappa \hspace{0.17em} De^{\text{o}} (\kappa y)\). With \(z = \kappa_q (y)\), \(J = D \kappa_q (y)\) (multiplication by \(\tau' (y / r)\)), \(h = h^{[2]}_q\), \(M' = \partial_q M_q\), and the unclipped response \(H^{\text{o}} = (K + M + \gamma z^2 + D^2 h (z))^{- 1}\), the difference has four groups (seven terms):

  1. the mass term \(M' y - JM' z\): the quadratic part is not clipped, but its flow was computed at \(z\);

  2. the motion of the clip, \(JD^2 h (z) \partial_q \kappa + (D \partial_q \kappa)^{\ast} Dh (z)\);

  3. \(\gamma (y - Jz) \text{diag} H^2\) and \(\gamma Jz \text{diag} (H^2 - (H^{\text{o}})^2)\): the quartic vertex sees \(y\), the correction sees \(z\), and the two responses differ by \(\gamma (y^2 - z^2)\) and by the chain-rule terms of the clipped Hessian, all supported on \(\{|y| > r_q \}\);

  4. the contraction of \(D^3 (h \circ \kappa) - JD^3 h (z)\) (the chain rule produces terms with \(\kappa''\), \(\kappa'''\), supported on large fields) and of \(D^3 h\) with \(H^2 - (H^{\text{o}})^2\).

Each group vanishes if \(\|y\|_{\infty} \leqslant r_q\), and each is bounded by \(g_q\) times \(|t|\) or \(r^{- 1} y^2 \chi\), in \(L^p\), by the same loop estimates ([FBSDE26]; Exercise 12.2 for the one-site version). There is no cancellation to exploit: the clipping error is genuinely of the size \((g_q / q) |y|\) of the whole drift on the large-field set. What saves us is that this set is rarely visited, and its occupation is controlled by the coercivity of the actual dynamics. Lecture 6 proves exactly this, with the weighted large-field action

\(\displaystyle \int_{q_{\ast}}^{\infty} q^{- 3 / 2} \hspace{0.17em} \gamma \|t_q (Y_q)\|_6^6 \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} q,\)

(the \(t\)-part of the large-field action of Lecture 6), whose weight is matched to the last bound in (12.4): \(q^{7 / 2} \cdot \gamma^4 q^{- 5} = \gamma^3 \cdot (\gamma q^{- 3 / 2})\), so \(\int q^{7 / 2} \|b^{\text{l}}_q \|_2^2 \mathrm{d} q \leqslant C \gamma^3 \int q^{- 3 / 2} \gamma \|t_q \|_6^6 \mathrm{d} q\), where \(q^{7 / 2}\) is the weight with which \(\|b^{\text{l}} \|_2^2\) enters the backward representation (Lecture 6).

The coordinate correction.
Finally, write the approximate coordinate equation as

\(\displaystyle K_q \Phi_q = K_q (Y_q + \varrho_q (Y_q)) + \tfrac{\gamma}{3} Y_q^3, \qquad \varrho_q (y) := C_q (M_q y + D \tilde{h}_q (y)),\) (12.5)

using \(DU (y) = \lambda y^3 = \frac{\gamma}{3} y^3\). The correction is a small contraction, \(\|D \varrho_q (y)\|_{p \to p} \leqslant \varepsilon_q := C (g_q + g_q^2)\), for all \(y\) and \(1 \leqslant p \leqslant \infty\), and \(\varrho_q (0) = 0\) by evenness. So, up to a small Lipschitz perturbation, the forecast is the solution of the cubic elliptic equation \(K_q (Y + \varrho) + \frac{\gamma}{3} Y^3 = K_q \Phi_q\) driven by the field at resolution \(q\). This is the equation whose coercivity Lecture 6 exploits.

12.4Why the Polchinski expansion is not enough

Lecture 4 gives two exact descriptions of the flow: the Polchinski equation for the force \(F\) at the field \(\Phi\), and the Wetterich equation at the forecast \(\psi\). We use the second, here and in Lecture 6. The first looks more economical, and the following programme is natural. Keep the bare cube \(- \lambda \varphi^3\) unclipped, so that the approximate force stays coercive at all fields. Expand the rest of the force in perturbation theory and clip only these higher orders. Pay for the clipping in the source of the equation for the remainder \(R = F - f\), using the a priori estimates. No Legendre transform would be needed.

Here we test the programme where everything can be computed: on one site. There is no spatial structure and no renormalisation, so whatever goes wrong has nothing to do with divergences. The result is that a frozen cube is the wrong approximation at large fields: the remainder must contain a cube of its own, and it cancels the coercivity that the programme relies on. The cure is to integrate the cube, that is, to solve the transport part of the flow exactly. This produces a global approximation with a uniformly small error, and it is precisely the forecast coordinate of Section 10.5.

Conventions. As in Exercise 10.4: \(N = 1\), \(\Delta = 0\), \(K_q = q\), \(V (\varphi) = \frac{\lambda}{4} \varphi^4\), \(\gamma = 3 \lambda\) and \(r_q = (q / \gamma)^{1 / 2}\). We write \(S = C_q = 1 / q\) for the remaining covariance, and \(x\) for the value of the field at resolution \(q\).

The exact force is linear at large fields.
The effective potential and the force are

\(\displaystyle V_q (x) = - \log \mathbb{E} \exp (- \frac{\lambda}{4} (x + S^{1 / 2} \xi)^4), \quad F_q = - V_q',\)

with \(\xi\) a standard Gaussian, and they solve the Polchinski and Burgers equations

\(\displaystyle \partial_q V + \frac{1}{2} S^2 V'' = \frac{1}{2} S^2 (V')^2, \quad \partial_q F + \frac{1}{2} S^2 F'' + S^2 FF' = 0. \) (12.6)

Let \(\mathbb{E}_x\) be the expectation for the probability density proportional to \(\exp (- \frac{\lambda}{4} y^4 - \frac{q}{2} (y - x)^2)\), and \(m_q (x) =\mathbb{E}_x Y\). Differentiating \(V_q\), and integrating by parts in \(y\), gives

\(\displaystyle F_q (x) = - \lambda \mathbb{E}_x Y^3 = q (m_q (x) - x) . \) (12.7)

This is (10.9): the posterior mean is the forecast, \(\psi_q = m_q (\Phi_q)\). So \(F_q\) measures how far the forecast lies behind the field.

Lemma 12.3. (Posterior mean) For all \(x\) and \(q\), \(|m_q (x) | \leqslant 2 \lambda^{- 1 / 4} + 2 (q|x| / \lambda)^{1 / 3}\).

Proof. Integrating the derivative of \(y\) times the density gives \(\lambda \mathbb{E}_x Y^4 + q\mathbb{E}_x Y^2 - qx\mathbb{E}_x Y = 1\). With \(M = (\mathbb{E}_x Y^4)^{1 / 4}\) this gives \(\lambda M^4 \leqslant 1 + q|x|M\), and Young's inequality gives \(\frac{\lambda}{2} M^4 \leqslant 1 + (q|x|)^{4 / 3} (2 \lambda)^{- 1 / 3}\). Finally \(|m_q | \leqslant M\).\(\Box\)

Therefore, by (12.7), the two regimes of the force are

\(\displaystyle F_q (x) \to - \lambda x^3 \quad (\text{fixed } x, q \to \infty), \quad F_q (x) = - qx + O (|x|^{1 / 3}) \quad (\text{fixed } q, |x| \to \infty) . \) (12.8)

The limits cannot be interchanged. The crossover is at \(|x| \sim r_q\), where the cube equals the Gaussian restoring force: \(\lambda r_q^3 \sim qr_q\). Beyond it the force is linear, because convolution with a Gaussian of variance \(S\) cannot produce a force steeper than \(x / S\).

Proposition 12.4. (The remainder of a frozen cube) Let \(f (x) = - \lambda x^3 + f_1 (x)\) with \(f_1 (x) = o (|x|^3)\), for instance linear terms plus bounded clipped corrections. At every fixed \(q\), the remainder \(R_q = F_q - f\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle \frac{R_q (x)}{\lambda x^3} \to 1 \quad (|x| \to \infty) .\)

So no estimate can make \(R\) a small fraction of the retained cube uniformly in the field. The cube in \(R\) is not a new counterterm, since there is nothing to renormalise here. It is the compensation for having frozen the leading term of a force that does not stay cubic.

The field equation does not see the cube.
The field equation (18.18) of Lecture 6 on one site is \(q_0 \Phi_q = J_q F_q (\Phi_q) + \eta_q\), where \(J_q = (q - q_0) / q\). Its noise is the filtered measurement noise, so \(\mathbb{E} \exp (h \eta_q) \leqslant \exp ((q - q_0) h^2 / 2)\) whatever the interaction (Lemma 13.2 of Lecture 6). With the frozen cube \(f = - \lambda x^3\) the equation looks coercive:

\(\displaystyle q_0 x + J_q \lambda x^3 = J_q R_q (x) + \eta_q (x) .\)

But by Proposition 12.4, \(J_q R_q\) cancels the cube on the left side at large fields. Inserting (12.7) shows what is left:

\(\displaystyle qx = (q - q_0) m_q (x) + \eta_q (x) . \) (12.9)

There is still confinement, but it is linear, and it comes from the sublinear growth of \(m_q\).

Proposition 12.5. (Tails on one site) If \(q^2 \geqslant 24 \lambda\), then \(|m_q (x) | \leqslant \frac{9}{10} |x|\) for \(|x| \geqslant r_q\), and for \(R \geqslant r_q\)

\(\displaystyle \mathbb{P}(| \Phi_q | \geqslant R) \leqslant 2 \exp (- \frac{q^2 R^2}{200 (q - q_0)}) .\)

Proof. The mean \(m\) has the sign of \(x\), the density being an even density tilted by \(\mathrm{e}^{qxy}\). Jensen's inequality in the identity of Lemma 12.3 gives \(\lambda m^4 + qm^2 - qxm \leqslant 1\). If \(x \geqslant r_q\) and \(m > \frac{9}{10} x\), then \(\lambda m^3 + q (m - x) > x (\frac{729}{1000} \lambda x^2 - \frac{q}{10}) \geqslant \frac{143}{1000} qx\), as \(\lambda x^2 \geqslant q / 3\), so the left side is at least \(\frac{1287}{10000} qx^2 \geqslant \frac{429}{10000} q^2 / \lambda > 1\), a contradiction. So \(| \eta_q (x) | \geqslant \frac{q}{10} |x|\) by (12.9), and we conclude with the sub-Gaussian bound.\(\Box\)

At \(R = r_q\) the exponent is at least \(q^2 / (600 \lambda)\). This estimate treats the combined restoring force through the posterior moment. It never uses the cube as a coercive term against a remainder regarded as a source.

Remark 12.6. (This does not transfer to the lattice) With a quadratic term \(\frac{r}{2} y^2\) in the action, Lemma 12.3 acquires the term \(((q + r)_- / \lambda)^{1 / 2}\). On the lattice the bare mass makes \(r\) divergent and negative, so this elementary moment bound is not uniform until the renormalised cancellations have been built in. The scalar tail bound is therefore not an independent input for the field theory.

What clipping does to the Polchinski equation.
For a potential \(U\) put \(D_q = \partial_q + \frac{1}{2} S^2 \partial_x^2\), \(B (U, U) = \frac{1}{2} S^2 (U')^2\) and \(\mathcal{E}_q (U) = D_q U - B (U, U)\). So \(\mathcal{E}_q (V_q) = 0\), and the force \(f = - U'\) has Burgers defect \(E = \partial_q f + \frac{1}{2} S^2 f'' + S^2 ff' = - \partial_x \mathcal{E}_q (U)\). Perturbation theory solves

\(\displaystyle D_q U_1 = 0, \quad D_q U_2 = B (U_1, U_1),\)

with \(U_1 = \frac{\lambda}{4} (x^4 + 6 Sx^2 + 3 S^2)\), whose leading term is the cube in the force, and \(U_2 = - \frac{1}{2} \lambda^2 Sx^6 + \ldots\), the first tree. Keep \(U_1\) unclipped and clip the correction: \(U^c = U_1 + U_2 \circ \kappa_q\), with the clip \(\kappa_q\) of (12.1). Writing \(\mathcal{C}U_2 = U_2 \circ \kappa_q\), a direct expansion gives the exact identity

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{E}_q (U^c) = (\mathcal{C}- 1) B (U_1, U_1) + [D_q, \mathcal{C}] U_2 - 2 B (U_1, \mathcal{C}U_2) - B (\mathcal{C}U_2, \mathcal{C}U_2) . \) (12.10)

Without the clip only the last two terms, of higher order, would be present. The clip reintroduces a defect at exactly the order that \(U_2\) was built to cancel. For \(|x| \geqslant 2 r_q\) the term \(\mathcal{C}U_2\) does not depend on \(x\), so \(\mathcal{E}_q (U^c) = c (q) - \frac{1}{2} S^2 (U_1')^2\), and the force defect is exactly

\(\displaystyle E_q (x) = S^2 U_1' (x) U_1'' (x) = 3 \lambda^2 S^2 x^5 (1 + O (S / x^2)) . \) (12.11)

This is the price of the clipping. At the edge of the clip it has size \((q / \lambda)^{1 / 2}\), which is not integrable in \(q\), so it can only be paid through the small probability of reaching the clip. Along the exact process \(\hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} \Phi_q = S^2 F_q (\Phi_q) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} q + S \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W_q\), Itô's formula gives for \(R_q = F_q (\Phi_q) - f_q^c (\Phi_q)\) the equation

\(\displaystyle \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} R_q = - [E_q (\Phi_q) + S^2 (f_q^c)' (\Phi_q) R_q] \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} q + \Xi_q \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W_q . \) (12.12)

The transport term has a favourable sign at large fields: \((f^c)' = - 3 \lambda (x^2 + S)\) for \(|x| \geqslant 2 r_q\), which is the dissipation one hopes to use (near the clip the sign fails: on the ball the quintic takes over beyond \(|x| \approx 0.77 r_q\)). But at large fields the bracket is \(3 \lambda^2 S^2 x^5 - 3 \lambda S^2 x^2 R\), and for the true \(R \sim \lambda x^3\) the two terms cancel at leading order. The source and the dissipation are the same quantity, so estimating them separately gives nothing. To close, one would need, for instance, a signed estimate that retains this cancellation, or a tail bound for \(\Phi\) obtained independently of \(R\). On one site Proposition 12.5 is such a bound: the probability of reaching the clip is at most of order \(\exp (- cq^2 / \lambda)\), so in expectation the source is exponentially small, and the frozen cube is not ruled out there. But that bound comes from the posterior moment, and by the remark above it does not transfer. The field equation alone cannot replace it, since its restoring force is the cube and \(R\) together, which is what we are trying to estimate.

Going further in perturbation theory does not help: any finite depth of expansion leaves the same structure (Exercise 12.5), and we shall see in a moment that the series of trees stops converging inside \(r_q\).

Integrating the cube.
The term responsible for all this is the nonlinear transport in (12.6). So solve it exactly: let \(f_q\) solve the Burgers equation without diffusion,

\(\displaystyle \partial_q f + S^2 ff' = 0, \quad f_{\infty} (x) = - \lambda x^3 . \) (12.13)

The characteristics give the solution in closed form.

Proposition 12.7. (The integrated cube) For \(x \in \mathbb{R}\), let \(y = y_q (x)\) be the unique real root of \(qy + \lambda y^3 = qx\). Then the solution of (12.13) is

\(\displaystyle f_q (x) = - \lambda y_q (x)^3 = q (y_q (x) - x), \) (12.14)

and it has the following properties.

  1. Variational form. \(y_q (x)\) is the minimiser of \(\frac{\lambda}{4} y^4 + \frac{q}{2} (y - x)^2\), and \(f_q = - \bar{V}_q'\) for the minimum value \(\bar{V}_q (x)\).

  2. Small fields. \(f_q (x) = q \sum_{k \geqslant 1} (- \lambda / q)^k c_k x^{2 k + 1}\) with \(c_k = \frac{1}{2 k + 1} \frac{(3 k) !}{k! (2 k) !}\), so \(f_q = - \lambda x^3 + 3 \lambda^2 Sx^5 - 12 \lambda^3 S^2 x^7 + \ldots\). This is the sum of the tree expansion. Its radius of convergence is \(|x| = \frac{2}{3} r_q\).

  3. Large fields. \(\lambda |y|^3 \leqslant q|x|\), so \(f_q (x) = - qx + O (|x|^{1 / 3})\) at fixed \(q\), as for the exact force, and \(f_q' = - \gamma y^2 / (1 + \gamma y^2 / q) \leqslant 0\) for all \(x\).

  4. Defect. \(f_q\) fails to solve Burgers by the diffusion term alone, which is \(q\) times the drift of Exercise 10.4:

    \(\displaystyle E_q = \frac{1}{2} S^2 f_q'' (x) = qb_q (y_q (x)), \quad b_q (y) = - \gamma y (q + \gamma y^2)^{- 3} . \) (12.15)

Proof. Differentiate \(x = y + \lambda Sy^3\) with \(d = 1 + \gamma y^2 / q\): \(y' = 1 / d\), \(\partial_S y = - \lambda y^3 / d\), \(f' = - \gamma y^2 / d\) and \(f'' = - 2 \gamma y / d^3\). Hence \(\partial_S f = \gamma \lambda y^5 / d = ff'\), which is (12.13) as \(\partial_q = - S^2 \partial_S\). For (a), the stationarity condition is \(\lambda y^3 + q (y - x) = 0\). For (b), use Lagrange inversion on \(y = x - \lambda Sy^3\); the nearest singularity is where \(d = 0\), that is at \(y = \pm \mathrm{i} r_q\), \(x = \pm \frac{2}{3} \mathrm{i} r_q\). Finally (c) and (d) are read off from the formulas, since \(d = 1 / (qH_q (y))\) with \(H_q (y) = (q + \gamma y^2)^{- 1}\).\(\Box\)

Item (b) explains why the depth of the expansion is not the issue. The tree series has the same complex singularity \(y = \pm \mathrm{i} r_q\) as the one-loop seed of Section 12.1, and its truncations at even \(k\) point outward at large fields.

The defect is now bounded for all fields, and it gives a global estimate.

Theorem 12.8. (Global error of the integrated cube) Let \(C_{\ast} = 3 \sup_z |z| (1 + 3 z^2)^{- 3}\), so \(C_{\ast} < \frac{1}{2}\). For all \(q > 0\),

\(\displaystyle \sup_{x \in \mathbb{R}} |F_q (x) - f_q (x) | \leqslant 2 C_{\ast} (\lambda / q)^{1 / 2} . \) (12.16)

Proof. Use \(S = 1 / q\) as time, starting from \(S = 0\). Subtracting, \(\hat{R} = F - f\) solves the linear problem

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{D} \hat{R} = \frac{1}{2} f'', \quad \mathcal{D}= \partial_S - \frac{1}{2} \partial_x^2 - F \partial_x - f', \quad \hat{R}_0 = 0.\)

The zeroth-order coefficient has the good sign, as \(- f' \geqslant 0\). With \(z = (\lambda S)^{1 / 2} y\), the source obeys \(\frac{1}{2} |f'' | \leqslant C_{\ast} (\lambda / S)^{1 / 2}\), which is integrable at \(S = 0\), so \(\beta (S) = 2 C_{\ast} (\lambda S)^{1 / 2}\) satisfies \(\mathcal{D} \beta \geqslant \frac{1}{2} |f'' |\). To apply the maximum principle on the line, take \(\chi = \mathrm{e}^{3 S} (1 + x^4)\). Then \(\mathcal{D} \chi \geqslant 0\), since \(6 x^2 \leqslant 3 (1 + x^4)\) and \(xF (x) \leqslant 0\); the last holds because \(F = - \lambda \mathbb{E}_x Y^3\), and \(\mathbb{E}_x Y^3\) has the sign of \(x\), the density being an even density tilted by \(\mathrm{e}^{qxy}\). Both \(F\) and \(f\) grow at most cubically, uniformly for bounded \(S\): \(|f| \leqslant \lambda |x|^3\) since \(|y| \leqslant |x|\), and \(\lambda \mathbb{E}_x Y^6 + q\mathbb{E}_x Y^4 - qx\mathbb{E}_x Y^3 = 3\mathbb{E}_x Y^2\) gives \(\mathbb{E}_x Y^4 \leqslant (|x| + (3 S)^{1 / 2})^4\), up to constants, hence \(|F| \leqslant C \lambda (|x|^3 + S^{3 / 2})\). Also \(\hat{R}\) is continuous at \(S = 0\), since the posterior concentrates at \(x\). So \(\pm \hat{R} - \beta - \varepsilon \chi\) is negative on the boundary of a large cylinder, hence inside it. Let the cylinder grow and then \(\varepsilon \to 0\).\(\Box\)

What this says about the choice of equation.
The comparison on one site is summarised as follows, for \(|x| \gg r_q\).

frozen cube, clipped corrections integrated cube
growth of \(f\) cubic linear, as for \(F\)
remainder \(|F - f|\) \(\sim \lambda |x|^3\) \(\leqslant 2 C_{\ast} (\lambda / q)^{1 / 2}\)
Burgers defect \(3 \lambda^2 S^2 x^5\) \(qb_q (y)\), bounded by \(C_{\ast} \lambda^{1 / 2} q^{- 3 / 2}\)

The integrated cube is the forecast coordinate. The equation \(qy + \lambda y^3 = qx\), at \(x = \Phi_q = P_q / q\), is the coordinate equation of Exercise 10.4, so \(y_q (\Phi_q) = Y_q\). Equations (12.7) and (12.14) then say that \(F_q - f_q = q (m_q - y_q)\): the remainder is \(K_q Z_q\), and the error \(Z\) of (10.15) is the posterior mean minus the posterior mode. By (12.16), on one site \(|Z_q | \leqslant 2 C_{\ast} \lambda^{1 / 2} q^{- 3 / 2}\) surely, for all fields.

Why Wetterich has no such problem. In the variable \(y\) the bare quartic does not flow at all: all the trees of (b) are contained in the map \(x \mapsto y\). The right side of (10.13) is a loop, and the defect \(e_q (y) = - \frac{1}{2} [H_q (y) - S]\) of \(\bar{\Gamma}_q = \frac{\lambda}{4} y^4\) is bounded by \(S / 2\). This is why Section 12.2 can leave the quartic untouched and clip only the corrections: what is clipped consists of loops, not of trees whose large-field sum is forced to change the growth of the action. This removes the frozen-cube problem specifically: the clipping error of Section 12.3 is still there, and it is what Lecture 6 pays for.

Where the coercivity is. Compare the same observation written in the two coordinates:

\(\displaystyle qy + \lambda y^3 = P_q, \quad qx = (q - q_0) m_q (x) + \eta_q .\)

In the coordinate \(y\) the cube is genuine, with the known right side \(P_q = (q - q_0) \psi_q + \eta_q\). This is the equation (13.4) of Lecture 6, whose coercivity the a priori estimates exploit. In the coordinate \(x\) the cube has already been used up, and only a linear restoring force is visible.

What the model points to. What the scalar model points to is the integration of the cube. The Wetterich equation and the FBSDE of Theorem 10.6 are one way to organise it; another would keep the original field, with \(f^{(0)}\) given by the variational formula of (a), and work with the defect \(\frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} (C_q^2 D^2 f^{(0)})\). On the lattice the variational formula still defines this force, with a nonlocal response, but its defect contains the loop contractions, starting with the tadpole, and must be renormalised. A complete proof along that route is not given in these lectures.

The scalar model proves less than a no-go theorem. It shows that a global estimate treating the remainder of a frozen cube as a small cubic perturbation is false. It does not show that every construction based on the Polchinski equation fails, nor does it give the asymptotics of the force on the lattice. There is also a limit to the integrated cube itself. With a quadratic term, the map \(y \mapsto y + S (\lambda y^3 + ry)\) has derivative \(1 + S (r + 3 \lambda y^2)\), which vanishes somewhere when \(1 + Sr < 0\) (Exercise 12.7). A divergent negative mass cannot simply be placed inside this monotone map, and this is one reason why the mass correction \(M_q\) of (12.1) is kept apart from the quartic and is required to be small relative to \(K_q\) (Lemma 12.1).

12.5The action below the auxiliary scale

The action above is built for \(q \geqslant q_{\ast}\), because its expansion needs \(g_q\) small. Nothing below \(q_{\ast}\) requires a construction: we freeze it. For \(0 < q < q_{\ast}\) set

\(\displaystyle \bar{\Gamma}_q (y) = \bar{\Gamma}_{q_{\ast}} (y) + \frac{1}{2} (q_{\ast} - q) \|y\|_2^2 . \) (12.17)

Then \(K_q + D^2 \bar{\Gamma}_q = K_{q_{\ast}} + D^2 \bar{\Gamma}_{q_{\ast}}\), so the coordinate equation \(K_q y + D \bar{\Gamma}_q (y) = P\) reads \(K_{q_{\ast}} y + D \bar{\Gamma}_{q_{\ast}} (y) = P\). Thus for \(q < q_{\ast}\), with \(x = C_q P\),

\(\displaystyle H_q = H_{q_{\ast}}, \quad y_q (x) = y_{q_{\ast}} (C_{q_{\ast}} K_q x), \quad C_{q_{\ast}} K_q = I - (q_{\ast} - q) C_{q_{\ast}} . \) (12.18)

The Legendre computation of Theorem 10.6 holds for any such family. So the system (10.16) holds on \([q_0, \infty)\), with \(Y_{q_0} = 0\) for \(\nu_N\): the initial value is deterministic, and no large mass is used. The action is continuous in \(q\) and piecewise \(C^1\), which is all that Itô's formula needs. The defect for \(s < q_{\ast}\) is explicit:

\(\displaystyle e_s (y) = - \frac{1}{2} \|y\|_2^2 + \Upsilon (y), \quad \Upsilon (y) = - \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} [H_{q_{\ast}} (y) - H_{q_{\ast}} (0)] . \) (12.19)

Here \(D \Upsilon\) is the trace part of the gradient of \(e_{q_{\ast}}\); it obeys \(\|D \Upsilon (y)\|_p \leqslant Cg_{q_{\ast}} \|y\|_p\), which is part of the proof of the global linear bound of Proposition 12.2 [FBSDE26]. As \(\Upsilon (0) = 0\), uniformly in \(N\), for \(s < q_{\ast}\),

\(\displaystyle |e_s (y) | \leqslant C \|y\|_2^2, \quad \|b_s (y)\|_p \leqslant C \|y\|_p, \quad \text{diag} H_s^2 \leqslant C, \) (12.20)

with \(b = - HDe = H (y - D \Upsilon)\). Here we used \(\|H\|_{p \to p} \leqslant C / q_{\ast}\). These are all the properties of \(s < q_{\ast}\) we use. Note that \(e\) jumps at \(q_{\ast}\); the jump is harmless, and the measure does not depend on \(q_{\ast}\). More precisely \(\bar{\Gamma}, y, e, b, Y, Z\) depend on \(q_{\ast}\), while, with \(\mu_N\) fixed (\(b_N\) then depends on \(q_{\ast}\), Theorem 11.2), \(\nu\), \(\psi\), \(\text{Cov}\) and the law of \(P\) do not.

12.6Summary

At the end of this lecture we have, at every lattice cutoff:

  • a counterterm \(\mu_N\), a mass path \(M_q\) and a globally convex action \(\bar{\Gamma}_q = U + \frac{1}{2} \langle y, M_q y \rangle + h^{[2]}_q \circ \kappa_q\), for \(q \geqslant q_{\ast}\) and frozen below it, all deterministic and constructed without any information on \(\nu_N\);

  • the exact FBSDE started from zero (10.16) for the approximate forecast \(Y\) and the error \(Z\), with noise coefficients bounded, above \(q_{\ast}\), as in Lemma 12.1;

  • above \(q_{\ast}\), a source \(b = b^{\text{b}} + b^{\text{l}}\), whose bounded-field part is integrable in the backward equation thanks to the two correction steps, and whose large-field part is controlled by sixth powers of \(Y\) on the large-field set.

Lecture 6 turns these deterministic bounds into estimates on \((Y, Z)\) uniform in \(N\).
