19Elementary kernels

We use the notation of Lecture 6: \(K_q = q - \Delta_N\), \(C_q = K_q^{- 1}\), \(G_q (y) = (K_q + \lambda y^2)^{- 1}\), \(H\) for the inverses of \(K_q + D^2 \Gamma\), \(M_q = \mu_q I + \Theta_q\) for the quadratic path, with \(\Theta_q = \Theta^{[3]}_q\) for the final one and \(M^{[j]}\) for the level masses of Section 20.4, \(\kappa_q\) for the clip, \(\varrho_q\) for the coordinate correction, and \(\text{Ctr}_E T\) for contraction. Recall \(g_q = \lambda q^{- 1 / 2}\) and \(r_q = (q / \lambda)^{1 / 2}\), so that \(\lambda r_q^2 = q\). Generic convolution kernels are denoted \(E, E'\), and the letters \(L, L'\) denote arbitrary kernels. Distance \(d\) is torus distance.

Forces and their primitives.
Lecture 6 is written in terms of forces. The proofs below expand the trace, so they are written for the primitives of those forces: \(h^{[j]}\) with \(Dh^{[j]} = \ell^{[j]}\) for the corrections (11.8), \(h^{[1]} =\mathcal{L}\) with \(D\mathcal{L}= \ell^{[1]}\) for the seed (11.6), \(\bar{\Gamma}\) with \(D \bar{\Gamma} = \bar{\mathcal{F}}\), \(e\) with \(De =\mathcal{D}\), and the trace map \(\frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} [(K + M + D^2 (U + h))^{- 1} - (K + M)^{- 1}]\), whose derivative is the loop force \(\mathcal{T}\) of Lecture 6. A vertex with \(k\) legs is \(D^k\) of the primitive and \(D^{k - 1}\) of the force, so the norms \(\textsf{S}_k\) and \(\mathcal{N}_k\) agree. No additive constant of a primitive enters any estimate.

There are two scales, which must not be confused. The reference mass \(q_0 > 0\) has arbitrary size; it fixes the covariance \(C = C_{q_0}\) of the measure, and it is the lower end of the range of Lemma 19.1. The auxiliary scale \(q_{\ast} \geqslant \text{max} (1, q_0)\) is large, depending on \(\lambda\) and on the bound \(B_0\) below; all the operators of Sections 19–21 after Lemma 19.1 are used at scales \(q \geqslant q_{\ast}\) and do not involve \(q_0\). So from Lemma 19.3 on we assume \(q \geqslant 1\). Constants may depend on the coupling and on finitely many derivative orders, but not on \(N\) or on the scale interval. For a kernel, \(\|C\|_{\text{row}} = \sup_x \int |C (x, z) | \mathrm{d} z\) is the row norm (Section 10.1 of Lecture 6); it bounds \(\|C\|_{p \to p}\) for every \(p\) when \(C\) is symmetric.

Lemma 19.1. (Free and killed resolvents) This is Lemma 10.1 of Lecture 6, with further items. For \(q \geqslant q_0\), uniformly in \(N\), with constants that depend on \(q_0\) only through scales \(q < 1\): the kernel of \(C_q\) is nonnegative, \(C_q 1 = q^{- 1} 1\), \(\|C_q \|_{p \to p} = q^{- 1}\) for \(1 \leqslant p \leqslant \infty\), and

\(\displaystyle a_2 (q) : = \text{Tr} C_q^2 \lesssim q^{- 1 / 2} \wedge N^3 q^{- 2}, \quad \text{Tr} C_q \lesssim N \wedge N^3 q^{- 1}, \quad \int d (0, z) C_q (0, z)^2 \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} z \leqslant Cq^{- 1} . \) (19.1)

For \(y \in H_N\) and \(\lambda > 0\),

\(\displaystyle 0 \leqslant G_q (y) (x, z) \leqslant C_q (x, z), \quad G_q (y) (q + \lambda y^2) = 1. \) (19.2)

Proof. The heat integral \(C_q = \int_0^{\infty} \mathrm{e}^{- qt} \mathrm{e}^{t \Delta_N} \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} t\) proves positivity, the row mass and the operator norms. Killing the lattice random walk at rate \(\lambda y^2\) proves the first part of (19.2). The second part is \((K_q + \lambda y^2) 1 = q + \lambda y^2\).

The symbol of \(- \Delta_N\) is \(4 N^2 \sum_i \text{sin}^2 (\pi n_i / N)\), with \(k = 2 \pi n\), comparable to \(|k|^2\) on the centred frequency cube. Thus \(a_2 (q) = \sum_k (q + c_k)^{- 2}\) and \(\text{Tr} C_q = \sum_k (q + c_k)^{- 1}\), with \(c_k\) this symbol, \(c_0 = 0\). Counting the \(N^3\) modes gives the bounds \(N^3 q^{- 2}\) and \(N^3 q^{- 1}\), with universal constants. Nonzero modes have \(|k| \geqslant 2 \pi\). Splitting at \(|k| = \sqrt{q}\) bounds \(\sum_{k \neq 0} (q + c|k|^2)^{- 2}\) by \(Cq^{- 1 / 2}\) for all \(q > 0\), and \(\sum_{k \neq 0} (q + c|k|^2)^{- 1} \leqslant CN\). The zero mode contributes \(q^{- 2}\) to \(a_2\) and \(q^{- 1}\) to the trace: for \(q \geqslant 1\) these are at most \(q^{- 1 / 2}\) and \(1\), and for \(q_0 \leqslant q < 1\) they are at most \(q_0^{- 3 / 2} q^{- 1 / 2}\) and \(q_0^{- 1}\).

Let \(q \geqslant 1\). A dyadic shell is a smooth Fourier cutoff supported where the frequency magnitude is comparable to \(L\), with \(L\) ranging over powers of two; together with a low-frequency cutoff these form a partition of unity. Such a shell of radius \(L \leqslant CN\) has kernel bounded by \(C_m L^3 (q + L^2)^{- 1} (1 + Ld)^{- m}\). This follows by summation by parts; on the final shell use the periodic sine symbol. Summing gives \(C_q (0, z) \leqslant C \text{min} (d^{- 1}, q^{- 1} d^{- 3})\) off the diagonal. For its weighted square split the radial integral at \(q^{- 1 / 2}\): \(\int_0^{q^{- 1 / 2}} r \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} r\) and \(q^{- 2} \int_{q^{- 1 / 2}}^{O (1)} r^{- 3} \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} r\) are both \(O (q^{- 1})\). Discrete annuli give the same bound above the lattice kinetic scale; the diagonal has zero distance weight.

Let \(q_0 \leqslant q < 1\). Separate the zero mode, whose kernel is the constant \(q^{- 1}\): the kernel of \(C_q - q^{- 1}\) has multiplier \(1_{k \neq 0} (q + c_k)^{- 1}\), and that of \(C_q - q^{- 1} - (C_1 - 1)\) has multiplier bounded by \(c_k^{- 2} 1_{k \neq 0}\), which is summable uniformly in \(N\). Hence \(C_q (0, z) \leqslant q^{- 1} + C + C_1 (0, z)\). Inserting this and the case \(q = 1\) gives \(\int d (0, z) C_q (0, z)^2 \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} z \leqslant C (q^{- 2} + 1) \leqslant C (1 + q_0^{- 1}) q^{- 1}\).\(\Box\)

Remark 19.2. (The zero mode) The dependence on \(q_0\) in Lemma 19.1 is genuine and comes only from the constant mode, whose eigenvalue of \(C_q\) is \(q^{- 1}\). On the one-site lattice \(a_2 (q) = q^{- 2}\) and \(\text{Tr} C_q = q^{- 1}\), so that the bounds \(a_2 \lesssim q^{- 1 / 2}\) and \(\text{Tr} C_q \lesssim N\) cannot be uniform as \(q \to 0\). The constants are universal for \(q \geqslant 1\). Since \(q_{\ast} \geqslant 1\), no constant below depends on \(q_0\) through this lemma.

For a symmetric centred convolution kernel \(\Theta\), that is, with \(\Theta 1 = 0\), put

\(\displaystyle v_1 (\Theta) = \int d (0, z) | \Theta (0, z) | \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} z, \quad \eta_q (\Theta) = q^{- 1 / 2} v_1 (\Theta) .\)

The diagonal is determined by centring. We never need its absolute row norm. Define the convolution algebra and module norms

\(\displaystyle \|E\|_{\mathcal{A}_q} = \int \left( 1 + \sqrt{q} d \right) |E|, \quad \|E\|_{\mathcal{H}_q}^2 = \int \left( 1 + \sqrt{q} d \right) |E|^2 .\)

Here a translation-invariant kernel is identified with its displacement function \(E (z) = E (0, z)\), all integrals are in that displacement, and \((E \ast E') (z) = \int E (z - u) E' (u) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} u\) is convolution. Products \(EE'\) inside a single spatial integral instead mean pointwise products. The triangle inequality for distance, Minkowski, and Cauchy–Schwarz give

\(\displaystyle \|E \ast E' \|_{\mathcal{A}_q} \leqslant \|E\|_{\mathcal{A}_q} \|E' \|_{\mathcal{A}_q}, \quad \|E \ast E' \|_{\mathcal{H}_q} \leqslant \|E\|_{\mathcal{H}_q} \|E' \|_{\mathcal{A}_q}, \quad \int d |EE' | \leqslant q^{- 1 / 2} \|E\|_{\mathcal{H}_q} \|E' \|_{\mathcal{H}_q} . \) (19.3)

Also \(\|C_q^j \|_{\mathcal{H}_q} \leqslant C_j q^{- (j - 1) - 1 / 4}\); for \(j > 1\) use the heat integral bound \(C_q^j \leqslant C_j q^{- (j - 1)} C_{q / 2}\). We also use \(\|C_q \|_{\mathcal{A}_q} \leqslant C / q\). Indeed the unwrapped lattice walk has second moment \(6 t\), so its expected torus distance at time \(t\) is at most \(\sqrt{6 t}\). Integrating against \(\mathrm{e}^{- qt} \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} t\) bounds the first moment of \(C_q\) by \(Cq^{- 3 / 2}\).

Lemma 19.3. (Centre before taking absolute values) Define spatial translation by \((\sigma_z f) (x) = f (x + z)\) and its symmetric second difference by \(\delta_z^2 = \sigma_z + \sigma_{- z} - 2 I\), where \(I\) denotes the identity operator. Then

\(\displaystyle \| \delta_z^2 C_q \|_{\mathcal{A}_q} \leqslant Cq^{- 1 / 2} d (0, z), \quad \|C_q \Theta \|_{\mathcal{A}_q} \leqslant C \eta_q (\Theta) . \) (19.4)

For \(M = \mu I + \Theta\) this implies

\(\displaystyle \|G_q (y) M\|_{\infty \to \infty} + \|C_q^{1 / 2} MC_q^{1 / 2} \|_{2 \to 2} \leqslant C (| \mu | / q + \eta_q (\Theta)) . \) (19.5)

Proof. A shell of radius \(L\) and multiplier size \(a_L\) contributes at most \(Ca_L \text{min} ((Ld)^2, 1) \left( 1 + \sqrt{q} / L + \sqrt{q} d \right)\) to the first norm. Use two derivatives of its kernel when \(Ld < 1\), and translates otherwise. Put \(\Lambda = \sqrt{q}\) and \(t = \Lambda d\). Let \(\vartheta_{\Lambda}\) be a smooth even Fourier multiplier equal to one below frequency \(\Lambda\) and zero above \(2 \Lambda\), with the usual scaled derivative bounds. Decompose

\(\displaystyle C_q = q^{- 1} \vartheta_{\Lambda} - q^{- 1} (- \Delta_N) C_q \vartheta_{\Lambda} + C_q (I - \vartheta_{\Lambda}) .\)

The last two terms have shell sizes \(q^{- 2} L^2\) below \(\Lambda\) and \(L^{- 2}\) above it. After dividing by \(q^{- 1}\) the sums are

\(\displaystyle \sum_{x \leqslant 1} x^2 \text{min} ((xt)^2, 1) (1 + x^{- 1} + t), \quad \sum_{x \geqslant 1} x^{- 2} \text{min} ((xt)^2, 1) (1 + x^{- 1} + t) .\)

Splitting at \(x = t^{- 1}\) bounds each by \(Ct\); for \(t \leqslant 1\) the only borderline high-shell term is \(t^2 (1 + | \log t|) \leqslant Ct\). The first term in the decomposition is bounded by \(Cq^{- 1} t\) too. Above the lattice frequency take \(\vartheta_{\Lambda} = I\); nonzero lattice distances have \(t \geqslant 1\), and the delta kernel has the same bound.

Now use the signed identity \(C_q \Theta = \frac{1}{2} \int \Theta (z) \delta_z^2 C_q \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} z\). The receiving identity \(G \Theta = (I - G \lambda y^2) C_q \Theta\) and \(\|I - G \lambda y^2 \|_{\infty \to \infty} \leqslant 2\) give the row bound. The form bound follows from the commuting Fourier multipliers of \(C_q\) and \(M\) and the row bound on \(C_q M\).\(\Box\)

Remark 19.4. (A necessary endpoint modification) The estimate \(\| \delta_z^2 C_q \|_1 \lesssim d (0, z)^2\) generally has a logarithmic loss at kinetic order two. We use one distance power and pay \(q^{- 1 / 2}\) instead. The recursion below produces exactly the compensating first-moment bound. This choice also rules out an unrecorded Laplacian in weak kernel limits: on \(d < \delta\) a smooth second difference is bounded by \(C \delta d\).

20.1Tensors and the iteration

The kernels of field derivatives will be called vertices. A physical \(k\)-tensor \(T\) represents a multilinear form by

\(\displaystyle T [u_1, \ldots, u_k] = \int T (x_1, \ldots, x_k) \prod_{i = 1}^k u_i (x_i) \prod_{i = 1}^k \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} x_i .\)

A slot means one argument \(x_i\). Fixing a slot as a root, and integrating all others absolutely, defines the rooted norm

\(\displaystyle s (T) = \text{max}_i \sup_{x_i} \int |T (x_1, \ldots, x_k) | \prod_{j \neq i} \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} x_j .\)

For a translation-invariant tensor define its first spatial moment by

\(\displaystyle v_1 (T) = \text{max}_{i, a < b} \sup_{x_i} \int d (x_a, x_b) |T (x_1, \ldots, x_k) | \prod_{j \neq i} \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} x_j .\)

All derivative tensors are symmetric in their slots. A local derivative is allowed to contain physical deltas: \(\delta_x (z) = N^3 1_{x = z}\) has integral one. This convention prevents hidden lattice-volume factors when slots coincide. On the field ball \(\mathcal{B}_q\), the set of \(y \in H_N\) with \(\|y\|_{\infty} < 2 r_q\), put

\(\displaystyle \textsf{S}_k (f) = \sup_{y \in \mathcal{B}_q} s (D^k f (y)), \quad \mathcal{N}_{k, q} (f) = \textsf{S}_k (f) + \sqrt{q} v_1 (D^k f (0)) .\)

Thus \(\textsf{S}_k\) controls the derivative everywhere in the field ball, whereas the spatial moment is required only at the zero field. The scalar bound \(\textsf{S}_k (f)\) is distinct from the posterior covariance \(S_q\) of (10.2). For a symmetric \(k\)-tensor \(T\), contraction of two slots against a kernel \(E\) means the physical integral

\(\displaystyle (\text{Ctr}_E T) (x_1, \ldots, x_{k - 2}) = \int E (u, v) T (x_1, \ldots, x_{k - 2}, u, v) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} u \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} v.\)

For tensor measures the same formula is interpreted after testing the remaining slots. Slots joined to inverse kernels in such a product are called operator slots; the remaining ones are external slots. The seed is the mass-subtracted logarithmic determinant, (11.6) of Lecture 6,

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{L}_q (y) = \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} [\log (I + C_q^{1 / 2} \lambda y^2 C_q^{1 / 2}) - C_q^{1 / 2} \lambda y^2 C_q^{1 / 2}] . \) (20.1)

The square root and logarithm are defined by diagonalising the positive finite-dimensional operators; in particular this is a finite matrix logarithm. The subtraction removes its quadratic field term. It is nonpositive with zero constant and quadratic jets, and

\(\displaystyle \partial_q \mathcal{L}_q (y) = \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} [G_q (y) - C_q] + \frac{\lambda}{2} a_2 (q) \|y\|_2^2 . \) (20.2)

For an even action define \((\Pi f) (y) = f (y) - f (0) - \frac{1}{2} D^2 f (0) [y, y]\); this removes the constant and quadratic Taylor coefficients, also called its low jets. For a quadratic path \(M_q = \mu_q I + \Theta_q\) (a scalar and an even, translation-invariant, centred kernel), whenever the displayed inverses exist, define the primitive of the trace map (11.7) of Lecture 6 (its field gradient is that map with \(\ell = Dh\))

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{T}_q [M, h] (y) = \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} [(K_q + M_q + D^2 (U + h) (y))^{- 1} - (K_q + M_q)^{- 1}] \) (20.3)

Its quadratic jet at zero is, by evenness, the operator \(D^2 \mathcal{T}_q [M, h] (0) =\mathfrak{S}_q [M, h]\) of (20.8) below. For a translation-invariant operator \(L\) write \(L = \langle L \rangle I + L^{\circ}\), with \(\langle L \rangle\) its constant-mode eigenvalue and \(L^{\circ}\) centred. Given a scalar path \(\mu\), the recursion has three nonlinear functions and two centred kernels, as in (11.8): with the level masses \(M^{[j]} = \mu I + \Theta^{[j]}\) and \(\Theta^{[1]} = 0\),

\(\displaystyle h^{[1]} =\mathcal{L}, \quad h_q^{[j + 1]} =\mathcal{L}_q - \int_q^{\infty} \Pi (\mathcal{T}_s [M^{[j]}, h^{[j]}] - \partial_s \mathcal{L}_s) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s, \quad \Theta^{[j + 1]}_q = - \int_q^{\infty} \mathfrak{S}_s [M^{[j]}, h^{[j]}]^{\circ} \mathrm{d} s, \quad j = 1, 2. \) (20.4)

Integrals are at fixed field, and the field balls increase with scale. The integrals are improper at infinite scale; their convergence is proved in Proposition 20.6. No derivative-losing infinite iteration is used.

20.2Two elementary receiving estimates

All kernels in this subsection are physical kernels, with normalised spatial measure. A closed cycle means the product of alternating vertex and inverse kernels, integrated over all joined operator slots. An external slot is left unintegrated until the rooted norm is taken. A marked factor is simply a selected factor that carries a difference estimate or a spatial-distance weight. Define

\(\displaystyle m (L) = \text{max} \left( \sup_x \int |L (x, u) | \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} u, \sup_u \int |L (x, u) | \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} x \right),\)
\(\displaystyle \mathfrak{l} (L) = \text{max} (\sup_x \|L (x, \cdot)\|_2, \sup_u \|L (\cdot, u)\|_2) .\)

Schur's inequality gives \(\|L\|_{p \to p} \leqslant m (L)\) for all \(1 \leqslant p \leqslant \infty\). Also \(\mathfrak{l} (LL'), \mathfrak{l} (L' L) \leqslant m (L') \mathfrak{l} (L)\): on one side use the \(L^2\) operator bound for \(L'\), and on the other use Minkowski and its absolute row or column mass. These statements do not require positive kernels.

Lemma 20.1. (A rooted closed cycle) This is (11.5) of Lecture 6 with its proof. Let \(T_1, \ldots, T_p\) be symmetric physical vertices, each with at least one external slot. Close their two operator slots into a cycle with \(p + 1\) symmetric inverse factors \(H\); two consecutive inverse factors may be combined into \(H^2\). Denote the resulting external tensor by \(\mathcal{Z}\). Then the absolute rooted norm of \(\mathcal{Z}\) is at most

\(\displaystyle \mathfrak{l} (H)^2 m (H)^{p - 1} \prod_{i = 1}^p s (T_i) . \) (20.5)

If all factors are translation invariant and every inverse factor has \(\mathcal{A}_q\) norm at most \(C / q\) and \(\mathcal{H}_q\) norm at most \(Cq^{- 1 / 4}\), then its first-moment version is

\(\displaystyle s (\mathcal{Z}) + \sqrt{q} v_1 (\mathcal{Z}) \leqslant C_p q^{- 1 / 2} q^{- (p - 1)} \prod_i \left( s (T_i) + \sqrt{q} v_1 (T_i) \right) . \) (20.6)

The same conclusions hold for mixed inverse factors with common bounds. A marked factor with both norms smaller by \(\rho\) gives the additional factor \(\rho\). For cycles with \(p\) inverse factors and \(p\) vertices, \(p \geqslant 2\), replace \(p - 1\) by \(p - 2\) in both bounds.

Proof. Choose the external root in \(T_1\). Integrate all external slots of the other vertices in absolute value. Their two-slot marginals \(\bar{T}_i\) have both Schur masses at most \(s (T_i)\), by symmetry. The open chain between the two operator slots of \(T_1\) starts and ends with an inverse. Its kernel is bounded by

\(\displaystyle \|H (u, \cdot)\|_2 \| \text{middle chain} \|_{2 \to 2} \|H (\cdot, v)\|_2 \leqslant \mathfrak{l} (H)^2 m (H)^{p - 1} \prod_{i > 1} s (T_i) .\)

Integrating the rooted \(T_1\) gives (20.5). Absolute kernels were used throughout; no cancellation or positivity of \(H\) was assumed.

For the spatial assertion, translation invariance identifies every rooted integral with its full spatial average. If the marked pair is in one vertex, charge its distance to that vertex's \(v_1\). Otherwise follow either arc of the closed cycle between the two marked external slots. The triangle inequality charges each piece to a within-vertex displacement or an inverse edge. A vertex charge gives a two-slot marginal with weighted row and column masses at most \(s (T_i) + \sqrt{q} v_1 (T_i)\). For edge charges the two arcs contain an inverse each. After the intervening vertices have been collapsed, write their convolution chains as \(E_1, E_2\). The closing integrals obey

\(\displaystyle \int \left( 1 + \sqrt{q} d \right) |E_1 (z) E_2 (- z) | \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} z \leqslant \|E_1 \|_{\mathcal{H}_q} \|E_2 \|_{\mathcal{H}_q} .\)

Use one inverse in each \(\mathcal{H}_q\) norm and every other inverse and collapsed vertex in \(\mathcal{A}_q\), using (19.3). There are \(p - 1\) remaining inverses. This gives (20.6); there are at most \(Cp\) distance charges. The identical proof treats marked factors and the version with one fewer inverse. The weighted estimate uses \(q^{- 1 / 2}\); the sharper unweighted factor \(\mathfrak{l} (H)^2 \leqslant Ca_2 (q)\) is retained separately when needed.\(\Box\)

Remark 20.2. (The vertex must pay for its own spatial extent) Take the nonlocal fourth vertex \(T (x_1, x_2, x_3, x_4) = 1\). Since \(C_q^2 1 = q^{- 2} 1\), its contraction is the constant two-slot kernel \(\text{Ctr}_{C_q^2} T = q^{- 2}\). Consequently

\(\displaystyle \frac{v_1 (\text{Ctr}_{C_q^2} T)}{s (\text{Ctr}_{C_q^2} T)} = \int_{\mathbb{T}^3} d (0, z) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} z > 0.\)

On a lattice replace the integral by its normalised sum. Thus closing two free inverses cannot by itself give a \(q^{- 1 / 2}\) spatial gain for an arbitrary nonlocal vertex. This is why (20.6) retains every within-vertex moment.

Lemma 20.3. (An inserted multiplication and a nonlocal vertex) For arbitrary kernels \(L, L'\), a symmetric third tensor \(T\), and a multiplication input \(\phi\), the two linear maps

\(\displaystyle \phi \mapsto \text{diag} (LM_{\phi} L'), \quad \phi \mapsto \text{Ctr}_{LM_{\phi} L'} T\)

have \(L^p\) operator norms at most \(\mathfrak{l} (L) \mathfrak{l} (L')\) and \(s (T) \mathfrak{l} (L) \mathfrak{l} (L')\), respectively, for every \(1 \leqslant p \leqslant \infty\). If a bounded covariance kernel \(E\) satisfies \(\|E\|_{\infty} : = \sup_{x, z} |E (x, z) | \leqslant c\), inserting a multiplication input in any one slot of \(\text{Ctr}_E T\) costs at most \(cs (T) \| \phi \|_p\).

Proof. For the diagonal map the positive majorant kernel is \(|L (x, a) L' (a, x) |\). Its row and column masses are bounded by \(\mathfrak{l} (L) \mathfrak{l} (L')\), using Cauchy–Schwarz in \(a\) and \(x\), respectively.

For the tensor contraction its majorant is

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{J} (x, a) = \int |T (x, u, v) | |L (u, a) L' (a, v) | \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} u \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} v.\)

For its row mass first integrate \(a\), obtaining at most \(\mathfrak{l} (L) \mathfrak{l} (L') s (T)\). For its column mass put \(\bar{T} (u, v) = \int |T (x, u, v) | \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} x\). Symmetry makes \(\| \bar{T} \|_{2 \to 2} \leqslant s (T)\), and therefore

\(\displaystyle \int \mathcal{J} (x, a) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} x \leqslant s (T) \|L (\cdot, a)\|_2 \|L' (a, \cdot)\|_2 .\)

Schur's inequality proves the assertion, including both endpoints \(p = 1, \infty\). For an input in a contracted slot, bound \(E\) by \(c\) and collapse the unused slot of \(T\); symmetry again supplies both Schur masses. An input in the open slot multiplies a function bounded by \(cs (T)\). Remark 20.5. (Small Hessians alone do not suffice) The higher individual bounds in this lemma are necessary (compare Exercise 11.3 of Lecture 6). On the one-site lattice take \(q = \lambda = 1\), \(M = 0\), and fixed small \(\alpha, \delta > 0\). Put

\(\displaystyle \hat{h}_n (y) = \frac{\alpha}{n^2} \left( \text{cos} (ny) - 1 + \frac{n^2 y^2}{2} \right), \quad h_n (y) = \hat{h}_n (y) + \frac{\delta}{12} y^4 .\)

\(\displaystyle D^4 (\mathcal{T}[h_n] -\mathcal{T}[\hat{h}_n]) (0) = 12 (2 \delta + \alpha \delta n^2 + \delta^2) .\)

Thus a trace Lipschitz estimate cannot follow from Hessian smallness and difference bounds alone. The displayed trace derivative is then multiplied by \(q^{- 1} r_q^{- 4}\) and still diverges with \(n\) at fixed \(q\). In our recursion the needed higher individual bounds follow at each finite step from the seed and the previous increment; they are not extra assumptions on the field.

21.1Clipping

Choose a smooth odd \(\tau\), equal to the identity on \([- 1, 1]\), constant outside \([- 2, 2]\), with \(0 \leqslant \tau' \leqslant 1\) and \(| \tau | < 2\). Define pointwise, as in (12.1),

Proof. We verify the clipping budgets (21.2). In this proof \(h = h_q^{[3]}\) is the unclipped correction and \(J = D \kappa_q (y)\) is the diagonal operator multiplying by \(\tau' (y / r_q)\). Proof. Expansion. Expand \(R_0 = C - CMC + CMCMR_0\) with \(C = C_q\). Three identities: \(\partial_q \text{Tr} C_q = - a_2\); \(\partial_q C_q (x) = - C_q^{(2)} (x)\), so \(\partial_q \int C_q^3 = - 3 \int C_q^2 C_q^{(2)}\); and \(\partial_q (\mu a_2) = \beta a_2 + \mu a_2'\), so \(- \lambda \mu a_2' + \lambda^2 a_2^2 = - \lambda \partial_q (\mu a_2) + \lambda (\beta + \lambda a_2) a_2\). At fixed \(N\), as \(q \to \infty\), \(\text{Tr} C_q\), \(\int C_q^3\) and \(\mu_q a_2 (q)\) tend to \(0\). Since \(\lambda^2 \cdot 2 \cdot \frac{1}{3} = \frac{2}{3} \lambda^2\), integrating \(\beta\) over \([q_{\ast}, \infty)\) gives \(\mu_N = b_{\ast} (1 + \lambda a_2 (q_{\ast})) - \lambda \text{Tr} C_{q_{\ast}} + \frac{2}{3} \lambda^2 \int C_{q_{\ast}}^3 + \int (\mathcal{R}+ \lambda (\beta + \lambda a_2) a_2)\), which is (22.1). The last integrand is \(O (\lambda^3 q^{- 3 / 2})\), because \(\beta + \lambda a_2 = O (\lambda^2 / q)\): indeed \(\mu a_2' = O (\lambda q^{1 / 2} q^{- 3 / 2})\).

Limit. The multiplier of \(\text{Tr} C - \text{Tr} C_{q_{\ast}}\) is \((q_{\ast} - q_0) ((q_0 + c_k) (q_{\ast} + c_k))^{- 1} \lesssim (1 + |k|)^{- 4}\), summable, with \(c_k \geqslant c |k|^2\), so this sum converges. Next \(C^3 - C_{q_{\ast}}^3 = (C - C_{q_{\ast}}) (C^2 + CC_{q_{\ast}} + C_{q_{\ast}}^2)\), where \(C - C_{q_{\ast}}\) converges uniformly, by the same summability, and the second factor converges in \(L^1\). At fixed \(q\), \(\beta_q^N\), \(\mu_q^N\) and the explicit terms converge by Proposition 22.1 and the convergence of the free kernels, hence so does \(\mathcal{R}_q^N\). The integrand is dominated by \(C \lambda^3 q^{- 3 / 2}\), so \(f_N \to f\) pointwise.

Inversion. By Proposition 20.8 and \(\lambda a_2 (q_{\ast}) \leqslant Cg_{q_{\ast}}\), \(f_N\) is increasing with \(|f_N (b_{\ast, 1}) - f_N (b_{\ast, 2}) - \delta b_{\ast} | \leqslant Cg_{q_{\ast}} | \delta b_{\ast} |\), and the same holds for \(f\). By (22.1), \(f_N (0) = \lambda (\text{Tr} C - \text{Tr} C_{q_{\ast}}) + O (\log q_{\ast})\), and \(\text{Tr} C - \text{Tr} C_{q_{\ast}} \leqslant C (q_0) + q_{\ast}^{1 / 2} / 10\) uniformly in \(N\) (the continuum value of the coefficient is \(\frac{1}{4 \pi} < \frac{1}{10}\), and the lattice sum is bounded by \(\frac{1}{10}\) uniformly in \(N\)). So for \(B_0\) above a universal constant and then \(q_{\ast}\) large depending on \(\lambda, q_0, B_0, r\), \(f_N (\mathcal{B})\) contains \(r\) for all \(N\). Monotonicity and pointwise convergence give \(b_{\ast, N} \to f^{- 1} (r)\).\(\Box\)

The measure depends only on \(\mu_N\), so the choice of \(q_{\ast}\) is immaterial for (22.2): we may take any large \(q_{\ast}\).

24The topology of the centred quadratic kernel

Let \(\mathcal{Q}_1\) be the space of even signed Radon measures on \(\mathbb{T}^3 \setminus 0\) with norm

\(\displaystyle v_1 (\Theta) = \int d (0, z) | \Theta | (\hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} z) < \infty .\)

Such a measure acts by the compensated formula

\(\displaystyle (\Theta f) (x) = \frac{1}{2} \int [f (x + z) + f (x - z) - 2 f (x)] \Theta (\hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} z) . \) (24.1)

For a lattice kernel the off-diagonal measure assigns mass \(N^{- 3} \Theta_N (0, z)\) to \(z \neq 0\); its diagonal is supplied by centring, \(\Theta_N 1 = 0\). There is no independent diagonal mass in \(\mathcal{Q}_1\): that coefficient belongs to \(\mu I\). The multiplier \(\hat{\Theta} (k)\) is characterised by \(\Theta e_k = \hat{\Theta} (k) e_k\).

Lemma 24.1. (Compensated kernels and weak paths) The space \(\mathcal{Q}_1\) is complete. The norm \(v_1\) is lower semicontinuous for vague convergence off zero. If \(\Theta_N \to \Theta\) vaguely off zero with bounded first moments, then for every smooth \(f\),

\(\displaystyle \iota_N \Theta_N j_N f \to \Theta f \quad \text{uniformly}, \quad | \hat{\Theta}_N (k) | \leqslant Cv_1 (\Theta_N) |k|,\)

and \(\hat{\Theta}_N (k) \to \hat{\Theta} (k)\) for each \(k\). The same conclusions are locally uniform in scale when convergence against compactly supported tests off zero is locally uniform in scale. Such a topology, with the scalar paths \(\mu^N\) converging locally uniformly, is called the weak path topology.

Proof. Multiplication by \(d (0, z)\) identifies \(\mathcal{Q}_1\) isometrically with the even finite signed measures on the punctured torus, a Banach space in total variation. On each closed annulus the weighted variation is the supremum over continuous tests dominated by \(d\). Taking increasing annuli gives lower semicontinuity.

For \(d (0, z) < \delta\) the second difference of a smooth function satisfies

\(\displaystyle |f (x + z) + f (x - z) - 2 f (x) | \leqslant C \|f\|_{C^2} d (0, z)^2 \leqslant C \delta \|f\|_{C^2} d (0, z) .\)

The small displacement contribution is therefore uniformly negligible, as the first moments are bounded. Away from \(d (0, z) < \delta\) use a continuous cutoff and the vague convergence; a finite net in \(x\) and the same bound for derivatives give uniformity in \(x\). Cell averages commute with translations by lattice displacements, so replacing \(f\) by its cell averages gives the same limit. There is no large distance tail on the torus.

For a Fourier character the multiplier is \(\int (\text{cos} (2 \pi k \cdot z) - 1) \Theta (\hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} z)\), and \(| \text{cos} (2 \pi k \cdot z) - 1| \leqslant C |k| d (0, z)\) proves the bound. This also justifies a Fourier approximation of a smooth \(f\), uniformly over the moment bounded family. The function \(\text{cos} (2 \pi k \cdot z) - 1\) vanishes to second order at \(z = 0\), so the first part with \(f = e_k\) gives the convergence of the multipliers. The scale uniform claims follow by the identical estimates. Bounded first moments prevent a hidden second order differential operator from surviving at zero.\(\Box\)

The strong metric \(d_{\ast}\) of (23.1), restricted to scalar paths, is the one used for the fixed point; on the centred kernels it only records their bounds. It is different from the weak path topology: lattice measures need not converge in total variation to their continuum limits. Both lattice and continuum paths are defined on \([q_{\ast}, \infty)\), so the same metric is used for all their contraction estimates. Weak convergence is still only local in scale.

25Physical vertex measures and their derivatives

If \(D^k f_N (y)\) has physical tensor kernel \(T_N\), associate the atomic measure

\(\displaystyle \mathfrak{v}_N = N^{- 3 k} \sum_{x_1, \ldots, x_k \in \Lambda_N} T_N (x_1, \ldots, x_k) \delta_{(x_1, \ldots, x_k)} .\)

Integrating smooth products against it evaluates the derivative on their lattice samples. Evaluation on cell averages differs by \(o (1)\), since samples and averages of a fixed smooth function differ uniformly by \(O (N^{- 1})\) and the tensor variation is bounded. We use atomic measures for weak convergence and cell kernels for operator comparisons. As in Section 20.1, \(s (T)\) is the rooted norm.

Lemma 25.1. (Root bounds pass to limits) A family with \(s (T_N) \leqslant C\) has weakly convergent subsequences as signed measures on the compact product torus. Its limiting variation has every one-slot marginal bounded by \(C \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} x\). Spatial first moment bounds also pass to the limit. In particular a two-slot limit defines a bounded operator on \(H\) of norm at most \(C\).

Proof. The total variation is at most \(C\), since the root variable has volume one. Weak compactness of bounded measures gives a subsequence. For a continuous nonnegative root test \(a\) and a continuous product space test \(\psi\) with \(| \psi | \leqslant 1\), the rooted estimate gives

\(\displaystyle \left| \int a (x_i) \psi \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} \mathfrak{v}_N \right| \leqslant CN^{- 3} \sum_{x \in \Lambda_N} a (x) .\)

Pass to the limit and take the supremum over \(\psi\) to obtain the marginal variation bound. Insert a distance in the tests for the moment version. A symmetric two-slot measure \(\textsf{T}\) therefore obeys

\(\displaystyle \left| \int u (x) v (z) \textsf{T} (\hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} x, \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} z) \right| \leqslant (\int |u (x) |^2 | \textsf{T} |)^{1 / 2} (\int |v (z) |^2 | \textsf{T} |)^{1 / 2} \leqslant C \|u\|_2 \|v\|_2 .\)

This defines its bounded operator, first on smooth functions, then by density on \(H\).\(\Box\)

Lemma 25.2. (Recovering derivatives by finite differences) Suppose scalar actions \(f_N (j_N \phi)\) converge for smooth profiles in an open bounded field domain, and their derivatives through order \(m + 1\) have uniform rooted bounds there. Then, on smaller compact finite-dimensional families of smooth profiles, all tested derivatives through order \(m\) converge. Their physical tensor measures converge weakly to the corresponding derivative measures.

Proof. Restrict to \(\phi + \sum_{i = 1}^d t_i u_i\), with smooth directions \(u_i\) and parameters a positive distance inside the domain. A mixed finite difference of order \(k \leqslant m\) and step \(\delta\) approximates the corresponding derivative with error \(C \delta\), by the bound on the derivative of order \(k + 1\). At fixed \(\delta\) its finitely many scalar values converge. Let \(N \to \infty\), then \(\delta \to 0\). The next derivative bound and finite parameter nets give uniformity on compact parameter sets.

Products of smooth one-variable tests determine a measure on the product torus: finite Fourier sums approximate every continuous product space test uniformly, and the common variation bound removes that approximation. This identifies the full weak vertex limit, not just a selected collection of tensor contractions. The limiting scalar action on these families has the stated derivatives, since the fundamental theorem of calculus passes to the limit on segments.\(\Box\)

For even corrections with zero quadratic jet, Taylor's formula also gives

\(\displaystyle D^2 h (\phi) = \int_0^1 (1 - t) D^4 h (t \phi) [\phi, \phi] \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} t. \) (25.1)

Thus the Hessian Schur bounds follow from the bounds on the fourth derivative on every bounded field set. Local diagonal terms cause no difficulty: they are diagonal measures, not ordinary functions of two independent variables.

26Compact resolvents receive weak Hessians

The four lemmas of this section (compact sandwich, and contraction against a moving covariance) are the second and third tools listed in Section 18.1 of Lecture 7.

Lemma 26.1. (Free and quadratic background consistency) For paths converging in the weak path topology,

\(\displaystyle \iota_N (K_q + M^N_q)^{- 1} j_N \to (q - \Delta + \mu_q + \Theta_q)^{- 1} \quad \text{in } \text{HS},\)

locally uniformly in \(q\). The same holds after adding \(\lambda (j_N \phi)^2\) for a fixed smooth bounded profile \(\phi\). In particular \(\iota_N C^N_q j_N \to C_q\) in \(\text{HS}\).

Proof. For each fixed Fourier mode the multipliers converge, by Lemma 24.1 and the convergence of the lattice Laplacian symbol. Their absolute values are bounded by \(C (q + c|k|^2)^{- 1}\), by the small relative form bound: \(| \mu_q | \leqslant Rg_q q\) and \(| \hat{\Theta}_q (k) | \leqslant CRg_q \lambda |k| \leqslant CRg_q (g_q^2 q + |k|^2)\). The squared tail beyond \(|k| = L\) is therefore at most \(C_I \sum_{|k| > L} (1 + |k|^2)^{- 2} \leqslant C_I / L\). The embedded lattice Fourier characters converge uniformly to their continuum characters on each fixed finite set of modes. The low and high frequency split proves Hilbert–Schmidt convergence.

For the multiplication potential put \(V_N = \lambda (\iota_N j_N \phi)^2\) and \(V = \lambda \phi^2\), so \(\|V_N - V\|_{\infty} \to 0\). If \(A_N, A\) denote the preceding background inverses and \(G_N, G\) the inverses with \(V_N, V\), the exact identity is

\(\displaystyle G_N - G = (I - G_N V_N) (A_N - A) (I - VG) + G_N (V - V_N) G.\)

All outer factors have bounded operator norms on the fixed profile, and the middle difference is Hilbert–Schmidt. The identity follows by expanding and using \(G_N = (I - G_N V_N) A_N\) and \(G = A (I - VG)\). This proves the assertion without an expansion in the field amplitude.\(\Box\)

Lemma 26.2. (Compact sandwich) Let \(G_N \to G\) in Hilbert–Schmidt norm. Let \(\textsf{B}_N\) be a uniformly bounded symmetric sequence converging weakly as operators to \(\textsf{B}\), meaning \(\langle f, \textsf{B}_N g \rangle \to \langle f, \textsf{B} g \rangle\) for all \(f, g \in H\). Assume

\(\displaystyle \text{max} (\|G_N \textsf{B}_N \|_{2 \to 2}, \|G \textsf{B} \|_{2 \to 2}) \leqslant \theta < 1.\)

Then

\(\displaystyle R_N = (I + G_N \textsf{B}_N)^{- 1} G_N \to R = (I + G \textsf{B})^{- 1} G \quad \text{in } \text{HS} .\)

Weak convergence of physical Hessian measures with uniform Schur bounds supplies the weak operator hypothesis.

Proof. A uniformly bounded weakly convergent sequence \(E_N \to 0\) satisfies \(AE_N D \to 0\) in Hilbert–Schmidt norm whenever \(A, D\) are Hilbert–Schmidt. Indeed, approximate both outer factors by finite rank operators: the finite matrix elements converge, and the two errors are bounded using \(\|XYZ\|_{\text{HS}} \leqslant \|X\|_{\text{HS}} \|Y\| \|Z\|\). Apply this to \(G (\textsf{B}_N - \textsf{B}) R\). Also \((G_N - G) (\textsf{B}_N - \textsf{B}) R \to 0\) in Hilbert–Schmidt norm. Now use

\(\displaystyle R_N - R = (I - R_N \textsf{B}_N) (G_N - G) (I - \textsf{B} R) + R_N (\textsf{B} - \textsf{B}_N) R,\)

where \(R_N = (I + G_N \textsf{B}_N)^{- 1} G_N\) in the last term. The inverse factors are uniformly bounded by \((1 - \theta)^{- 1}\), so both terms vanish. Weak measure convergence gives weak operator convergence on smooth product tests, and the Schur bound extends it to arbitrary \(L^2\) tests by density.\(\Box\)

In the applications \(G_N\) includes the quadratic background and the nonnegative local potential. Here \(\phi_N = j_N \phi\) for a fixed smooth bounded profile \(\phi\), \(h_N\) is the nonlinear correction at whichever finite depth is being considered, and \(\textsf{B}_N = D^2 h_N (\phi_N)\). The finite hierarchy gives \(\| \textsf{B}_N \| \leqslant Cg_q q\), and \(\|G_N \| \leqslant C / q\), so the same large \(q_{\ast}\) ensures the smallness required in the lemma. The limit inherits these bounds and defines a positive inverse before the convergence argument is used.

Lemma 26.3. (A continuous covariance kernel) On bounded field sets and compact scale intervals, for every \(0 < a < \frac{1}{2}\), the rows of the lattice inverses \(R_N\) above satisfy

\(\displaystyle \|R_N (x, \cdot) - R_N (x', \cdot)\|_2 \leqslant C_{I, a} d (x, x')^a . \) (26.1)

Their squares have bounded kernels with the corresponding modulus in both variables. If \(R_N \to R\) in Hilbert–Schmidt norm, the squared physical kernels converge uniformly, under cell embedding, to a continuous kernel \(R^2 (x, z)\).

Proof. For \(R_{0, N} = (K_q + M_q)^{- 1}\), Parseval and the multiplier bound give

\(\displaystyle \|R_{0, N} (x, \cdot) - R_{0, N} (x', \cdot)\|_2^2 \leqslant Cd (x, x')^{2 a} \sum_k \frac{|k|^{2 a}}{(q + c|k|^2)^2} \leqslant C_{I, a} d (x, x')^{2 a} .\)

The sum converges because \(a < \frac{1}{2}\). At a bounded field, write \(R_N = R_{0, N} - R_{0, N} (\lambda \phi_N^2 + \textsf{B}_N) R_N\); the factor to the right of \(R_{0, N}\) is bounded on \(L^2\). Taking row differences proves (26.1). Since \(R_N^2 (x, z)\) is the inner product of the two inverse rows, their common \(L^2\) bound proves the covariance modulus.

For cell embeddings that modulus has an additional error \(CN^{- a}\), as each point is replaced by its cell centre. The kernels are precompact in the uniform norm up to this vanishing error, for instance by interpolating their grid values continuously. On the other hand Hilbert–Schmidt convergence implies \(R_N^2 \to R^2\) in Hilbert–Schmidt norm, by the bounded operator norms. Thus every uniform subsequential limit equals the same \(L^2\) kernel. This proves uniform convergence and continuity.\(\Box\)

Lemma 26.4. (Contraction against a moving covariance) Let physical vertex measures \(T_N\) converge weakly with bounded variation, and let \(R_N\) be as in the preceding lemma. Any contraction of two slots of \(T_N\) against \(R_N^2\) converges weakly in the remaining slots. In particular the scalar trace converges when \(T_N\) is a Hessian.

Proof. After testing the remaining slots by a continuous function, split the difference as

\(\displaystyle \int (R_N^2 - R^2) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} T_N + \int R^2 \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} (T_N - T) .\)

The first term tends to zero by uniform convergence and bounded variation; the second by weak convergence against the fixed continuous kernel. The use of atomic vertices and cell covariances is consistent: the former evaluate the latter at their grid values, and the uniform cell error tends to zero. For a Hessian the trace identity follows first for finite rank approximations of the smoothing inverses, then by the same kernel bound. Equivalently, the Schur factorisation of its symmetric measure as \(M_{\sqrt{a}} \textsf{E} M_{\sqrt{a}}\), \(\| \textsf{E} \| \leqslant 1\), makes the double sandwich trace class; here \(a (x) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} x\) is its variation marginal. Thus no trace of a single, unsquared divergent inverse is taken.\(\Box\)

27Convergence of the seed and the two trace steps

Lemma 27.1. (The seed has a cutoff independent limit) The scalar seed actions converge on smooth bounded profiles, locally uniformly in scale and on finite-dimensional profile families. Their tested derivatives through order nine converge. Their physical derivative measures converge weakly at these profiles.

Proof. Subtract before taking a diagonal. The exact force identity is

\(\displaystyle D\mathcal{L}_q (\phi) (x) = - \lambda^2 \phi (x) \int C_q (x, z) \phi (z)^2 G_q (\phi) (z, x) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} z. \) (27.1)

It follows from \(D\mathcal{L}= \lambda \phi \text{diag} (G - C)\) and \(G - C = - C \lambda \phi^2 G\). Both inverses converge in Hilbert–Schmidt norm by Lemma 26.1. Cauchy–Schwarz in their two kernel variables proves \(L^1\) convergence of this force. Its uniform bound \(C_I \| \phi \|_{\infty}^3\) upgrades convergence to every finite spatial \(L^p\). Integrate \(\langle D\mathcal{L}_q (t \phi), \phi \rangle\) over \(t \in [0, 1]\) to identify the scalar action, with value zero at the origin. The ten derivative bounds of Proposition 11.2 and Lemma 25.2 give the nine tested derivatives. All estimates are uniform on compact profile families and scale intervals. In particular neither \(\text{diag} C_q\) nor \(\text{diag} G_q\) is assigned a separate continuum limit.\(\Box\)

Proposition 27.2. (Consistency of the finite nonlinear recursion) For prescribed level masses converging in the weak path topology, the functions \(h^{[j], N}\) of (11.8) converge on smooth profiles inside their field balls. Tested derivatives converge through orders \(9, 7, 5\) at depths \(j = 1, 2, 3\), respectively. All convergences are uniform on compact scale and admissible parameter sets. Corresponding inverse operators converge in Hilbert–Schmidt norm, including all interpolated inverses used below. This is the passage of the trace steps to the limit announced in Section 18.1 of Lecture 7.

Proof. The assertion at depth one is Lemma 27.1. Suppose scalar and tested convergence hold at depth \(j\). The Hessians converge weakly with uniform Schur bounds. Lemmas 26.1 and 26.2 identify the inverses with Hessian \(\theta D^2 h^{[j]}\), \(0 \leqslant \theta \leqslant 1\). The convergence is uniform in \(\theta\), since the resolvent identity gives a common Lipschitz bound in that parameter.

Let \(G_M = (K_q + M_q + \lambda \phi^2)^{- 1}\) and \(R_{\theta h} = (K_q + M_q + \lambda \phi^2 + \theta D^2 h^{[j]} (\phi))^{- 1}\). The scalar trace map decomposes exactly as

\(\displaystyle \text{Tr} (G_M - R_{0, q}) = - \text{Tr} (R_{0, q} \lambda \phi^2 G_M), \quad \text{Tr} (R_h - G_M) = - \int_0^1 \text{Ctr}_{R_{\theta h}^2} D^2 h^{[j]} (\phi) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} \theta . \) (27.2)

The first term converges as a product of Hilbert–Schmidt operators. The second converges by Lemma 26.4. Thus the scalar trace map converges at every admissible smooth profile. The finite derivative bounds and finite differences also identify its quadratic jet at zero, so its projection by \(\Pi\) converges.

It remains to pass the improper scale integral in (11.8). A fixed profile admissible at scale \(q\) remains admissible at all later scales. The initial trace comparison (Lemma 20.4), the common coefficient ball, and Taylor's formula to fourth order give

\(\displaystyle | \Pi (\mathcal{T}_s [M^{[j]}, h^{[j]}] - \partial_s \mathcal{L}_s) (\phi) | \leqslant C \lambda^3 s^{- 2} \| \phi \|_4^4 . \) (27.3)

Consequently the omitted tail beyond \(L\) is at most \(C \lambda^3 L^{- 1} \| \phi \|_4^4\), uniformly in the cutoffs. Pass to the limit first on \([q, L]\), then send \(L \to \infty\). This proves scalar convergence at depth \(j + 1\). Its derivative budget is eight at depth two and six at depth three, so finite differences identify derivatives through seven and five. These orders include the fourth zero-field vertex needed by the mass equation. There is one genuinely unused derivative at the final step; it cannot be omitted.

The identities \(\partial_q h^{[j + 1]} = \Pi \mathcal{T}_q [M^{[j]}, h^{[j]}]\) and the corresponding differentiated estimates give local scale equicontinuity. 28The scalar fixed point converges

The continuum source is a measure valued kernel. Weak convergence does not supply an integral in the total variation norm. The following construction is sufficient and avoids that assumption.

Lemma 28.1. (Integrating weak measure sources) Let \(\mathfrak{C}_s \in \mathcal{Q}_1\) be even measures off zero, measurable when tested against every compactly supported continuous function off zero, and suppose \(v_1 (\mathfrak{C}_s) \leqslant f (s)\) with \(f \in L^1 ([q_{\ast}, \infty))\). Then the weak prescription

\(\displaystyle \Theta_q = - \int_q^{\infty} \mathfrak{C}_s \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s \) (28.1)

defines a unique element of \(\mathcal{Q}_1\), with

\(\displaystyle v_1 (\Theta_q) \leqslant \int_q^{\infty} f (s) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s, \quad v_1 (\Theta_t - \Theta_q) \leqslant \int_q^t f (s) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s \quad (t \geqslant q) .\)

In particular the path is continuous in its first moment norm. If \(\mathfrak{C}^N_s \to \mathfrak{C}_s\) locally uniformly in scale against annular tests, with the same integrable bound, then \(\Theta^N \to \Theta\) in the weak path topology.

Suppose in addition that \(\mathfrak{S}_s\) are even finite signed measures on the whole torus, weakly continuous in scale and locally bounded in total variation, that \(\mathfrak{C}_s\) are their restrictions off zero, with the centring supplied as in (24.1), so that \(\mathfrak{S}_s^{\circ}\) is the centred convolution they define, and let \(\beta_s\) be any continuous scalar function. If \(\mu_q = b_{\ast} + \int_{q_{\ast}}^q \beta_s \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s\) and \(M_q = \mu_q I + \Theta_q\), then for smooth \(u\),

\(\displaystyle (M_t - M_q) u = \int_q^t (\beta_s u +\mathfrak{S}_s^{\circ} u) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s \quad \text{in } H, \quad \frac{\hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d}}{\hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} q} (M_q u) = \beta_q u +\mathfrak{S}_q^{\circ} u \quad \text{in } H. \) (28.2)

The scalar \(\beta\) need not be the constant mode of \(\mathfrak{S}\); in the scheme of (20.4) it comes from the final level and \(\mathfrak{S}\) from the previous one. Here \(\mathfrak{S}_s^{\circ} u\) means convolution. This does not assert differentiability of the measure path in total variation.

Proof. Write \(d (z) = d (0, z)\). On the punctured torus the functional

\(\displaystyle \psi \mapsto - \int_q^{\infty} \int d (z) \psi (z) \mathfrak{C}_s (\hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} z) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s\)

is bounded on \(C_0 (\mathbb{T}^3 \setminus 0)\) with the uniform norm: the space of continuous \(\psi\) such that the set where \(| \psi | \geqslant \varepsilon\) is compact in the punctured torus for every \(\varepsilon > 0\). The norm of the functional is at most \(\int_q^{\infty} f\). The inner integrals are measurable: first take compactly supported tests and then approximate in the uniform norm, using the bound on \(d |\mathfrak{C}_s |\). The Riesz representation theorem gives a finite signed measure \(\sigma_q\) for this functional. Define \(\Theta_q = d^{- 1} \sigma_q\) off zero. It is a locally finite signed Radon measure with \(\int d | \Theta_q | \leqslant \int_q^{\infty} f\) and the required test integrals. Uniqueness follows from compactly supported tests. The identical argument on \([q, t]\) proves the difference bound and continuity. For cutoff convergence, first integrate on \([q, L]\) and then bound the common tail by \(\int_L^{\infty} f\) times the weighted test norm. This is uniform for \(q\) in a compact interval. Evenness passes through the test prescription.

For the final assertion, with \(\delta_z^2 u = u (\cdot + z) + u (\cdot - z) - 2 u\), centring and evenness give on smooth \(u\)

\(\displaystyle \frac{1}{2} \int \delta_z^2 u\mathfrak{C}_s (\hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} z) =\mathfrak{S}_s u - \langle \mathfrak{S}_s \rangle u =\mathfrak{S}_s^{\circ} u,\)

where \(\langle \mathfrak{S}_s \rangle =\mathfrak{S}_s (\mathbb{T}^3)\); the scalar \(\beta_s\) enters only the scalar part. This integral is legitimate even for an infinite first moment measure: \(\| \delta_z^2 u\|_2 \leqslant C_u d (z)^2\). The preceding weak integral and Fubini, with the integrable first moment bound, give the first identity in (28.2), initially against smooth tests. The constant \(b_{\ast}\) cancels in the difference. To justify the strong \(H\) form, convolution by \(\mathfrak{S}_s\) has norm at most \(\|\mathfrak{S}_s \|_{\text{TV}}\). For a finite Fourier sum, \(\mathfrak{S}_s u\) is continuous in \(H\) by weak continuity of \(\mathfrak{S}_s\). Approximation by Fourier sums and the locally uniform total variation bound give this continuity for every \(u \in H\). Thus the right side is the integral of a continuous \(H\)-valued function, and its fundamental theorem of calculus proves the derivative assertion. On each compact scale interval \(M_t - M_q\) consequently extends to a bounded operator on \(H\), even when the individual \(M_q\) are only defined on a dense domain. No Banach space differentiability theorem for measures is used.\(\Box\)

Remark 28.2. (Norm continuity does not imply a measure derivative) Let \(e_1 = (1, 0, 0)\) and, for \(0 < a < t < c < \frac{1}{4}\), set \(\mathfrak{C}_t = \frac{1}{2} (\delta_{te_1} + \delta_{- te_1})\) off zero. On a Fourier mode the centred multiplier is instead \(\text{cos} (2 \pi k_1 t) - 1\), and differentiating the integrated multiplier is valid. This illustrates exactly the distinction in the lemma.

29Clipping and the complete drift

We keep all quadratic, covariance and scale-clipping terms. The exact gradient of the defect for \(q \geqslant q_{\ast}\), computed in Lemma 21.2, is

\(\displaystyle De_q (y) = M_q' y + D \partial_q \tilde{h}_q (y) + D \Upsilon_q (y), \quad D \Upsilon_q (y) = \lambda y \text{diag} H_q (y)^2 + \frac{1}{2} \text{Ctr}_{H_q (y)^2} D^3 \tilde{h}_q (y), \) (29.1)

with \(D \Upsilon_q = D \left( - \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} [H_q - H_q (0)] \right)\) the trace part. Write \(\Upsilon = \Upsilon_{q_{\ast}}\). Following Section 12.4, for \(0 < q < q_{\ast}\) the coefficients are frozen:

\(\displaystyle H_q = H_{q_{\ast}}, \quad De_q (y) = - y + D \Upsilon (y), \quad b_q (y) = H_{q_{\ast}} (y) (y - D \Upsilon (y)) . \) (29.2)

The term \(D \partial_q \tilde{h}_q\) and the mass derivative do not appear below \(q_{\ast}\), which is why \(b_q\) jumps at \(q_{\ast}\).

Since \(\| \iota_N z_N - \iota_N \phi_N \|_2 \leqslant \| \iota_N z_N - z\|_2 + \eta + \| \phi - \textsf{P}_N \phi \|_2\), the three term split

\(\displaystyle H^N_q (z_N) - H_q (z) = [H^N_q (z_N) - H^N_q (\phi_N)] + [H^N_q (\phi_N) - H_q (\phi)] + [H_q (\phi) - H_q (z)]\)

(with embeddings omitted) and Proposition 29.1 give the claim as \(N \to \infty\) and then \(\eta \to 0\). The same split, after testing with \(f \in H\), gives the weak convergence of \(De\) and \(D \Upsilon\), and without testing that of \(b\) and \(\varrho\).

The form of \(\varrho_q\) follows by the Lipschitz bound \(\|D \tilde{h}^N_q (y) - D \tilde{h}^N_q (z)\|_2 \leqslant s (D^2 \tilde{h}_q) \|y - z\|_2 \leqslant Cg_q q \|y - z\|_2\) and (12.2), passed to the weak limit, together with the fact that the multiplier \((q + 4 \pi^2 |k|^2)^{- 1} (\mu_q + \hat{\Theta}_q (k))\) is bounded.

For the measurability, on smooth \(\phi\) the maps \(q \mapsto H_q (\phi), b_q (\phi), \varrho_q (\phi)\) are limits of continuous functions of \(q\) (the second piecewise), so are Borel. For explicit jointly measurable versions, choose in each supremum ball a countable dense collection of smooth profiles and select the first profile within \(H\) distance \(2^{- n}\) of the input. This is a Borel selection. The continuity estimates identify the limit of the resulting countably valued maps. Supremum balls are closed in \(H\), so their increasing union gives Borel coefficient maps on bounded fields. Fixed values may be assigned outside this union, which does not affect the actual integrals. For \(q < q_{\ast}\) everything reduces to scale \(q_{\ast}\), and \(y \mapsto y\) is continuous.\(\Box\)

30Random inputs

The conclusion below is the statement of Section 18.1 of Lecture 7, used in the proofs of Theorems 18.4 and 18.6. We work on one probability space. All fields \(Y^N\) are measurable functions on \(\Omega \times I\) with values in \(H_N\), and \(Y\) is a random element of \(L^2 (I \times \mathbb{T}^3)\); \(I\) is a compact interval in \((0, \infty)\), and the coefficients for \(q < q_{\ast}\) are the frozen ones (29.2). If \(I\) contains \(q_{\ast}\) in its interior, split it there.

Theorem 30.1. (Coefficient identification along random fields) Suppose that \(\iota_N Y^N \to Y\) in \(L^2 (I \times \mathbb{T}^3)\) in probability and that \((\sup_{q \in I} \|Y^N_q \|_{\infty})_N\) is tight. Then \(Y\) is almost surely bounded on \(I \times \mathbb{T}^3\) and, in probability,

\(\displaystyle \iota_N H^N_q (Y^N_q) j_N \to H_q (Y_q) \quad \text{in } L^2 (I ; \text{HS}), \quad \iota_N b^N_q (Y^N_q) \to b_q (Y_q) \quad \text{in } L^2 (I ; H), \) (30.1)

and, for \(I \subset [q_{\ast}, \infty)\), \(\iota_N \varrho^N_q (Y^N_q) \to \varrho_q (Y_q)\) in \(L^2 (I ; H)\). In particular this holds if, for almost every \(q \in I\), \(\iota_N Y^N_q \to Y_q\) uniformly on \(\mathbb{T}^3\) in probability, with \(\sup_I \|Y^N \|_{\infty}\) bounded in probability.

For \(q < q_{\ast}\) the limits are \(H_q = H_{q_{\ast}}\) and \(b_q (y) = H_{q_{\ast}} (y) (y - D \Upsilon (y))\), with \(\Upsilon\) the limit of \(- \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} [H^N_{q_{\ast}} (y) - H^N_{q_{\ast}} (0)]\), given by (30.3) below.

Proof. Bounded fields. Let \(L < \infty\). Consider nonrandom fields with \(\|z\|_{\infty} \leqslant L\) on \(I \times \mathbb{T}^3\). Integrating (29.3)–(29.5) over \(I\), on this set \(\|y^2 - z^2 \|_2 \leqslant 2 L \|y - z\|_2\) gives

\(\displaystyle \|H^N (y) - H^N (z)\|_{L^2 (I ; \text{HS})} + \|b^N (y) - b^N (z)\|_{L^2 (I ; H)} \leqslant C_{I, L} \|y - z\|_{L^2 (I ; H_N)}, \) (30.2)

and the same for the continuum maps of Proposition 29.3: uniform continuity in strong space–time \(L^2\). Smooth space–scale profiles bounded by \(L\) are dense in this set: convolve in space and scale, using a bounded extension at the scale endpoints. Cell averages preserve the supremum bound and converge strongly.

Reduction to a net. Fix \(\varepsilon > 0\). Choose \(L\) so that the probability that \(\sup_I \|Y^N \|_{\infty} > L\) is below \(\varepsilon\) for all \(N\). The closed ball \(S_L\) of radius \(L\) of \(L^{\infty} (I \times \mathbb{T}^3)\) is closed in \(L^2 (I \times \mathbb{T}^3)\), so the convergence in probability gives \(\mathbb{P} (Y \notin S_L) \leqslant \text{liminf} \mathbb{P} (\iota_N Y^N \notin S_L) \leqslant \varepsilon\). As \(\varepsilon\) is arbitrary, \(Y\) is almost surely bounded. The law of the single random element \(Y\) of the Polish space \(L^2 (I \times \mathbb{T}^3)\) is tight, so there is a compact \(\mathcal{S} \subset S_L\) with \(\mathbb{P} (Y \notin \mathcal{S}) \leqslant 2 \varepsilon\).

Given \(\eta > 0\), choose finitely many smooth space–scale profiles \(\phi^1, \ldots, \phi^m\), bounded by \(L\), so that every point of \(\mathcal{S}\) is within \(\eta\) of one of them in \(L^2 (I \times \mathbb{T}^3)\). On the event that \(Y \in \mathcal{S}\), that \(\sup_I \|Y^N \|_{\infty} \leqslant L\) and that \(\| \iota_N Y^N - Y\|_{L^2} \leqslant \eta\), whose probability is at least \(1 - 4 \varepsilon\) for large \(N\), pick \(i\) with \(\|Y - \phi^i \|_{L^2} \leqslant \eta\). Then

\(\displaystyle \|H^N (Y^N) - H (Y)\| \leqslant \|H^N (Y^N) - H^N (\phi^i_N)\| + \text{max}_{j \leqslant m} \|H^N (\phi^j_N) - H (\phi^j)\| + \|H (\phi^i) - H (Y)\|,\)

in \(L^2 (I ; \text{HS})\), with embeddings omitted. The first term is at most \(C_{I, L} (2 \eta + \text{max}_j \| \phi^j - \textsf{P}_N \phi^j \|_{L^2})\) by (30.2), the last at most \(C_{I, L} \eta\), and the middle term tends to zero by Proposition 29.1, deterministically. So the probability that the left side exceeds \(4 C_{I, L} \eta\) is at most \(5 \varepsilon\) for large \(N\). Letting \(\eta \to 0\) and then \(\varepsilon \to 0\) proves (30.1) for \(H\). The same argument applies to \(b\) and \(\varrho\). The limits are the maps of Proposition 29.3, evaluated at \(Y_q\) for almost every \(q\); these are measurable by the last assertion of that proposition.

The uniform hypothesis. Assume now that for almost every \(q \in I\), \(\| \iota_N Y^N_q - Y_q \|_{\infty} \to 0\) in probability, with \(\sup_I \|Y^N \|_{\infty}\) tight. By Fubini and dominated convergence, \(\mathbb{E} \int_I \text{min} (1, \| \iota_N Y^N_q - Y_q \|_{\infty}) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} q \to 0\). Along a subsequence, almost surely, \(\iota_N Y^N_q \to Y_q\) uniformly for almost every \(q\). Let \(A_N\) be the event that \(\sup_I \|Y^N \|_{\infty} \leqslant L\), with \(\mathbb{P} (A_N) \geqslant 1 - \varepsilon\). The event that \(A_N\) occurs infinitely often along the subsequence has probability at least \(1 - \varepsilon\), and on it \(\|Y_q \|_{\infty} \leqslant L\) for almost every \(q\). So \(\mathbb{P} (\|Y\|_{\infty} \leqslant L) \geqslant 1 - \varepsilon\), and on \(A_N\) intersected with this event,

\(\displaystyle \int_I \| \iota_N Y^N_q - Y_q \|_2^2 \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} q \leqslant (1 + 2 L)^2 \int_I \text{min} (1, \| \iota_N Y^N_q - Y_q \|_{\infty})^2 \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} q,\)

whose expectation tends to zero. So \(\iota_N Y^N \to Y\) in \(L^2 (I \times \mathbb{T}^3)\) in probability, and the preceding part applies.

The frozen coefficients. The continuity and the consistency used above were established for the frozen drift in Lemma 29.2 and Proposition 29.1. Since \(\Upsilon^N (0) = 0\),

\(\displaystyle \Upsilon^N (y) = - \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} [H^N_{q_{\ast}} (y) - H^N_{q_{\ast}} (0)] = \int_0^1 \langle D \Upsilon^N (ty), y \rangle \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} t, \quad \Upsilon (y) = \int_0^1 \langle D \Upsilon (ty), y \rangle \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} t, \) (30.3)

and this limit, for fields converging as in Proposition 29.3, is proved in Theorem 31.2 below. \(D \Upsilon\) is the trace part in the gradient (29.1) of \(e_{q_{\ast}}\), as in (12.8).\(\Box\)

Remark 30.2. (Which hypotheses are used) The theorem needs less than Section 18.1 of Lecture 7 assumes: uniform convergence in space is not used, only strong \(L^2 (I \times \mathbb{T}^3)\) convergence, and the sup bound need only be tight, with no moments. The estimate (29.5) requires no bound at all, and (29.3) requires only the \(L^4\) convergence of the field, but we do not use this. Moments of the sup norm such as those of Lemma 17.1 are one way of obtaining the tightness.

31The coordinate, high modes and stochastic integrals

We now prove Lemma 18.3 and the statement on stochastic integrals in Section 18.1 of Lecture 7. Let \(\tau_a\) denote the lattice translation by \(a \in \Lambda_N\).

Lemma 31.1. (Discrete compactness) Let \(u_N \in H_N\) be bounded in \(L^{\infty}\) with \(\text{limsup}_N \sup_{|a| \leqslant \delta} \| \tau_a u_N - u_N \|_{\infty} \leqslant \varpi (\delta)\), where \(\varpi (\delta) \to 0\) as \(\delta \to 0\). Then every subsequence has a further subsequence along which \(\iota_N u_N\) converges uniformly to a continuous function. If moreover \(\iota_N u_N \to u\) in \(H\), then \(u\) is continuous and \(\iota_N u_N \to u\) uniformly along the whole sequence.

Proof. Let \(\rho_{\delta}\) be a mollifier supported in the ball of radius \(\delta\). If \(z\) lies in the cell of \(x\) and \(|w| \leqslant \delta\), then \(z - w\) lies in the cell of a lattice point within \(\delta + C / N\) of \(x\). So \(\| \iota_N u_N - \rho_{\delta} \ast \iota_N u_N \|_{\infty} \leqslant \sup_{|a| \leqslant \delta + C / N} \| \tau_a u_N - u_N \|_{\infty}\), whose limsup is at most \(\varpi (2 \delta)\). For each fixed \(\delta\), the functions \(\rho_{\delta} \ast \iota_N u_N\) are bounded in \(C^1\), so by the Ascoli theorem and a diagonal argument over \(\delta = 1 / m\) they converge uniformly along a subsequence for every \(m\). By the last bound \(\iota_N u_N\) is uniformly Cauchy along it, and the limit is a uniform limit of the continuous limits of \(\rho_{1 / m} \ast \iota_N u_N\). If \(\iota_N u_N \to u\) in \(H\), then every such limit equals \(u\).\(\Box\)

The continuum coordinate. Applying \(C_s\), the coordinate equation \(K_s y + D \bar{\Gamma}_s (y) = K_s x\) reads, by (12.5),

\(\displaystyle y + \varrho_s (y) + \frac{\lambda}{3} C_s y^3 = x \quad (s \geqslant q_{\ast}) . \) (31.1)

We take this equation, with the maps of Proposition 29.3, as the continuum coordinate equation, for \(y \in L^{\infty}\). For \(s < q_{\ast}\) we use (12.7), that is \(y_s (x) = y_{q_{\ast}} (x - (q_{\ast} - s) C_{q_{\ast}} x)\).

The continuum defect is defined on \(L^{\infty}\) by

\(\displaystyle e_s (y) = \int_0^1 \langle De_s (ty), y \rangle \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} t \quad (s \geqslant q_{\ast}), \quad e_s (y) = - \frac{1}{2} \|y\|_2^2 + \Upsilon (y) \quad (s < q_{\ast}) . \) (31.2)

Theorem 31.2 shows that it is the limit of the lattice defects.

Theorem 31.2. (Consistency) This is Lemma 18.3 of Lecture 7. Let \(s > 0\), \(x_N \in H_N\), let \(x\) be continuous on \(\mathbb{T}^3\), and suppose \(\iota_N x_N \to x\) uniformly. Then:

  1. There is a unique \(y_s (x) \in L^{\infty}\) solving the continuum coordinate equation, it is continuous, and \(\iota_N y^N_s (x_N) \to y_s (x)\) uniformly. Moreover \(\|y_s (x) - y_s (x')\|_{\infty} \leqslant 3 \|x - x' \|_{\infty}\) for \(s \geqslant q_{\ast}\), with the constant \(6\) for \(s < q_{\ast}\).

  2. If \(z_N \in H_N\) are bounded in \(L^{\infty}\) and \(\iota_N z_N \to z\) in \(H\), then \(e^N_s (z_N) \to e_s (z)\) and \(\Upsilon^N (z_N) \to \Upsilon (z)\). In particular \(e^N_s (y^N_s (x_N)) \to e_s (y_s (x))\).

  3. For \(s \geqslant q_{\ast}\), uniformly in \(N\), \(|e^N_s (y) | \leqslant Cg_s \|y\|_2^2\); for \(s < q_{\ast}\), \(|e^N_s (y) | \leqslant C \|y\|_2^2\).

Proof. Let first \(s \geqslant q_{\ast}\).

Uniqueness. Let \(y, y' \in L^{\infty}\) solve (31.1), and put \(\delta = y - y'\), \(f = D \tilde{h}_s (y) - D \tilde{h}_s (y')\) and \(w = \frac{\lambda}{3} (y^3 - (y')^3)\), which lie in \(H\). By the form of \(\varrho_s\) in Proposition 29.3, in Fourier variables,

\(\displaystyle m_s (k) \hat{\delta} (k) = - \hat{f} (k) - \hat{w} (k), \quad m_s (k) = s + 4 \pi^2 |k|^2 + \mu_s + \hat{\Theta}_s (k) .\)

By the small relative form bound used in the proof of Lemma 26.1, \(m_s (k) \geqslant \frac{1}{2} (s + 4 \pi^2 |k|^2)\). So \(\delta \in H^2\), and \(y - x \in H^1\) by (31.1). Multiplying by the conjugate of \(\hat{\delta} (k)\) and summing,

\(\displaystyle \frac{s}{2} \| \delta \|_2^2 \leqslant \sum_k m_s (k) | \hat{\delta} (k) |^2 = - \langle \delta, f \rangle - \langle \delta, w \rangle \leqslant Cg_s s \| \delta \|_2^2,\)

as \(\langle \delta, w \rangle \geqslant 0\) by the monotonicity of the cube. For \(q_{\ast}\) large this gives \(\delta = 0\).

Convergence. Put \(y_N = y^N_s (x_N)\). As \(\bar{\Gamma}^N_s\) is even, \(y^N_s (0) = 0\), so Lemma 16.3 gives \(\|y_N \|_{\infty} \leqslant 3 \|x_N \|_{\infty}\), which is bounded. As \(\bar{\Gamma}^N_s\) is translation invariant, the uniqueness of the coordinate gives \(y^N_s (\tau_a x_N) = \tau_a y^N_s (x_N)\), so the same lemma gives

\(\displaystyle \| \tau_a y_N - y_N \|_{\infty} \leqslant 3 \| \tau_a x_N - x_N \|_{\infty} \leqslant 6 \| \iota_N x_N - x\|_{\infty} + 3 \varpi_x (|a| + C / N),\)

with \(\varpi_x\) the modulus of continuity of \(x\). By Lemma 31.1, along a subsequence \(\iota_N y_N \to y\) uniformly, with \(y\) continuous. Pass to the limit in the lattice form of (31.1). Then \(\iota_N (y_N^3) \to y^3\) uniformly and \(\iota_N C^N_s j_N \to C_s\) in \(\text{HS}\), by Lemma 26.1 with vanishing path, so that \(\iota_N C^N_s y_N^3 \to C_s y^3\) in \(H\). By (29.7), \(\iota_N \varrho^N_s (y_N) \to \varrho_s (y)\) in \(H\). Thus \(y\) solves (31.1). So a bounded solution exists, it is continuous, and by uniqueness every subsequential limit equals it: the whole sequence converges. The Lipschitz bound passes to the limit, using \(x_N = j_N x\) and \(x'_N = j_N x'\).

Defect. The lattice defect satisfies \(e^N_s (0) = 0\), as \(\tilde{h}^N_s\) and its scale derivative vanish at \(0\), and so \(e^N_s (y) = \int_0^1 \langle De^N_s (ty), y \rangle \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} t\). Let \(z_N, z\) be as in (b). For each \(t\), \(\iota_N (tz_N) \to tz\) in \(H\) and is bounded, so by Proposition 29.3, \(\iota_N De^N_s (tz_N) \to De_s (tz)\) weakly in \(H\), and \(\iota_N z_N \to z\) strongly; the pairing converges. As \(De^N_s (0) = 0\) by evenness, (29.4) with second field \(0\) gives \(\|De^N_s (tz_N)\|_2 \leqslant C (\|z_N \|_2 +\|z_N^2 \|_2)\), so dominated convergence in \(t\) gives \(e^N_s (z_N) \to e_s (z)\). The same proof applies to \(\Upsilon^N (z_N)\) with \(D \Upsilon^N\), by (30.3). This also proves the limit claim in (30.3). Part (a) makes the choice \(z_N = y^N_s (x_N)\) admissible.

The bound (c). The proof of the global linear bound of Proposition 12.2 (Lemma 21.2) gives \(\|De^N_s (y)\|_p \leqslant Cg_s \|y\|_p\) for all \(y\), and so \(|e^N_s (y) | \leqslant Cg_s \|y\|_2^2\). For \(s < q_{\ast}\) this is (12.9).

The frozen case \(s < q_{\ast}\). By (12.7), \(y^N_s (x_N) = y^N_{q_{\ast}} (x_N')\) with \(x_N' = x_N - (q_{\ast} - s) C^N_{q_{\ast}} x_N\). The operator \(C^N_{q_{\ast}}\) has norm \(1 / q_{\ast}\) on \(L^{\infty}\) and commutes with lattice translations, so \(u_N = C^N_{q_{\ast}} x_N\) is bounded and \(\| \tau_a u_N - u_N \|_{\infty} \leqslant q_{\ast}^{- 1} \| \tau_a x_N - x_N \|_{\infty}\). Also \(\iota_N u_N \to C_{q_{\ast}} x\) in \(H\) by Lemma 26.1. So by Lemma 31.1, \(\iota_N x_N' \to x' = x - (q_{\ast} - s) C_{q_{\ast}} x\) uniformly, with \(x'\) continuous. The case \(s = q_{\ast}\) gives (a), with Lipschitz constant \(3 \|I - (q_{\ast} - s) C_{q_{\ast}} \|_{\infty \to \infty} \leqslant 6\). By definition \(e^N_s (y) = - \frac{1}{2} \|y\|_2^2 + \Upsilon^N (y)\); \(\| \iota_N z_N \|_2^2 \to \|z\|_2^2\) by the strong convergence, and \(\Upsilon^N (z_N) \to \Upsilon (z)\) as shown. This proves (b).\(\Box\)

The following gives the second claim of Section 18.1 of Lecture 7: the identity \(H^N = (I - H^N D^2 \bar{\Gamma}^N) C^N\) and its consequence on high modes. Let \(F^N_{> m}\) be the Fourier projection on \(H_N\) onto the modes \(k \in \Lambda^{\ast}_N\) with \(|k| > m\), and \(F_{\leqslant m}\) the Fourier projection on \(H\) onto the modes \(|k| \leqslant m\).

Lemma 31.3. (High modes of the response) For \(q \geqslant q_{\ast}\), \(y \in H_N\) with \(\|y\|_{\infty} \leqslant L\), and \(m \geqslant 1\),

\(\displaystyle H^N_q (y) = (I - H^N_q (y) D^2 \bar{\Gamma}^N_q (y)) C^N_q, \quad \|H^N_q (y) D^2 \bar{\Gamma}^N_q (y)\|_{2 \to 2} \leqslant C (1 + \lambda L^2 / q), \) (31.3)

and hence, uniformly in \(N\),

\(\displaystyle \|H^N_q (y) F^N_{> m} \|_{\text{HS}} \leqslant C (1 + \lambda L^2 / q) m^{- 1 / 2} . \) (31.4)

For \(q < q_{\ast}\) the bound (31.4) holds with \(H^N_q = H^N_{q_{\ast}}\).

Proof. The identity is \(H^N (K_q + D^2 \bar{\Gamma}^N) = I\). By (12.1), \(D^2 \bar{\Gamma}^N = \lambda y^2 + \mu_q + \Theta_q + D^2 \tilde{h}_q (y)\). By Lemma 12.1, \(\|H^N \|_{2 \to 2} \leqslant C / q\). So \(H^N (\lambda y^2 + \mu_q + D^2 \tilde{h}_q)\) has norm at most \(C (\lambda L^2 / q + g_q)\), by (11.12) and (12.2). The kernel \(\Theta_q\) is not bounded on \(L^2\) uniformly in \(N\), and we argue by the other resolvent identity. By Lemma 24.1, \(\Theta_q C_q\) has the multiplier \(\hat{\Theta}_q (k) (q + c_k)^{- 1}\), of size at most \(Cv_1 (\Theta_q) q^{- 1 / 2} \leqslant CR \lambda^2 q^{- 1} \leqslant \frac{1}{2}\). From \(H^N = C_q - C_q D^2 \bar{\Gamma}^N H^N\),

\(\displaystyle (I + \Theta_q C_q) \Theta_q H^N = \Theta_q C_q (I - (\lambda y^2 + \mu_q + D^2 \tilde{h}_q) H^N),\)

so that \(\| \Theta_q H^N \|_{2 \to 2} \leqslant C \lambda^2 q^{- 1} (1 + \lambda L^2 / q)\), and \(H^N \Theta_q\) is its adjoint. This proves (31.3). Next

\(\displaystyle \|C^N_q F^N_{> m} \|_{\text{HS}}^2 = \sum_{k \in \Lambda^{\ast}_N, |k| > m} (q + c_k)^{- 2} \leqslant C \sum_{|k| > m} (1 + |k|^2)^{- 2} \leqslant C / m,\)

and multiplying by the bounded factor of (31.3) gives (31.4). The frozen case uses the same identity at \(q_{\ast}\).\(\Box\)

The projected noise. Let \(W\) be a cylindrical Brownian motion on \(H\). As in Section 18.3 of Lecture 7, its Fourier projection is \(W^N = E_N W\), where

\(\displaystyle E_N f = \sum_{k \in \Lambda^{\ast}_N} \langle f, e_k \rangle e^N_k,\)

a real operator \(H \to H_N\) with \(E_N E_N^{\ast} = I\), as \(N\) is odd and the \(e^N_k\) are orthonormal in \(H_N\). The alternative is \(W^N = j_N W\), with \(j_N j_N^{\ast} = j_N \iota_N = I\). Both are cylindrical on \(H_N\). We show that both give the same limit.

Theorem 31.4. (Stochastic integrals against the projected noise) Let \(W\) be a cylindrical Brownian motion on \(H\) for a filtration \((\mathcal{F}_q)\), and let \(Y^N\) and \(Y\) be progressively measurable, and satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem 30.1 on a compact interval \(I\). Let \(W^N\) be either projection of \(W\). Then

\(\displaystyle \mathbb{E} \sup_{t \in I} \| \int_{I, q \leqslant t} \iota_N H^N_q (Y^N_q) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W^N_q - \int_{I, q \leqslant t} H_q (Y_q) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W_q \|_2^2 \to 0. \) (31.5)

In particular \(\int_I \iota_N H^N (Y^N) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W^N \to \int_I H (Y) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W\) in \(H\), in probability. For \(q < q_{\ast}\) this holds with the frozen coefficients (29.2), and \(I\) may contain \(q_{\ast}\).

Proof. Let \(E^{\sharp}_N\) be either \(E_N\) or \(j_N\). Then \(\int \iota_N H^N (Y^N) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W^N = \int \mathcal{J}^N \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W\), with the integrand \(\mathcal{J}^N_q = \iota_N H^N_q (Y^N_q) E^{\sharp}_N\), which is progressively measurable. So by Doob's inequality and the Itô isometry the left side of (31.5) is at most \(4\mathbb{E} \int_I \| \Phi^N_q - H_q (Y_q)\|_{\text{HS}}^2 \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} q\). By (12.3) and (12.9), \(\| \Phi^N_q \|_{\text{HS}}^2 \leqslant \text{Tr} H^N_q (Y^N_q)^2 \leqslant C_I\) deterministically, and the same bound holds for the limit by lower semicontinuity. It is therefore enough to show that \(\Phi^N \to H (Y)\) in \(L^2 (I ; \text{HS})\) in probability.

For \(E^{\sharp}_N = j_N\) this is Theorem 30.1. For \(E^{\sharp}_N = E_N\), it remains to show that \(\| \iota_N H^N_q (Y^N_q) (E_N - j_N)\|_{\text{HS}} \to 0\) in \(L^2 (I)\), in probability. Work on the event that \(\sup_I \|Y^N \|_{\infty} \leqslant L\) and split with \(F_{\leqslant m}\) and \(I - F_{\leqslant m}\).

Low modes. \(E_N e_k = e^N_k\), while \(j_N e_k =\mathfrak{s}_N (k) e^N_k\) with \(\mathfrak{s}_N (k) = \prod_i \text{sinc} (\pi k_i / N)\). So for \(m\) fixed, \(\|(E_N - j_N) F_{\leqslant m} \|_{\text{HS}} \to 0\), and \(\|H^N \|_{2 \to 2} \leqslant C\).

High modes, Fourier. Since \(E_N (I - F_{\leqslant m}) = F^N_{> m} E_N\) and \(\|E_N \| \leqslant 1\), (31.4) gives \(\|H^N E_N (I - F_{\leqslant m})\|_{\text{HS}} \leqslant C_L m^{- 1 / 2}\), uniformly in \(N\) and in \(q \in I\).

High modes, cell average. This operator does not preserve high modes exactly, because of aliases, but

\(\displaystyle \| \iota_N H^N_q (Y^N_q) j_N (I - F_{\leqslant m})\|_{\text{HS}} \leqslant \| \iota_N H^N_q (Y^N_q) j_N - H_q (Y_q)\|_{\text{HS}} + \|H_q (Y_q) (I - F_{\leqslant m})\|_{\text{HS}} .\)

The first term tends to \(0\) in \(L^2 (I)\) in probability by Theorem 30.1. The second tends to \(0\) as \(m \to \infty\), for each \(q\), and it is dominated, so it tends to \(0\) in \(L^2 (I)\) almost surely. Sending \(N \to \infty\) and then \(m \to \infty\) and \(L \to \infty\) gives the claim.

For the final assertions, the frozen case is included in Theorem 30.1. If \(I\) contains \(q_{\ast}\), split it there; the integrand is defined for all \(q\), and the formula for \(H_q\) is continuous across \(q_{\ast}\), as the drift is not involved.\(\Box\)

Remark 31.5. (Across the auxiliary scale) \(\bar{\Gamma}_q\) is continuous in \(q\) at \(q_{\ast}\), and \(y_{q_{\ast} -} = y_{q_{\ast}}\) by (12.7), as \(C_{q_{\ast}} K_{q_{\ast}} = I\). So \(Y_q = y_q (x_q)\) is continuous across \(q_{\ast}\), though \(b_q\) jumps there. Its forward equation is obtained by integrating on each side and adding; the jump of \(b\) does not matter for convergence in \(L^2 (I ; H)\). The adaptedness of \(Y^N\) and \(Y\) in Theorem 31.4 is a genuine hypothesis, not implied by the convergence. In Section 18.3 of Lecture 7 it holds since the explicit flow is a strong solution, adapted to \(W\).

32.1Regularity in space and scale

On a finite scale interval \(I = [q_{\ast}, Q]\) write the field equation (13.5) as the linear elliptic equation

\(\displaystyle K_{q_0} V_q = f_q, \qquad f_q = (q - q_0) (\zeta_q - \varrho_q (Y_q) + Z_q) - \frac{\lambda}{3} Y_q^3 . \) (32.1)

The energies control \(\mathbb{E} \int_I \|Y_q \|_6^6 \mathrm{d} q\) (since \(|Y|^6 \leqslant r_q^6 + |t_q |^6\)) and \(\mathbb{E} \int_I \|Z_q \|_2^2 \mathrm{d} q\), and \(\mathbb{E} \| \zeta_q \|_2^2 \leqslant C \sqrt{q}\). Hence \(\mathbb{E} \int_I \|f_q \|_2^2 \leqslant C_I\), and testing with \(V\),

\(\displaystyle \mathbb{E} \int_I (\| \nabla_N V_q \|_2^2 +\|V_q \|_2^2) \mathrm{d} q \leqslant C_I .\)

So the part \(V\) of the approximate forecast is in \(H^1\), and \(Y = V + \zeta - \varrho (Y)\) inherits the regularity of the noise \(\zeta\). This is that of the free field, whatever the interaction: by Lemma 13.2, \(\mathbb{E} \| \zeta_q \|_{H^{\sigma}}^2 \leqslant C (q - q_0) \sum_k (1 + |k|^2)^{\sigma} (q + c|k|^2)^{- 2}\), finite uniformly in \(N\) for \(\sigma < \frac{1}{2}\).

Proposition 32.1. (Forward compactness) For \(0 < \sigma, \theta < \frac{1}{2}\),

\(\displaystyle \sup_N \mathbb{E} \left[ \int_I \| \iota_N Y^N_q \|_{H^{\sigma}}^2 \mathrm{d} q +\| \iota_N Y^N \|^2_{H^{\theta} (I ; H)} \right] < \infty .\)

The embedded approximate forecasts are tight in every space–time \(L^p\), and polynomials of them converge along strongly convergent subsequences. The forward equation gives the Hölder bound \(\sup_N \mathbb{E} \| \iota_N Y^N \|^8_{C^{1 / 4} (I ; H)} < \infty\), and averaging in time against the \(H^{\sigma}\) bound gives uniform Fourier tails \(\lim_m \sup_N \mathbb{E} \sup_{q \in I} \|(I - \textsf{F}_m) \iota_N Y^N_q \|_2^2 = 0\) (\(\textsf{F}_m\) the projection on frequencies \(|k| \leqslant m\)); hence they are also tight in \(C (I ; H)\).

The spatial bound combines the \(H^1\) bound on \(V\), the \(H^{\sigma}\) regularity of \(\zeta\) (\(\sigma < \frac{1}{2}\), for which the cell embedding is uniformly bounded), and the contraction property of \(\varrho\): since \(\varrho\) commutes with lattice translations \(\tau_a\), (12.5) gives \(\| \tau_a \varrho (Y) - \varrho (Y)\|_2 \leqslant \varepsilon_q \| \tau_a Y - Y\|_2\), so the increments of \(Y\) are controlled by those of \(V + \zeta\); the scale regularity uses the forward equation, whose drift \(b (Y) + H (Y) Z\) has bounded integrated square by Proposition 12.2 and the energies, and whose martingale part has bounded bracket density. The supremum bound of Lemma 17.1 upgrades \(L^2\) compactness to every \(L^p\).

32.2The limit by compactness: the noise

The delicate point is the backward martingale. Fields converging in law do not imply that the stochastic integrals converge to stochastic integrals of the limits.

Remark 32.2. (Hidden noise) Let \(B\) be a real Brownian motion and \(L^n_t = \int_0^t (1 + \sin (ns)) \mathrm{d} B_s\). Then \((L^n, B)\) converges in law to \((B + 2^{- 1 / 2} \tilde{B}, B)\), with \(\tilde{B}\) an independent Brownian motion: the integrands converge weakly to \(1\), but their squares converge weakly to \(\frac{3}{2}\). A limiting FBSDE obtained this way would have an extra martingale, orthogonal to the driving noise.

To exclude this one needs strong compactness of the integrands. For the forward equation the integrand \(H (Y)\) is an explicit function of \(Y\), and strong convergence follows from the coefficient limits. For the backward equation the integrand is \(\Xi = \text{Cov} - H (Y)\), and we need strong compactness of the posterior covariance \(\text{Cov}_q\). It comes from its exact dynamics.

Lemma 32.3. (Riccati equation of the posterior covariance) At each \(N\), the conditional covariance \(\text{Cov}_q > 0\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle \text{dCov}_q = - \text{Cov}_q^2 \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} q +\mathfrak{k}_q [\mathrm{d} W_q],\) (32.2)

where \(\mathfrak{k}_q\) is the third conditional cumulant of \(\Phi_{\infty}\) given \(\mathcal{F}_q\).

Proof. The conditional law is an exponential family in \(p = K_q \Phi_q\) (proof of Proposition 10.4 of Lecture 6), with log-partition function \(\mathcal{K}_q (p) = \log \int \mathrm{e}^{\langle p, \varphi \rangle - \frac{q - q_0}{2} \| \varphi \|_2^2} \nu_N (\mathrm{d} \varphi)\), so \(\text{Cov}_q = D^2 \mathcal{K}_q (p)\) and \(\mathfrak{k}_q = D^3 \mathcal{K}_q (p)\). Differentiating, \(\partial_q \mathcal{K}+ \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} D^2 \mathcal{K}+ \frac{1}{2} \|D\mathcal{K}\|_2^2 = 0\); differentiate this twice in \(p\): \((\partial_q + \frac{1}{2} \Delta_p + D\mathcal{K} \cdot D_p) D^2 \mathcal{K}= - (D^2 \mathcal{K})^2\), and apply Itô's formula along \(\mathrm{d} p = D\mathcal{K} (p) \mathrm{d} q + \mathrm{d} W\), which follows from (10.6), \(\partial_q K_q = I\) and \(\psi_q = \Phi_q + C_q F_q\).\(\Box\)

This is (8.9) of Lecture 4 for \(\partial_q \mathcal{R}= 1\): conditional variance is resolved at the rate \(\text{Cov}^2\). For a smooth test \(f\), the process \(\langle f, \text{Cov}_q f \rangle \geqslant 0\) is a nonnegative supermartingale, with drift \(- \| \text{Cov}_q f\|^2\), and initial value \(\text{Var}_{\nu_N} \langle \Phi_{\infty}, f \rangle\), bounded uniformly in \(N\) by Corollary 17.2. A nonnegative supermartingale has controlled oscillations: after stopping at the first time it exceeds a level \(K\) (an event of probability at most \(C_f / K\)), its decreasing part \(A\) and the bracket of its martingale part \(J\) satisfy \(\mathbb{E}A_Q \leqslant C_f\) and \(\mathbb{E} [J]_Q \leqslant K^2 + 2 KC_f\), which gives time regularity in \(W^{a, 1}\), \(a < \frac{1}{2}\), and hence strong compactness of each finite block \(\langle f_i, \text{Cov} f_j \rangle\). In this subsection \(I = [q_0, Q]\), since \(\psi\), \(W\) and the covariance do not involve \(Y\). Spatial tails come from the isometry (recall \(\psi_{q_0} =\mathbb{E} \Phi_{\infty} = 0\), \(\nu_N\) being even), with symmetry exchanging input and output tails, \(\mathbb{E} \int_I \|(I - \textsf{F}_m) \text{Cov}_q \|_{\text{HS}}^2 \mathrm{d} q =\mathbb{E} \|(I - \textsf{F}_m) \psi_Q \|_2^2\), and from the terminal averaging \(\psi_Q =\mathbb{E}_Q \int_L^{L + 1} (Y_s + Z_s) \mathrm{d} s\), using the \(H^{\sigma}\) bound of Proposition 32.1 and \(\mathbb{E} \|Z_s \|_2^2 \leqslant Cs^{- 5 / 2}\) (first \(m \to \infty\), then \(L \to \infty\)). Altogether \(\text{Cov}^N\) is tight in strong \(L^2 (I ; \text{HS})\). Since \(S \mapsto \int SS^{\ast}\) is continuous from \(L^2 (I ; \text{HS})\) to \(C (I ; \text{trace class})\), the brackets of \(\psi^N\) are tight in \(C (I)\); a continuous martingale with tight brackets and uniformly small maximal Fourier tails (from the terminal averaging above and Doob's inequality) is tight in \(C (I ; H)\). Hence \(\psi^N\), and \(Z^N = \psi^N - Y^N\), are tight in \(C (I ; H)\). Strong convergence of the integrands gives convergence of the brackets \(\int \text{CovCov}^{\ast}\) and of the covariations with \(W\), and then:

Proposition 32.4. (Identification of the martingales) Along a subsequence, on a common filtered probability space, the limit of the forecast satisfies \(\psi_q = \int_{q_0}^q \text{Cov}_s \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W_s\) with \(W\) a cylindrical Brownian motion for the joint filtration, \(X_q = \int_{q_0}^q C_s \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W_s\), and for \(q \geqslant q_0\) (for \(q < q_{\ast}\) see the paragraph after Theorem 32.5)

\(\displaystyle Y_q = \int_{q_0}^q (b_s (Y_s) + H_s (Y_s) Z_s) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s + \int_{q_0}^q H_s (Y_s) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W_s, \quad Z_q = - \int_{q_0}^q (b_s (Y_s) + H_s (Y_s) Z_s) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s + \int_{q_0}^q \Xi_s \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W_s . \) (32.3)

The argument is: the limiting \(\psi\) is a continuous square-integrable martingale for the joint filtration (fixed-time martingale identities pass to the limit with uniform integrability); its bracket and its covariation with \(W\) are those of \(\int \text{Cov} \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W\); so \(\psi - \int \text{Cov} \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W\) has zero bracket and vanishes. No extra martingale survives. The same argument for the forward equation identifies its noise with the same \(W\), and identifies the limit of the auxiliary Gaussian field \(X\) as \(\int C \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W\), not a new Gaussian field.

32.3The limit by compactness: the result

Theorem 32.5. (Construction of \(\Phi^4_3\)) Let \(\lambda, q_0 > 0\), \(r \in \mathbb{R}\), with the counterterm of Theorem 11.3 (or \(q_{\ast}\) large and \(b_{\ast, N} \to b_{\ast}\), \(|b_{\ast, N} | \leqslant B_0 \lambda q_{\ast}^{1 / 2}\)). Along a subsequence of lattices (the whole sequence, by Theorem 18.6):

  1. the embedded processes \((\psi^N, X^N, Y^N, Z^N)\), with \(X^N_q = \int_{q_0}^q C_s \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W^N_s\) the auxiliary Gaussian field, converge in law in \(C (I ; H)\) for compact \(I \subset [q_0, \infty)\), together with the embedded innovations \(W^N\) in \(C (I ; H^{- \vartheta})\), \(\vartheta > \frac{3}{2}\), and \(\text{Cov}^N, \Xi^N\) in strong \(L^2 (I ; \text{HS})\); all jointly and with an almost sure representation;

  2. the limit solves the FBSDE (32.3) on \([q_0, \infty)\), \(Y_{q_0} = Z_{q_0} = 0\), with the deterministic continuum coefficients \(b, H\), limits of those of Lecture 6 and unique in their ball, an \(H\)-cylindrical Brownian motion \(W\) for the joint filtration, obtained as the limit of the lattice innovations, a symmetric nonnegative integrand \(\text{Cov} = H (Y) + \Xi\), and \(\mathbb{E} \|Z_q \|_2^2 \leqslant Cq^{- 5 / 2}\) for large \(q\);

  3. there is a terminal field \(\Phi\) with \(Y_q \to \Phi\) in \(H^{- s}\), almost surely and in mean square, for every \(s > \frac{1}{2}\). Its law \(\nu\) is the weak limit of the lattice measures \(\nu_N\), and for smooth \(h\),

    \(\displaystyle \langle \psi_q, h \rangle =\mathbb{E} [\langle \Phi, h \rangle |\mathcal{F}_q] : \) (32.4)

    the forecast remains the conditional mean of the terminal field;

  4. the limiting thresholded energies \(E_{\infty}, J_{\infty}\) satisfy the bounds of Theorem 16.2. Indeed, the lattice identities \(V_q = C_q \left[ (q - q_0) \psi_q - \frac{\lambda}{3} Y_q^3 \right]\) and \(\zeta_q = X_q + C_q \int_{q_0}^q (\psi_s - X_s) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s - (q - q_0) C_q \psi_q\) need neither \(\varrho\) nor \(W\), so \(V^N\) and \(\zeta^N\) converge by (a), the \(L^6\) convergence of \(Y^N\) and the Hilbert–Schmidt convergence of the free resolvent; the integrands of \(E\) and \(J\) are lower semicontinuous in these variables, and Fatou's lemma applies.

Below the auxiliary scale. Above \(q_{\ast}\) the proof is that of Section 32.2, Propositions 32.1 and 32.4, with the moments of \(Y_{q_{\ast}}\) from Section 17, and spatial compactness at \(q_{\ast}\) by one-sided averaging on \([q_{\ast}, q_{\ast} + \delta]\). The processes \(\psi, W, \text{Cov}\) never involve \(Y\): for \(q \leqslant Q\), \(\psi_q =\mathbb{E}_q \psi_Q\) gives their moments, and the Riccati argument starts at \(q_0\) from the deterministic \(\text{Var}_{\nu_N} \langle \Phi^N_{\infty}, j_N f \rangle\). On \(I = [q_0, q_{\ast}]\), \(C_{q_{\ast}} K_s \Phi^N_s = C_{q_{\ast}} W^N_s + \int_{q_0}^s C_{q_{\ast}} \psi^N_u \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} u\) is tight in \(C (I ; L^{\infty})\): the first term by Kolmogorov, the second as \(C_{q_{\ast}} : L^2 \to C^{1 / 2}\) and \(\psi^N\) converges in \(C (I ; H)\). By (12.7) and Lemmas 16.3, 18.3, \(Y^N_s = y^N_{q_{\ast}} (C_{q_{\ast}} K_s \Phi^N_s) \to Y\) uniformly on \(I \times \mathbb{T}^3\), so \(Z^N \to Z\) in \(C (I ; H)\). The convergence stated at the end of Section 18.1, at the single scale \(q_{\ast}\), gives \(H (Y^N) \to H (Y)\) in \(L^2 (I ; \text{HS})\) and \(b (Y^N) \to b (Y)\) in \(L^2 (I ; H)\), so \(\Xi^N \to \Xi\) and the limit solves the system on \(I\). At \(q_0\), \(\Phi_{q_0} = 0\): so \(Y_{q_0} = 0\) since \(\bar{\mathcal{F}}\) is odd, and \(Z_{q_0} = \psi_{q_0} =\mathbb{E}_{\nu} \Phi = 0\).

So the posterior structure survives the continuum limit: \(\psi = Y + Z\) is a closed observable, the conditional mean of the terminal field; \(Y\) is computed from the explicit action; and the error \(Z\) is small at high resolution. This is the FBSDE of Lecture 4, in forecast coordinates, for the limit \(\nu\) of the renormalised lattice measures, with the full noise retained.

32.4Density at a fixed scale

The following is not used in the lectures: it gives the law at a fixed high scale, and it is where the second chaos of \(\bar{V}_q\) becomes visible.

Proposition 32.6. (Density at a fixed scale) Let \(q\) be large enough, beyond the auxiliary scale and such that \(\sup_N \|Q_q^{1 / 2} \Theta^N_q Q_q^{1 / 2} \| \leqslant \frac{1}{4}\). There are constants \(c_N (q)\) with: \(\mathrm{e}^{- \bar{V}_q + c_N}\) is bounded in \(L^p (\gamma_{Q_q})\), \(1 < p < 2\), uniformly in \(N\), and converges in probability along the coupling to a function \(\mathrm{e}^{- \bar{V}^{\circ}_q}\). The measure

\(\displaystyle \rho_q (\hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} x) = \mathrm{e}^{- \bar{V}_q^{\circ} (x) - A (q, x)} \gamma_{Q_q} (\hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} x) \) (32.5)

is finite, and for bounded continuous cylinder \(O\),

\(\displaystyle \int O \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} \nu = \frac{\int \mathrm{e}^{- \bar{V}^{\circ}_q (x)} \gamma_{Q_q} (\hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} x) \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{R}^{q, x}} \left[ O (Y_{\infty}) \mathrm{e}^{- \int_q^{\infty} e_s (Y_s) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s} \right]}{\int \mathrm{e}^{- \bar{V}^{\circ}_q (x)} \gamma_{Q_q} (\hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} x) \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{R}^{q, x}} \left[ \mathrm{e}^{- \int_q^{\infty} e_s (Y_s) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s} \right]} . \) (32.6)

The law of \(\Phi_q\) under \(\nu\) is \(\rho_q\) normalised, the convergence holds for the whole sequence without the limit \(q \to \infty\), and the renormalised partition functions \(\int \mathrm{e}^{- V_{q, N} +\mathcal{C}_N (q) + c_N} \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} \gamma_{Q_q}\) converge to the total mass of \(\rho_q\).

Proof. Bounds on \(\bar{V}\). On the unit volume, \(\bar{V}_q (x) = \min_y \left[ \bar{\Gamma}_q (y) + \frac{1}{2} \langle x - y, K_q (x - y) \rangle \right]\), the functional being strictly convex for \(q \geqslant q_{\ast}\). As \(U + \frac{1}{2} \mu_q y^2 + \tilde{h}_q \geqslant - C_q\) (the quartic dominates, as \(\textsf{S}_4 (h_q)\) is small, and \(| \tilde{h}_q (y) | \leqslant C \textsf{S}_4 (h_q) \|y\|_4^4\)), \(\bar{V}_q (x) \geqslant - C_q + \frac{1}{2} \langle x, \Theta_q' x \rangle\), with \(\Theta_q' = K_q (K_q + \Theta_q)^{- 1} \Theta_q\). With \(c_N = \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} (Q_q \Theta'_q)\), the determinant formula for the second chaos gives the \(L^p\) bound, since the operator has norm \(\leqslant \frac{1}{2}\) and is Hilbert–Schmidt, uniformly in \(N\); here the multiplier of \(\Theta_q\) is at most \(C |k|\) and that of \(Q_q\) at most \(C |k|^{- 4}\). Convergence in probability is Step 4 of the proof of Theorem 32.13, without \(\chi_q\): the second chaos in \(L^2\), the other terms by continuity for the uniform norm.

Cut-off. For fixed \(N\), the law of \(\Phi_q\) is proportional to \(\mathrm{e}^{- \bar{V}_q + c_N - A_N (q, \cdot)} \gamma_{Q_q}\) by the definition of \(A_N\). For \(\chi_R\) a continuous cut-off of \(\|x\|_{\infty}\), on its support \(A_N\) is bounded and continuously convergent (Theorem 18.4), and \(\iota_N x_N \to x\) uniformly in probability; with the \(L^p\) bound,

\(\displaystyle I_N (R) : = \int \chi_R \mathrm{e}^{- \bar{V}_q + c_N - A_N} \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} \gamma_{Q_q} \to I (R) : = \int \chi_R \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} \rho_q,\)

with \(I (R) > 0\), and the same with a bounded continuous function under the integral. By Step 1 of the proof of Theorem 32.13, that is (12.5) and Lemma 17.1, \(\sup_N \mathbb{P} (\| \Phi^N_q \|_{\infty} > R / 2) \to 0\) as \(R \to \infty\). So for \(R\) large, \(I_N (R) / Z_N \geqslant \frac{1}{2}\), where \(Z_N\) is the total mass, and hence \(\text{limsup}_N Z_N \leqslant 2 I (R) < \infty\); \(\rho_q\) is finite by Fatou. Letting \(R \to \infty\) the law of \(\Phi_q^N\) converges to \(\rho_q\) normalised, as does \(Z_N\) to its total mass.

The formula. The kernel form follows by disintegrating \(\nu_N\) at scale \(q\), and using (18.12) with the uniform convergence in probability: the cylinder function \(\Pi^N_q O (x_N)\) is bounded, and converges for continuously convergent arguments. So \(\int O \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} \nu = \int \Pi_q O \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} \rho_q / \rho_q (1)\), which by (18.12) and (32.5) is (32.6), the factor \(\mathrm{e}^A\) cancelling.\(\Box\)

32.5Feynman–Kac comparison along the explicit flow

Let \(\bar{V}_q (x) = \Gamma^{\sharp} (y) + \frac{1}{2} \langle x - y, K_q (x - y) \rangle\), where \(y = y_q (x)\) solves \(K_q y + D \Gamma^{\sharp} (y) = K_q x\), for \(\Gamma^{\sharp}\) one of the actions below. Let \(e^{\sharp} = \partial_q \Gamma^{\sharp} - \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} [H^{\sharp} - H^{\sharp} (0)]\) and \(c (q) = \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} (C_q - H_q (0))\).

Lemma 32.7. (Hamilton–Jacobi defect) \(\partial_q \bar{V} + \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} (C_q^2 D^2 \bar{V}) - \frac{1}{2} \|C_q D \bar{V} \|_2^2 = e^{\sharp} \circ y + c (q)\), provided \(H^{\sharp} (0) = H_q (0)\).

Proof. By the envelope theorem \(\partial_q \bar{V} = \partial_q \Gamma^{\sharp} (y) + \frac{1}{2} \|x - y\|_2^2\). Further \(D \bar{V} = K_q (x - y)\), and since \(Dy = H^{\sharp} K_q\), \(D^2 \bar{V} = K_q - K_q H^{\sharp} K_q\). So \(\text{Tr} (C_q^2 D^2 \bar{V}) = \text{Tr} (C_q - H^{\sharp})\) and \(\|C_q D \bar{V} \|_2 = \|x - y\|_2\).\(\Box\)

The penalty. Let \(\chi \in C^{1, 1}\), smooth off \(\frac{15}{16}\) and nondecreasing, with \(\chi (u) = 0\) for \(u \leqslant \frac{15}{16}\), \(\chi (u) = 1\) for \(u \geqslant 1\), and \(\chi (u)^2 = \left( u - \frac{15}{16} \right)^4\) near \(\frac{15}{16}\). For a constant \(A\) put \(\mathfrak{g}_s (y) = g_s \int y^2 \chi^2 (|y| / r_s)\) and

\(\displaystyle \delta_q (y) = A \int_q^{\infty} \mathfrak{g}_s (y) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s = \int F_q (y (x)) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} x, \quad \Gamma^{\pm} = \bar{\Gamma} \pm \delta .\)

\(F_q\) is even and \(C^4\), vanishes on \(|u| \leqslant \frac{15}{16} r_q\), and the \(s\) integral runs up to \(s_u = \frac{256}{225} \lambda u^2\). Let \(v = |u| / r_q - \frac{15}{16}\). Then for \(v > 0\), with constants independent of \(A\) and \(q\),

\(\displaystyle |F_q'' (u) | \leqslant CAg_q (q + \lambda u^2) 1_{v > 0}, \quad |F_q'' (u) | \leqslant CAq^{1 / 2} \lambda \min (v_+^3, 1) \frac{|u|}{r_q} \quad \text{for all } u, \) (32.7)

the second from differentiating \(u^2 \int_q^{s_u} \lambda s^{- 1 / 2} \chi^2 (|u| / r_s) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s\) twice in \(u\): near the threshold the integral is of order \(u^2 g_q qv^5\), and for \(|u| \gg r_q\) it is of order \(A \lambda^{3 / 2} |u|^3\).

Lemma 32.8. (Global bounds for the comparison actions) For \(A\) fixed and \(q \geqslant q_A\), \(\Gamma^{\pm}\) satisfy the conclusions of Lemma 12.1, \(H^{\pm} (0) = H_q (0)\), and \(\|b^{\pm} (y)\|_2 \leqslant C_A \lambda q^{- 3 / 2} \|y\|_2\).

Proposition 32.12. (The comparison) Let \(\mathcal{C}_N (q) = \int_q^{\infty} c (s) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s\), which is finite at fixed \(N\). For \(q \geqslant \max (q_{\ast}, q_A)\), if \(\|y_q (x)\|_{\infty} \leqslant \frac{15}{16} r_q\), then, uniformly in \(N\),

\(\displaystyle |V_{q, N} (x) - \bar{V}_{q, N} (x) -\mathcal{C}_N (q) | \leqslant C \lambda^5 q^{- 2} \|y\|_4^4 + C \lambda^4 q^{- 1 / 2} (1 + \lambda q^{- 1} \|y\|_2^2) + C \lambda^4 q^{- 1} \|y\|_2^2 . \) (32.10)

Proof. Let \(\xi_s\) be the free flow, \(\hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} \xi = C_s \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W\), \(\xi_q = x\). Then \(\mathrm{e}^{- V_q (x)} =\mathbb{E} \mathrm{e}^{- V_T (\xi_T)}\) for \(T > q\), as \(V\) solves the Polchinski equation. Therefore, with \(\Delta^{\pm}_T = V_T - \bar{V}_T^{\pm}\),

\(\displaystyle \mathrm{e}^{- V_q (x)} = \mathrm{e}^{- \bar{V}_q^{\pm} (x) - \int_q^T c} \bar{\mathbb{E}}^{\pm} \exp (- \Delta^{\pm}_T (\bar{\Phi}_T) - \int_q^T e^{\pm} (Y_s) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s) . \) (32.11)

Upper bound for \(V_q\). Use \(\Gamma^+\) and Jensen in (32.11): \(V_q (x) \leqslant \bar{V}_q^+ (x) + \int_q^T c + \bar{\mathbb{E}}^+ \Delta^+_T (\bar{\Phi}_T) + \bar{\mathbb{E}}^+ \int_q^T e^+\). So the limsup of \(\bar{\mathbb{E}}^+ \Delta^+_T\) is at most \(0\).

Lower bound. Let \(\mathcal{G}_T\) be the event \(\| \xi_T \|_{\infty} \leqslant T^{1 / 8}\). Since \(V_T \geqslant \inf V_N > - \infty\) at fixed \(N\), \(\mathbb{E} \mathrm{e}^{- V_T (\xi_T)} 1_{\mathcal{G}_T^c} \leqslant C_N \mathbb{P} (\mathcal{G}_T^c) \to 0\). Further \(|V_T (x) - V_N (x) | = O_N (T^{- 1 / 8})\) on the same set, by Gaussian integration, since the remaining variance per site is \(O_N (T^{- 1})\). So

\(\displaystyle \mathrm{e}^{- V_q (x)} \leqslant \mathrm{e}^{o (1)} \mathrm{e}^{- \bar{V}_q^- (x) - \int_q^T c} \bar{\mathbb{E}}^- \exp (- \int_q^T e^- (Y_s) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s) + o (1) .\)

Theorem 32.13. (Uniqueness) Let \(q_{\ast} \geqslant \max (\hat{q}, q_A)\) and \(b_{\ast, N} \to b_{\ast}\) in \(\mathcal{B}\), in particular for the explicit counterterm (22.2). Then the whole sequence \(\iota_N \nu_N\) converges weakly in \(H^{- s}\), \(s > \frac{1}{2}\), to a measure \(\nu\). The joint law of \(\Phi\) and of the Polchinski process \(\Phi_q = (q - q_0) C_q \Phi + C_q B_{q - q_0}\), \(B\) independent of \(\Phi\), is unique. The dynamics are not used.

Proof. Fix \(\delta \in \left( 0, \frac{1}{12} \right)\), \(\rho_q = q^{5 / 12 + \delta}\), and \(\chi_q (x) = \tau (\|x\|_{\infty} / \rho_q)\) with \(\tau\) continuous, \(\tau = 1\) on \(\left[ 0, \frac{1}{2} \right]\), \(\tau = 0\) on \([1, \infty)\).

Step 1: the good set. By (12.5), \(\Phi_q = Y_q + \varrho_q (Y_q) + \frac{\lambda}{3} C_q Y_q^3\), so \(\| \Phi_q \|_{\infty} \leqslant 2 \|Y_q \|_{\infty} + \frac{\lambda}{3 q} \|Y_q \|_{\infty}^3\). Proposition 14.4, whose constant involves \(\|C_{q_0} \|_{2 \to \infty}\), finite with \(a_2 (q_0) \leqslant q_0^{- 2} + C\) uniformly in \(N\), with Lemma 14.5 and Theorem 16.2 gives Lemma 17.1: \(\|Y_q \|_{\infty} \leqslant \mathcal{Y}q^{5 / 12} \log^{1 / 6} (2 + q)\) for \(q \geqslant q_{\ast}\), with \(\mathcal{Y}\) having moments uniformly in \(N\). Therefore \(p_q : = \sup_N \mathbb{P} (\| \Phi^N_q \|_{\infty} > \rho_q / 2) \to 0\).

Step 2: the error. If \(\|x\|_{\infty} \leqslant \rho_q\) then \(\|y_q (x)\|_{\infty} \leqslant 3 \rho_q\). Indeed with \(u = y + \varrho_q (y)\), \(K_q (u - x) + \frac{\lambda}{3} y^3 = 0\). At a positive maximum of \(u - x\), \(K_q (u - x) > 0\), so \(y < 0\) there, and \(u - x \leqslant \| \varrho_q (y)\|_{\infty} + \|x\|_{\infty}\); similarly at a negative minimum. For \(q\) large, \(3 \rho_q \leqslant \frac{15}{16} r_q\), and (32.10) gives on \(\text{supp} \chi_q\)

\(\displaystyle |V_{q, N} - \bar{V}_{q, N} -\mathcal{C}_N (q) | \leqslant \varepsilon_q' : = C (q^{- 1 / 3 + 4 \delta} + q^{- 1 / 2} + q^{- 2 / 3 + 2 \delta} + q^{- 1 / 6 + 2 \delta}) \to 0, \) (32.12)

the last term coming from \(C \lambda^4 q^{- 1} \|y\|_2^2\) with \(\|y\|_{\infty} \leqslant 3 \rho_q\); it tends to zero as \(\delta < \frac{1}{12}\).

Step 3: total variation. \(\Phi^N_q\) has law \(\mu_{N, q} \propto \mathrm{e}^{- V_{q, N}} \gamma_{Q_q}\), with \(Q_q = C - C_q\). Let \(\hat{\nu}_{N, q} \propto \chi_q \mathrm{e}^{- \bar{V}_{q, N}} \gamma_{Q_q}\). On \(\text{supp} \chi_q\) the two unnormalised densities have ratio \(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathcal{C}_N (q) + \theta}\), \(| \theta | \leqslant \varepsilon_q'\); so the density of \(\hat{\nu}_{N, q}\) with respect to the measure \(\chi_q \mu_{N, q} / \int \chi_q \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} \mu_{N, q}\) lies in \([\mathrm{e}^{- 2 \varepsilon_q'}, \mathrm{e}^{2 \varepsilon_q'}]\). The constant cancels, whether or not it converges. Removing \(\chi_q\) costs at most \(2 p_q\) in total variation, so

\(\displaystyle d_{\text{TV}} (\mu_{N, q}, \hat{\nu}_{N, q}) \leqslant d_q : = 2 p_q + 2 (\mathrm{e}^{2 \varepsilon_q'} - 1) \to 0,\)

uniformly in \(N\), the convention being the total mass of the difference.

Step 4: the reference law converges at fixed \(q\). Let \(x_N \sim \gamma_{Q_{q, N}}\) be coupled through common Fourier Gaussians. The multiplier \((q - q_0) ((q_0 + c_k) (q + c_k))^{- 1} \lesssim (1 + |k|)^{- 4}\) gives, by the Kolmogorov criterion, \(\iota_N x_N \to x\) in probability in \(L^{\infty}\). \(\displaystyle \bar{V}_q (x) = U (y) + \frac{\mu_q}{2} \|y\|_2^2 + \frac{1}{2} \langle y, \Theta_q y \rangle + \tilde{h}_q (y) + \frac{1}{2} \langle D \bar{\Gamma}_q (y), x - y \rangle .\)

\(\displaystyle \langle y, \Theta y \rangle = \langle x, \Theta x \rangle + 2 \langle x, \Theta w \rangle + \langle w, \Theta w \rangle, \quad | \langle x, \Theta w \rangle | \leqslant C \|x\|_2 v_1 \|w\|_{H^1},\)

all bounded on the support except the second chaos \(\langle x, \Theta_q x \rangle\). Step 5: from the scale to the field. For smooth \(h\), as \(\Phi_q = J_q \Phi_{\infty} + C_q B_{q - q_0}\) and \(I - J_q = C_q K_{q_0}\),

\(\displaystyle \langle \iota_N (\Phi_{\infty} - \Phi_q), h \rangle = \langle \Phi_{\infty}, C_q K_{q_0} j_N h \rangle - \langle C_q B_{q - q_0}, j_N h \rangle .\)

By (17.6), the first has mean absolute value at most \(C \|C_q K_{q_0} j_N h\|_{\ast} \leqslant Cq^{- 1} \|h\|_{C^2}\), as \(C_q\) has norm \(q^{- 1}\) on each \(L^p\). The second has variance at most \(\|h\|_2^2 / q\). So for \(G = g (\langle \cdot, h_1 \rangle, \ldots, \langle \cdot, h_k \rangle)\) with \(g\) bounded and Lipschitz,

\(\displaystyle \text{limsup}_{N, N'} |\mathbb{E}G (\iota_N \Phi_{\infty}^N) -\mathbb{E}G (\iota_{N'} \Phi_{\infty}^{N'}) | \leqslant 2 \|g\|_{\infty} d_q + C_G q^{- 1 / 2} .\)

Let \(q \to \infty\). Cylinder functions separate measures on \(H^{- s}\) and Theorem 17.5 gives tightness, so the whole sequence converges. Since \(B^N\) is a Brownian motion independent of \(\Phi^N_{\infty}\), independence passes to the limit, with \(\Phi_q = (q - q_0) C_q \Phi + C_q B_{q - q_0}\).\(\Box\)

What this proof does not give. This argument identifies only the joint law of \(\Phi\) and the Polchinski process. It does not by itself give uniqueness for the system of Theorem 32.5(b) as an equation, nor that \(\psi_q\) is measurable for the filtration of the Polchinski process; these follow from the density in Lecture 7, Theorem 18.6(b) and Theorem 18.11. It does not cover \(H^{- s}\) with \(s\) near \(\frac{1}{2}\) for quartic moments.

32.7Symmetrised Girsanov comparison

This section gives a second proof of Steps 2–3 of Theorem 32.13, which replaces all of Section 32.5. It follows the idea of Gubinelli and Meyer for sine–Gordon [GM24], Proposition 4.9, where the limit law is written as an explicit density over an explicit SDE, and the backward remainder disappears from the identification. There the smoothed force \(Q_t F_t\) and the defect are bounded uniformly in the field [GM24], Proposition 3.13, there is a spatial cut-off, and the flow starts from zero. Here the defect is unbounded, and this older route uses the action only above \(q_{\ast}\), without the frozen extension of Section 12.4, so we use the symmetrised entropy at a fixed scale, which needs only expectations of the defect, and let the scale tend to infinity at the end. In particular neither the penalty, nor the sub- and supersolutions, nor the exponential Riccati bound, nor Lemma 32.9 is needed.

Let \(y = y_q (x)\) solve \(K_q y + D \bar{\Gamma}_q (y) = K_q x\), and let \(\bar{V}_q (x) = \bar{\Gamma}_q (y) + \frac{1}{2} \langle x - y, K_q (x - y) \rangle\) be the Moreau transform of the action. Let \(e_q = \partial_q \bar{\Gamma}_q - \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} [H_q - H_q (0)]\) be its defect, and let \(V_q\) be the exact potential, \(\mathrm{e}^{- V_q (x)} =\mathbb{E} \mathrm{e}^{- V_N \left( x + \int_q^{\infty} C_s \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W_s \right)}\), with force \(F_q = - DV_q\). By the envelope theorem and \(Dy = H_q K_q\),

\(\displaystyle \partial_q \bar{V} + \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} (C_q^2 D^2 \bar{V}) - \frac{1}{2} \|C_q D \bar{V} \|_2^2 = e_q \circ y + c (q), \quad c (q) = \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} (C_q - H_q (0)) . \) (32.13)

So \(\bar{V}\) solves the Polchinski equation up to its defect, and \(\hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} \bar{\Phi} = - C_s^2 D \bar{V}_s (\bar{\Phi}) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s + C_s \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W\) is an explicit flow, whose coordinate \(Y = y_s (\bar{\Phi}_s)\) solves \(\hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} Y = b (Y) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s + H (Y) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W\), \(b = - HDe\): indeed \(\hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} (K_s \bar{\Phi}) = Y \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s + \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W\), and one applies Itô's formula to \(K_s Y + D \bar{\Gamma}_s (Y) = K_s \bar{\Phi}\). It contains no \(Z\).

Lemma 32.14. (The defect on all fields) Let \(\pi_s\) be the pointwise projection on \([- r_s, r_s]\). For all \(y\) and \(s \geqslant q_{\ast}\),

\(\displaystyle |e_s (y) | \leqslant C \lambda^5 s^{- 3} \| \pi_s y\|_4^4 + C \lambda^4 s^{- 2} \| \pi_s y\|_2^2 + C \lambda^3 s^{- 5 / 2} \|t_s (y)\|_6^6 . \) (32.14)

Proof. On the ball \(e_s = e_s^{\text{o}}\), so \(|e_s (\pi_s y) | \leqslant C \lambda^5 s^{- 3} \| \pi_s y\|_4^4 + C \lambda^4 s^{- 2} \| \pi_s y\|_2^2\) by the zero scalar jets, the centred quadratic jet and (11.14). With \(z_{\theta} = \pi_s y + \theta (y - \pi_s y)\), \(e_s (y) - e_s (\pi_s y) = \int_0^1 \langle De_s (z_{\theta}), y - \pi_s y \rangle \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} \theta\), and \(y - \pi_s y\) is supported on the set \(\mathcal{A}\) where \(|y| > r_s\), with norm at most \(\|t_s \|_2\). Write \(De = D \kappa De^{\text{o}} \circ \kappa +\mathfrak{D}\). Then \(|De^{\text{o}} | \leqslant \textsf{S}_4 (e^{\text{o}}) (2 r_s)^3 / 6 + \|\mathcal{E}^c_s \|_{\text{row}} 2 r_s \leqslant C \lambda^{7 / 2} s^{- 3 / 2}\) and \(|\mathcal{A}|^{1 / 2} \leqslant r_s^{- 1} \|t_s \|_2\), so the first term contributes at most \(C \lambda^4 s^{- 2} \|t_s \|_2^2\). By (21.11), which bounds \(\mathfrak{D}\) before multiplication by \(H\) (the bound stated for \(b^{\text{l}} = - H\mathfrak{D}\) would not suffice, as \(H^{- 1}\) is unbounded), \(\|\mathfrak{D}_s (z)\|_2 \leqslant Cg_s (\|t_s (z)\|_2 + r_s^{- 1} \|z^2 \chi_s (z)\|_2)\); since \(|z_{\theta} | \leqslant |y|\) and \(z_{\theta}\) has size above \(r_s\) only on \(\mathcal{A}\), the second term contributes at most \(Cg_s (\|t_s \|_2^2 + r_s^{- 1} \|t_s \|_4^2 \|t_s \|_2)\). On the support of \(t_s\) we have \(|t_s | \geqslant r_s\), so \(\|t_s \|_2 \leqslant r_s^{- 2} \|t_s \|_6^3\) and \(\|t_s \|_4^2 \leqslant r_s^{- 1} \|t_s \|_6^3\). All these are at most \(Cg_s r_s^{- 4} \|t_s \|_6^6 = C \lambda^3 s^{- 5 / 2} \|t_s \|_6^6\).\(\Box\)

Fix \(\delta \in \left( 0, \frac{1}{12} \right)\), \(\rho_q = q^{5 / 12 + \delta}\), and \(\chi_q (x) = \tau (\|x\|_{\infty} / \rho_q)\), with \(\tau\) continuous, \(\tau = 1\) on \(\left[ 0, \frac{1}{2} \right]\), \(\tau = 0\) on \([1, \infty)\). Let \(\mu_{N, q} \propto \mathrm{e}^{- V_{q, N}} \gamma_{Q_q}\) be the law of \(\Phi_q^N\), and \(\hat{\nu}_{N, q} \propto \chi_q \mathrm{e}^{- \bar{V}_{q, N}} \gamma_{Q_q}\) the explicit law.

Proposition 32.15. (Comparison by Girsanov) For \(q\) large, so that \(p_q \leqslant \frac{1}{2}\) and \(r_q \geqslant 6 \rho_q\), there are \(d_q \to 0\), independent of \(N\), with \(\| \mu_{N, q} - \hat{\nu}_{N, q} \|_{\text{TV}} \leqslant d_q\). Precisely, with \(p_q = \sup_N \mathbb{P} (\| \Phi_q^N \|_{\infty} > \rho_q / 2)\) and \(\sigma_q = \lambda^5 q^{- 2} \rho_q^4 + \lambda^4 q^{- 1} \rho_q^2 + \lambda^4 q^{- 1 / 2} + \lambda^2 q^{- 1}\), one has \(d_q \leqslant C (p_q + \sigma_q^{1 / 2})\), and \(p_q \to 0\) by Step 1 of Theorem 32.13.

Proof. The two path laws. On \([q, \infty)\) let \(\mathbb{P}\) be the exact law, conditioned softly: initial law \(\chi_q \mu_{N, q} / \int \chi_q \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} \mu_{N, q}\), drift \(C_s^2 F_s\). Let \(\mathbb{R}\) be the explicit one: initial law \(\hat{\nu}_{N, q}\), drift \(- C_s^2 D \bar{V}_s\). Both are absolutely continuous with respect to the free flow \(\hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} \xi = C_s \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W\) with initial law \(\gamma_{Q_q}\). On \([q, T]\) the first has density proportional to \(\chi_q (\xi_q) \mathrm{e}^{- V_T (\xi_T)}\), since \(\mathrm{e}^{- V}\) is harmonic. By (32.13) and Itô's formula, the second has density proportional to

\(\displaystyle \chi_q (\xi_q) \mathrm{e}^{- \bar{V}_q (\xi_q)} \exp (- \bar{V}_T (\xi_T) + \bar{V}_q (\xi_q) + \int_q^T (e_s (y_s (\xi_s)) + c (s)) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s) .\)

This is a true martingale density: the drift is \(- C_s (x - y_s (x))\), Lipschitz in \(x\) at fixed \(N\) since \(Dy = HK\). The factors at \(\xi_q\) cancel: this is why no estimate at the initial scale is needed. So \(\log \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} \mathbb{P}/ \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} \mathbb{R}= - \Delta_T (\xi_T) - \int_q^T e_s (Y_s) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s + \text{const}\), with \(\Delta_T = V_T - \bar{V}_T\). The constant includes \(\int c\) and the normalisations, which diverge with \(N\); it does not matter, since in the symmetrised relative entropy it cancels:

\(\displaystyle \text{Ent} (\mathbb{P}|\mathbb{R}) + \text{Ent} (\mathbb{R}|\mathbb{P}) =\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{R}} \left[ \Delta_T + \int_q^T e_s (Y_s) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s \right] -\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}} \left[ \Delta_T + \int_q^T e_s (Y_s) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s \right] . \) (32.15)

At fixed \(N\): \(| \mu_T - \mu_N | = O_N (T^{- 1})\), \(\| \Theta_T \|_{\text{row}} = O_N (T^{- 1 / 2})\), \(\textsf{S}_4 (h_T) = O_N (T^{- 2})\) and \(|y - x| \leqslant C_N T^{- 1} (\|x\|_{\infty} +\|x\|_{\infty}^3)\) give \(| \bar{V}_T - V_N | = O_N (T^{- 1 / 4})\) on \(\|x\|_{\infty} \leqslant T^{1 / 8}\), and Gaussian integration, the remaining variance per site being \(O_N (T^{- 1})\), gives \(|V_T - V_N | = O_N (T^{- 1 / 8})\) there. For all \(x\), \(| \Delta_T (x) | \leqslant C_N (1 +\|x\|_{\infty}^4)\) uniformly in \(T\): \(\inf V_N \leqslant V_T (x) \leqslant C_N (1 +\|x\|_{\infty}^4)\) by Jensen, and \(- C_N \leqslant \bar{V}_T (x) \leqslant \bar{\Gamma}_T (x) \leqslant C_N (1 +\|x\|_{\infty}^4)\), as \(U\) dominates \(\tilde{h}_T\). The moments of \(\xi_T\) are uniform in \(T\): under \(\mathbb{P}\) since \(\Phi_T = J_T \Phi_{\infty} + C_T B_{T - q_0}\); under \(\mathbb{R}\) by the Ito computation below with \(\int^{\infty} a_2 \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s < \infty\) at fixed \(N\), and \(x = Y + \varrho_T (Y) + \frac{\lambda}{3} C_T Y^3\). So \(\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}} \Delta_T, \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{R}} \Delta_T \to 0\). With the bounds below, \(\log \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} \mathbb{P}/ \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} \mathbb{R}\) is integrable under both laws at each \(T\), so both entropies are finite and (32.15) is justified. By Pinsker's inequality the squared total variation at the initial scale is at most the sum of the two expectations of \(\int_q^{\infty} |e_s (Y_s) | \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s\), and removing \(\chi_q\) costs at most \(2 p_q\). It remains to bound these expectations, by (32.14).

Under the exact law. \(\int_q^{\infty} \lambda^3 s^{- 5 / 2} \|t_s \|_6^6 \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s \leqslant \lambda^2 q^{- 1} J_{\infty}\). Further \(\| \pi_s Y_s \|_4^4 \leqslant r_s^2 \|Y_s \|_2^2\), and \(\mathbb{E} \|Y_s \|_2^2 \leqslant C (1 + s^{1 / 2})\) by Itô's formula for \(\|Y_s \|_2^2\) along the forward equation of Theorem 10.6, from \(q_{\ast}\), with the moments of \(Y_{q_{\ast}}\) and \(\mathbb{E} \|Z_u \|_2^2 \leqslant Cu^{- 5 / 2}\) of Theorem 16.2, \(\|H\| \leqslant C / u\), the linear bound on \(b\) and \(\text{Tr} H^2 \leqslant Cu^{- 1 / 2}\). So the first two terms of (32.14), both at most \(C \lambda^4 s^{- 2} \|Y_s \|_2^2\), integrate in expectation to at most \(C \lambda^4 q^{- 1 / 2}\). The conditioning costs at most a factor \(2\).

Under the explicit law. Here \(Y_q = y\) with \(\|y\|_{\infty} \leqslant 3 \rho_q\) by the maximum principle: with \(u = y + \varrho_q (y)\), \(K_q (u - x) + \frac{\lambda}{3} y^3 = 0\), and at a positive maximum of \(u - x\) we have \(y < 0\), so \(u - x \leqslant \| \varrho_q (y)\|_{\infty} + \|x\|_{\infty}\); similarly at a negative minimum. Thus \(\|u\|_{\infty} \leqslant 2 \|x\|_{\infty} + \varepsilon_q \|y\|_{\infty}\), with \(\varepsilon_q\) the contraction constant of (12.5), and \(\|y\|_{\infty} \leqslant 3 \|x\|_{\infty}\) for \(q\) large. Let \(\xi_s = Y_s - y\), which is a different \(\xi\) from the free flow. By the global linear bound of Proposition 12.2, \(\|b (Y)\|_p \leqslant C \lambda s^{- 3 / 2} \|Y\|_p\) for every \(p\) (it follows from the exact gradient of \(e\), without the ledger), and \(\text{diag} H^2 \leqslant Cs^{- 1 / 2}\), Itô's formula gives, for \(p = 2\) and \(p = 12\), \(\mathbb{E} \| \xi_s \|_p^p \leqslant C (s^{p / 4} + (g_q \rho_q)^p)\). So, as \(\| \pi_s Y\|_4^4 \leqslant 8 \|y\|_4^4 + 32 r_s^2 \| \xi \|_2^2\), and \(\| \pi_s Y\|_2^2 \leqslant 2 \|y\|_2^2 + 2 \| \xi \|_2^2\), the first two terms of (32.14) integrate to at most \(C \lambda^5 q^{- 2} \rho_q^4 + C \lambda^4 q^{- 1} \rho_q^2 + C \lambda^4 q^{- 1 / 2}\). For large \(q\), \(r_s \geqslant r_q \geqslant 6 \rho_q\), so where \(|Y| > r_s\) we have \(| \xi | > r_s / 2\) and \(|Y| \leqslant 2 | \xi |\). Thus \(\|t_s \|_6^6 \leqslant Cr_s^{- 6} \| \xi_s \|_{12}^{12}\), and the third term integrates to at most \(C \lambda^6 q^{- 3 / 2}\).\(\Box\)

The bound on \(\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\) is where UV stability enters. So the backward equation is used only through Theorem 16.2, for \(J_{\infty}\) and \(\mathbb{E} \|Z_s \|_2^2\); under \(\mathbb{R}\) the comparison has no \(Z\), no conditional expectation and no exponential moment. What is lost is the pointwise bound on \(V_q - \bar{V}_q\) on typical fields which a Feynman–Kac comparison gives: here one gets total variation at a fixed scale, and that is all the theorem needs.

Remark 32.16. (The backward error alone) The drifts differ by \(C_s^2 (F_s - \bar{f}_s)\) with \(\bar{f} = - D \bar{V}\), and \(C_s (F_s - \bar{f}_s) = Z_s\). So, from the same initial law at scale \(q\), Girsanov's theorem gives directly that the relative entropy of the exact path law with respect to the explicit flow is \(\frac{1}{2} \int_q^{\infty} \mathbb{E} \|Z_s \|_2^2 \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s \leqslant Cq^{- 3 / 2}\), hence total variation \(\leqslant Cq^{- 3 / 4}\). This replaces the backward error by a change of measure, but it does not identify the limit: it reduces the terminal law to the law at scale \(q\), which is the unknown, since the flow does not forget the low modes. In [GM24] this does not arise, as the flow starts from a deterministic point; the same holds in Lecture 7, where the frozen action starts the flow at \(q_0\) from zero (Theorem 18.5). The symmetrised entropy above compares the laws at scale \(q\) as well, through the cancellation of the factors at \(\xi_q\).

With Proposition 32.15, Steps 1, 4 and 5 of Theorem 32.13 conclude the proof as before: it uses Theorem 16.2, Proposition 14.4, Lemma 14.5, (11.14), Lemma 12.1, and Proposition 12.2 with its ledger. On this route the condition \(q_{\ast} \geqslant q_A\) of Theorem 32.13 is not needed.
