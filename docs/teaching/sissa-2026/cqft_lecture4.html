Abstract

Building on the scale decomposition and the stochastic observables of Lecture 3, we introduce interactions through the Polchinski equation for the effective potential. Its probabilistic counterpart is a forward–backward stochastic differential equation (FBSDE) for the field seen at increasing resolution, whose effective force is a martingale. Writing the FBSDE in the coordinate given by the conditional mean of the terminal field removes its drift and integrates the transport part of the force flow exactly. In the gradient case the remaining equation for the force is the Wetterich equation for the effective average action, whose propagator is the conditional covariance. We then discuss the variational structure of the flow (Boué–Dupuis formula and Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation) in finite and infinite volume. The additional material covers the algebraic and perturbative structure of the flow (semigroup, flatness, counterterms for \(\Phi^4_3\)), and the Dyson–Schwinger equations at every scale. The treatment is formal: we compute as if all objects were smooth and integrable, and we indicate where the real difficulties lie.

Introduction

In Lecture 3 we decomposed the Gaussian free field along a resolution parameter and took as primary objects the stochastic observables: martingales in the filtration of finite-resolution measurements [BCG26]. In this lecture we introduce interactions. The effective potentials solve the Polchinski equation, and the interacting field seen at increasing resolution solves a forward–backward stochastic differential equation whose force is itself an observable. See also [Pol84, BK87, Sal99, BBD24] for the renormalisation group side and [BG20, BG24] for the variational side.

Structure.
Part I (Sections 7–9) is the core of the lecture: the Polchinski equation and its FBSDE, the FBSDE in force coordinates and the Wetterich equation, and the variational structure. Part II (Sections 10 and 11) is additional material, referred to from Part I where needed.

What is formal.
Rigorous statements require a finite-dimensional setting: a finite lattice, or a Fourier cutoff on the torus, together with a finite terminal resolution \(T\). There, all the identities below are theorems under mild growth assumptions on the interaction, and their proofs are the computations we give. The hard problems are the limits \(T \to \infty\) (ultraviolet) and infinite volume (infrared), together with the locality of the limiting objects. We mark with (formal) the statements that we only derive at this level.

Conventions.
As in Lectures 1–3: \(\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{T}^d_L\) or \(\mathbb{R}^d\), \(A = m^2 - \Delta\), \(C = A^{- 1}\) with kernel \(G\), \(\mu_m\) the GFF, \(\langle f, g \rangle = \int fg\) for real functions. For a functional \(F\) on smooth fields, \(DF (\varphi) \in \mathcal{S}' (\mathcal{M})\) and \(D^2 F (\varphi)\) are its first and second derivatives, \(D_h F = \langle DF, h \rangle\). \(H_n (a ; c)\) are the Hermite polynomials of variance \(c\), and \(\mu^Q\) is the centred Gaussian measure with covariance \(Q\).

7Interactions: the Polchinski equation and FBSDEs

7.1Effective potentials

Fix a finite volume \(\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{T}^d_L\) and a terminal resolution \(T < \infty\), which plays the role of an ultraviolet cutoff: the reference field \(X_T \sim \mu^{Q_T}\) is smooth. Let \(V_T\) be an interaction, a functional of smooth fields bounded below. Our main example is

\(\displaystyle V_T (\varphi) = \int_{\mathbb{T}^d_L} \left[ \lambda H_4 (\varphi (x) ; c_T) + \gamma_T \hspace{0.17em} H_2 (\varphi (x) ; c_T) \right] \mathrm{d} x,\) (7.1)

with a mass counterterm \(\gamma_T\) to be fixed in Section 10.2 (\(\gamma_T = 0\) for \(d = 2\)). The interacting measure at cutoff \(T\) is

\(\displaystyle \nu_T (\mathrm{d} \varphi) = Z_T^{- 1} \mathrm{e}^{- V_T (\varphi)} \mu^{Q_T} (\mathrm{d} \varphi) .\) (7.2)

Definition 7.1. The effective potential at resolution \(t \in [0, T]\) is

\(\displaystyle \mathrm{e}^{- V_t (\varphi)} := \int \mathrm{e}^{- V_T (\varphi + \zeta)} \hspace{0.17em} \mu^{Q_{T, t}} (\mathrm{d} \zeta) .\) (7.3)

\(V_t\) is the interaction felt by the field at resolution \(t\) once the fluctuations between \(t\) and \(T\) have been integrated out: it is the Wilson effective action. At \(t = 0\), \(V_0 (0) = - \log Z_T\) is the free energy. Here is the first key fact.

Proposition 7.2. (Marginals) Let \(\mathbb{P}\) be defined by \(\mathrm{d} \mathbb{P}/ \mathrm{d} \mathbb{P}_0 = Z_T^{- 1} \mathrm{e}^{- V_T (X_T)}\), where \(\mathbb{P}_0\) is the law of the Gaussian process \(X\). Then \(\text{Law}_{\mathbb{P}} (X_T) = \nu_T\), and for every \(t \leq T\)

\(\displaystyle \text{Law}_{\mathbb{P}} (X_t) = \nu_t, \qquad \nu_t (\mathrm{d} \varphi) := Z_T^{- 1} \mathrm{e}^{- V_t (\varphi)} \mu^{Q_t} (\mathrm{d} \varphi) .\) (7.4)

Moreover, the conditional law of \(X_T\) given \(\mathcal{F}_t\) is

\(\displaystyle \mu_t^{X_t} (\mathrm{d} \varphi), \qquad \mu_t^{\phi} (G) := \mathrm{e}^{V_t (\phi)} \int G (\phi + \zeta) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{e}^{- V_T (\phi + \zeta)} \hspace{0.17em} \mu^{Q_{T, t}} (\mathrm{d} \zeta) .\) (7.5)

Proof. Under \(\mathbb{P}_0\), \(X_T = X_t + (X_T - X_t)\) with an independent increment of law \(\mu^{Q_{T, t}}\). Hence \(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}} [G (X_t)] = Z_T^{- 1} \mathbb{E}_0 [G (X_t) \mathrm{e}^{- V_T (X_T)}] = Z_T^{- 1} \mathbb{E}_0 [G (X_t) \mathrm{e}^{- V_t (X_t)}]\). The conditional law follows in the same way.\(\Box\)

So at every resolution the field is again a Gibbs measure, with reference covariance \(Q_t\) and potential \(V_t\). The measure-valued martingale \((\mu_t^{X_t})_t\) goes from \(\nu_T\) at \(t = 0\) to \(\delta_{X_T}\) at \(t = T\): it is a stochastic localisation of \(\nu_T\) in the sense of Eldan [Eld23], adapted to the covariance profile [BBD24, BCG26].

Theorem 7.3. (Polchinski equation [Pol84]) (formal) \(V_t\) solves the backward Hamilton–Jacobi equation

\(\displaystyle \partial_t V_t = \tfrac{1}{2} \langle DV_t, \dot{Q}_t \hspace{0.17em} DV_t \rangle - \tfrac{1}{2} \text{Tr} \left( \dot{Q}_t \hspace{0.17em} D^2 V_t \right), \qquad t \in [0, T],\) (7.6)

with terminal condition \(V_T\).

Proof. \(u_t := \mathrm{e}^{- V_t} = \mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2} \Delta_{Q_{T, t}}} u_T\) is a Gaussian convolution, where \(\Delta_Q := \text{Tr} (QD^2)\). Since \(\partial_t Q_{T, t} = - \dot{Q}_t\), it solves the backward heat equation \(\partial_t u_t = - \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} (\dot{Q}_t D^2 u_t)\). Insert \(D^2 u = (DV \otimes DV - D^2 V) u\) and divide by \(- u\).\(\Box\)

The nonlinear term generates new, generally nonlocal, interactions (trees), and the trace term contracts pairs of fields with the covariance of the shell \([t, t + \mathrm{d} t]\) (loops). The substitution \(u = \mathrm{e}^{- V}\), which linearises the equation, is a Cole–Hopf transform: we come back to this in Section 7.4, and in Section 10.

7.2The Polchinski process

Theorem 7.4. (FBSDE for the interacting field) (formal) Under \(\mathbb{P}\) the process \(\Phi_t := X_t\), the interacting field at resolution \(t\), solves

\(\displaystyle \mathrm{d} \Phi_t = \dot{Q}_t \hspace{0.17em} F_t \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} t + \dot{Q}_t^{1 / 2} \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W^{\mathbb{P}}_t, \qquad F_t = - DV_t (\Phi_t) =\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}_t [- DV_T (\Phi_T)],\) (7.7)

with \(\Phi_0 = 0\) and a \(\mathbb{P}\)-cylindrical Brownian motion \(W^{\mathbb{P}}\). In particular the effective force \((F_t)_{t \leq T}\) is a \(\mathbb{P}\)-martingale: an observable field of the interacting theory.

Proof. The density process is \(M_t := \mathbb{E}_0 [\mathrm{d} \mathbb{P}/ \mathrm{d} \mathbb{P}_0 |\mathcal{F}_t] = Z_T^{- 1} \mathrm{e}^{- V_t (X_t)}\), by Proposition 7.2. By Itô's formula and (7.6), its drift vanishes and \(\mathrm{d} M_t = - M_t \langle DV_t (X_t), \dot{Q}_t^{1 / 2} \mathrm{d} W_t \rangle\). By Girsanov's theorem, \(W^{\mathbb{P}}_t = W_t + \int_0^t \dot{Q}_s^{1 / 2} DV_s (X_s) \mathrm{d} s\) is a \(\mathbb{P}\)-Brownian motion, which is (7.7). Differentiating (7.3) gives \(DV_t (\phi) = \mu_t^{\phi} (DV_T)\), which is the second identity. Alternatively, Itô's formula for \(DV_t (\Phi_t)\), together with the derivative of (7.6), shows that its drift vanishes. Here one uses that \(D^2 V_t\) is symmetric.\(\Box\)

Read as an equation for an unknown pair \((\Phi, F)\), (7.7) is a forward–backward system [MY99]. The field is driven forward in resolution by a force that is determined backward, as the conditional expectation of a functional of the terminal field:

\(\displaystyle \Phi_t = \int_0^t \dot{Q}_s F_s \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s + \int_0^t \dot{Q}_s^{1 / 2} \mathrm{d} W_s, \qquad F_t =\mathbb{E}_t [- DV_T (\Phi_T)], \qquad t \in [0, T] .\) (7.8)

Here and below we drop the superscript and write \(W\) for the Brownian motion driving the interacting field. The Polchinski equation is not needed to state (7.8), and \(\nu_T = \text{Law} (\Phi_T)\) is recovered from the solution. This is the starting point of FBSDE constructions of Euclidean fields (Figure 7.1): one solves (7.8) with renormalised \(V_T\), with estimates uniform in \(T\) and in the volume, and takes the limit. The field coordinate \(\Phi\) is not an observable, only a semimartingale. Its martingale part is the Gaussian noise, and the observables are built from it and from \(F\).

Remark 7.5. (Closed versus local) At finite \(T\) the force is a closed observable, \(F_t =\mathbb{E}_t [F_T]\). For \(\Phi^4\), \(F_T (x) = - 4 \lambda H_3 (\Phi_T (x) ; c_T) - 2 \gamma_T H_1 (\Phi_T (x) ; c_T)\) is local. As \(T \to \infty\) one expects the force to converge as an observable. In \(d = 2\) the limit is closed, \(F_{\infty} = - 4 \lambda : \Phi_{\infty}^3\):. In \(d = 3\) it is expected not to be. If it were, \(F_t =\mathbb{E}_t [F_{\infty}]\) with \(F_{\infty} \in L^1\), the DS equation of Section 11 would pass to the limit (\(T \to \infty\), then \(t \to \infty\)), with \(F_{\infty}\) paired only with functionals of \(\Phi_{\infty}\). Conditioning on \(\sigma (\Phi_{\infty})\) would then give a logarithmic derivative of the \(\Phi^4_3\) measure that is an integrable function of the field, \(\langle Ah, \Phi_{\infty} \rangle -\mathbb{E} [F_{\infty} (h) | \Phi_{\infty}]\). This is not expected: the regularised logarithmic derivatives do not converge in \(L^2\) [ALZ06]. The limiting force would then be an observable in the proper sense, a compatible family with no terminal representative. Locality of the limiting force is the crux of the construction [BCG26].

7.3Observables of the interacting theory

For a functional \(G\) of the terminal field, the observable \(O_t =\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}_t [G (\Phi_T)]\) is \(O_t = G_t (\Phi_t)\), with

\(\displaystyle G_t (\phi) = \mu_t^{\phi} (G) = \mathrm{e}^{V_t (\phi)} \int G (\phi + \zeta) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{e}^{- V_T (\phi + \zeta)} \mu^{Q_{T, t}} (\mathrm{d} \zeta) .\) (7.9)

Formally, \(G_t\) solves the backward Kolmogorov equation of the interacting dynamics,

\(\displaystyle \partial_t G_t + \tfrac{1}{2} \text{Tr} (\dot{Q}_t D^2 G_t) - \langle \dot{Q}_t DV_t, DG_t \rangle = 0, \qquad G_T = G,\) (7.10)

which is the Doob transform of the Gaussian heat equation by \(\mathrm{e}^{- V_t}\) (Exercise 7.3). Conversely, any solution of (7.10) yields an observable \(G_t (\Phi_t)\); at finite \(T\) it is closed, but as \(T \to \infty\) it need not be. The force is the case \(G = - DV_T\), and is special because it also drives the dynamics.

Tangent observables.
Let \(V_T^{\theta}\) depend on a coupling \(\theta\), and let \(U_t := \partial_{\theta} V_t^{\theta}\). Differentiating (7.3) gives \(U_t (\Phi_t) =\mathbb{E}^{\theta}_t [\partial_{\theta} V_T^{\theta} (\Phi_T)]\), an observable, and

\(\displaystyle \partial_{\theta} \mathbb{E}^{\nu^{\theta}_T} [G] = - \text{Cov}^{\nu_T^{\theta}} (G, \partial_{\theta} V^{\theta}_T) .\) (7.11)

Thus the tangent observables, such as the running \(: \varphi^4\): for \(\theta = \lambda\) (together with the derivative of the counterterm \(\partial_{\lambda} \gamma_T \int : \varphi^2\):), describe the response of the theory to a change of coupling. They are the observables conjugate to the coordinates of theory space.

7.4The force and a Burgers equation

Differentiating (7.6), the effective force \(F_t = - DV_t\), now as a function of the field, solves

\(\displaystyle \partial_t F_t = - DF_t [\dot{Q}_t F_t] - \tfrac{1}{2} \text{Tr} (\dot{Q}_t D^2 F_t), \qquad F_T = - DV_T,\) (7.12)

an infinite-dimensional, backward, viscous Burgers equation. The Cole–Hopf transform \(F = D \log u\) linearises it, which is the statement that \(u = \mathrm{e}^{- V}\) solves a heat equation. Two remarks on (7.12):

  1. it uses only the force, which is a local quantity (intensive), while \(V_t\) is extensive, of order of the volume. This matters in infinite volume (Section 9.2);

  2. the Burgers flows for different covariances commute, and the curvature vanishes, when \(DF_t\) is symmetric, i.e. when \(F_t\) is a gradient [BCG26]. For a non-gradient force, as for the general Wilson–Itô diffusions of [BCG26], the flows do not commute.

8The FBSDE in force coordinates: the Wetterich equation

In this section the forward–backward system (7.8) is the primary object. We look for the coordinates in which it is simplest. They remove the predictable part of the forward dynamics, and they integrate exactly the bilinear (tree) part of the force flow (7.12). In the gradient case the resulting equation for the force is the Wetterich equation for the effective average action [Wet93, BTW02]. Throughout,

\(\displaystyle S_t := Q_T - Q_t, \qquad \dot{S}_t = - \dot{Q}_t, \qquad S_T = 0,\)

is the covariance still unresolved at resolution \(t\), and \((\Phi, F)\) solves (7.8) with a martingale force \(F_t = F_t (\Phi_t)\), \(\mathrm{d} F_t = \Upsilon_t \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W_t\).

8.1State and costate

Pontryagin's maximum principle.
Consider first a deterministic problem: minimise the cost \(J (u) = g (x_T) + \int_t^T \ell (x_s, u_s) \mathrm{d} s\) over controls \(u\) on \([t, T]\), where the state solves \(\dot{x}_s = b (x_s, u_s)\), \(x_t = x\). With the Hamiltonian

\(\displaystyle H (x, p, u) := \langle p, b (x, u) \rangle - \ell (x, u),\)

Pontryagin's principle [PBGM62] says that along an optimal pair \((x, u)\) there is a costate \(p\) such that

\(\displaystyle \dot{x}_s = \partial_p H, \qquad \dot{p}_s = - \partial_x H, \qquad p_T = - Dg (x_T), \qquad u_s \in \arg \max_v H (x_s, p_s, v) .\)

The state runs forward from its initial value, the costate backward from its terminal value: a two-point boundary value problem. In the smooth case the principle is the first-order condition. If \(\xi = \partial_{\varepsilon} x\) is the variation of the state under \(u \to u + \varepsilon v\), then \(\dot{\xi} = \partial_x b \xi + \partial_u bv\), \(\xi_t = 0\), and the costate equation is exactly what makes \(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} s} \langle p, \xi \rangle = \langle \partial_x \ell, \xi \rangle + \langle p, \partial_u bv \rangle\). Integrating over \([t, T]\) and using the terminal condition,

\(\displaystyle \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} \varepsilon} J (u + \varepsilon v) |_{\varepsilon = 0} = - \int_t^T \langle \partial_u H (x_s, p_s, u_s), v_s \rangle \mathrm{d} s.\)

So the costate is the Lagrange multiplier of the constraint \(\dot{x} = b\), and an optimal control is a critical point of the Hamiltonian.

The costate is a gradient.
Let \(V (t, x) = \min_u J (u)\) be the value function. Comparing the cost from \((t, x)\) with the cost from \((t + \mathrm{d} t, x + b \mathrm{d} t)\) gives the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation

\(\displaystyle \partial_t V = H_{\ast} (x, - DV), \qquad H_{\ast} (x, p) := \max_u H (x, p, u), \qquad V (T, \cdot) = g.\)

Its characteristics are Hamilton's equations for \(H_{\ast}\), and along them \(p_s = - DV (s, x_s)\): the costate is the sensitivity of the optimal cost to a displacement of the state, i.e. the force that the future exerts on the present.

The Hamiltonian of the field.
In the problem of Theorem 9.1 the state is the field, \(b (x, u) = \dot{Q}_s^{1 / 2} u\), \(\ell = \frac{1}{2} \|u\|^2\) and \(g = V_T\). Then \(H (p, u) = \langle p, \dot{Q}_s^{1 / 2} u \rangle - \frac{1}{2} \|u\|^2\) is maximal at \(u = \dot{Q}_s^{1 / 2} p\), and

\(\displaystyle H_{\ast} (p) = \frac{1}{2} \langle p, \dot{Q}_s p \rangle\)

is the Hamiltonian of a free particle with mass matrix \(\dot{Q}_s^{- 1}\): it does not depend on the state. Hence \(\dot{p} = 0\) and \(\dot{x} = \dot{Q}_s p\): the costate is conserved and the state moves along the shear (8.1). All the interaction sits in the terminal condition \(p = - DV_T (x + S_t p)\), an implicit equation for the momentum, solved by a sum of tree graphs (Exercise 8.2). The corresponding Hamilton–Jacobi equation \(\partial_t V = \frac{1}{2} \langle DV, \dot{Q}_t DV \rangle\) is the Polchinski equation (7.6) without its trace term.

With noise.
For a controlled diffusion \(\mathrm{d} X = b (X, u) \mathrm{d} s + \sigma \mathrm{d} W\), with \(\sigma\) independent of the control, the stochastic maximum principle [Bis73, Pen90, YZ99] keeps the Hamiltonian and the maximisation, but the costate becomes an adapted process which solves a backward stochastic differential equation,

\(\displaystyle \mathrm{d} p_s = - \partial_x H \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s + \Upsilon_s \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W_s, \qquad p_T = - Dg (X_T) .\)

The martingale term \(\Upsilon \mathrm{d} W\) is the price of adaptedness: the costate may not anticipate the noise. For the field \(\partial_x H = 0\), so the costate is a martingale, \(p_t =\mathbb{E}_t [- DV_T (X_T)]\), and since the value function is the effective potential (Theorem 9.1), \(p_t = - DV_t (X_t)\). The noise adds the Itô term to the Hamilton–Jacobi equation, which becomes the Polchinski equation (7.6): its quadratic, Hamilton–Jacobi part generates the trees, and the trace term generates the loops. The forward–backward system (7.8) is the stochastic Hamiltonian system of this problem.

The FBSDE is the stochastic Pontryagin system of the control problem of Theorem 9.1. The controlled field \(\mathrm{d} X = \dot{Q}_t^{1 / 2} (u \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} t + \mathrm{d} W)\) has Hamiltonian \(\langle p, \dot{Q}_t^{1 / 2} u \rangle - \frac{1}{2} \|u\|^2\), which does not depend on the state. By the stochastic maximum principle (Exercise 8.1), the adjoint process \(p\) has no drift, \(\mathrm{d} p_t = \Upsilon_t \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W_t\), with terminal value \(p_T = - DV_T (X_T)\), and the optimal control is \(u_t = \dot{Q}_t^{1 / 2} p_t\). Thus

the field \(\Phi\) is the state, and the force \(F\) is the costate.

The function \(F_t (\cdot)\) with \(F_t = F_t (\Phi_t)\) is the decoupling field of the FBSDE, and the Burgers equation (7.12) is its quasilinear PDE; \(\Upsilon_t = DF_t (\Phi_t) \dot{Q}_t^{1 / 2}\) is the martingale representation integrand of the costate. The two terms of (7.12) have distinct origins. The transport term \(- DF [\dot{Q} F]\) comes from the drift of the state, and generates the tree diagrams. The diffusion term \(- \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} (\dot{Q} D^2 F)\) is the Itô correction, and generates the loops.

Without noise the state–costate pair follows the Hamiltonian flow of the free Hamiltonian \(h_s (p) = \frac{1}{2} \langle p, \dot{Q}_s p \rangle\): \(\dot{p} = 0\) and \(\dot{x} = \dot{Q}_s p\). From \(t\) to \(T\) this flow is the linear shear

\(\displaystyle \Sigma_t : (x, p) \longmapsto (x + S_t p, \hspace{0.27em} p) .\) (8.1)

Its trajectories are the characteristics of the transport part of (7.12) (Exercise 8.2).

8.2Removing the predictable drift

The drift \(\dot{Q}_t F_t\) of the state is predictable, and the costate is a martingale. Hence the drift that remains to be accumulated on \([t, T]\) is predicted, at resolution \(t\), by transporting the current state with the current costate along the free flow (8.1).

Proposition 8.1. (The forecast) (formal) The conditional mean of the terminal field is

\(\displaystyle \psi_t := \mathbb{E}_t [\Phi_T] = \Phi_t + S_t F_t,\) (8.2)

i.e. \((\psi_t, F_t) = \Sigma_t (\Phi_t, F_t)\). It is a martingale, with

\(\displaystyle \mathrm{d} \psi_t = \dot{Q}_t^{1 / 2} \mathrm{d} W_t + S_t \Upsilon_t \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W_t = (\mathbb{1} + S_t DF_t (\Phi_t)) \dot{Q}_t^{1 / 2} \mathrm{d} W_t .\) (8.3)

Proof. Integrate the forward equation from \(t\) to \(T\) and condition on \(\mathcal{F}_t\): since \(F\) is a martingale, \(\mathbb{E}_t \int_t^T \dot{Q}_s F_s \mathrm{d} s = S_t F_t\). For the differential, the drift \(\dot{Q}_t F_t\) of \(\Phi\) cancels against \(\dot{S}_t F_t\), and \(S_t \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} F_t = S_t \Upsilon_t \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W_t\).\(\Box\)

So \(\psi\) is a closed observable, the field observable of [BCG26]. The field splits as

\(\displaystyle \Phi_t = \psi_t - S_t F_t :\) (8.4)

a martingale, minus the drift still expected on the unresolved scales, which vanishes at \(t = T\). The noise of \(\psi\) in (8.3) has two sources: the fresh fluctuation of the shell, \(\dot{Q}_t^{1 / 2} \mathrm{d} W_t\), and the revision of the forecast of the remaining drift, \(S_t \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} F_t\). The coupling between the forward and the backward equations, which was in the drift of \(\Phi\), now sits in the volatility of \(\psi\). The coordinate \(\psi\) is the interaction picture with respect to the noiseless (classical) part of the dynamics.

8.3The FBSDE in the forecast coordinate

Assume that the map \(x \mapsto \psi = x + S_t F_t (x)\) is invertible, and write its inverse as

\(\displaystyle x = \psi + S_t \mathscr{F}_t (\psi), \qquad \mathscr{F}_t (\psi) := - F_t (x),\) (8.5)

so that \(F_t = - \mathscr{F}_t (\psi_t)\): the costate is now a function of the forecast. Introduce the response and the regulator

\(\displaystyle H_t (\psi) := (S_t^{- 1} + D \mathscr{F}_t (\psi))^{- 1}, \qquad \mathscr{R}_t := S_t^{- 1} - Q_T^{- 1}, \qquad \dot{\mathscr{R}}_t = S_t^{- 1} \dot{Q}_t S_t^{- 1} \geq 0.\) (8.6)

Differentiating the two maps \(x \leftrightarrow \psi\) gives \(\mathbb{1} + S_t DF_t (x) = (\mathbb{1} + S_t D \mathscr{F}_t (\psi))^{- 1} = H_t (\psi) S_t^{- 1}\).

Theorem 8.2. (FBSDE in force coordinates) (formal) In the coordinates \((\psi, \mathscr{F})\), the system (7.8) becomes

\(\displaystyle \mathrm{d} \psi_t = H_t (\psi_t) \hspace{0.17em} S_t^{- 1} \dot{Q}_t^{1 / 2} \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} W_t, \qquad \mathscr{F}_t (\psi_t) =\mathbb{E}_t [DV_T (\psi_T)],\) (8.7)

with \(\psi_0 = S_0 F_0 =\mathbb{E} [\Phi_T]\) and \(\psi_T = \Phi_T\). The forward equation has no drift, and its covariance is \(\mathcal{C}_t := H_t \dot{\mathscr{R}}_t H_t^{\ast}\). The decoupling field \(\mathscr{F}\) solves the pure diffusion equation

\(\displaystyle \partial_t \mathscr{F}_t + \tfrac{1}{2} \hspace{0.17em} \text{Tr} \left( \mathcal{C}_t \hspace{0.17em} D^2 \mathscr{F}_t \right) = 0, \qquad \mathscr{F}_T = DV_T .\) (8.8)

Proof. The forward equation is (8.3) with \(\mathbb{1} + S_t DF_t = H_t S_t^{- 1}\), and its covariance is

\(\displaystyle H_t S_t^{- 1} \dot{Q}_t S_t^{- 1} H_t^{\ast} = H_t \dot{\mathscr{R}}_t H_t^{\ast} .\)

The backward equation is \(F_t =\mathbb{E}_t [F_T]\) with \(\psi_T = \Phi_T\). Since \(\mathscr{F}_t (\psi_t)\) is a martingale and \(\psi\) has no drift, the drift in Itô's formula for \(\mathscr{F}_t (\psi_t)\) is the left-hand side of (8.8).\(\Box\)

Compare (8.8) with the Burgers equation (7.12): the transport term has been absorbed exactly by the change of coordinates, and only the Itô term is left. If it is dropped, \(\mathscr{F}_t = DV_T\) does not move: all the tree diagrams are contained in the coordinate change (8.5). The loops are generated only by the quadratic variation \(\mathcal{C}_t\) of the forecast, i.e. by the randomness of the noise and of the force revisions, and the nonlinearity has moved into the field dependence of \(\mathcal{C}_t\). None of this uses a gradient structure or a Gaussian convolution: Theorem 8.2 holds for any FBSDE of the form (7.8) with a Markov martingale force, gradient or not, for which the coordinate change is invertible. For a general terminal force \(F_T =\mathfrak{f} (\Phi_T)\), replace \(DV_T\) by \(-\mathfrak{f}\) in (8.7) and (8.8).

8.4Conditional moments

For the Polchinski process the response has a direct meaning.

Proposition 8.3. (formal) If \(F_t = - DV_t\) then

\(\displaystyle H_t (\psi_t) = \text{Cov}_t := \text{Cov}^{\mathbb{P}} (\Phi_T \hspace{0.17em} | \hspace{0.17em} \mathcal{F}_t) = S_t - S_t D^2 V_t (\Phi_t) S_t .\)

In particular \(H_t > 0\) is symmetric, \(\mathbb{1} + S_t DF_t = \text{Cov}_t S_t^{- 1}\) is always invertible, and \(H_t \leq S_t\) when \(V_T\) is convex.

Proof. By (7.5), the conditional law \(\mu_t^{\phi}\) is the tilt of \(\mu^{S_t} (\cdot - \phi)\) by \(\mathrm{e}^{- V_T}\); differentiating twice in \(\phi\) gives \(\text{Cov} (\mu_t^{\phi}) = S_t - S_t D^2 V_t (\phi) S_t\). On the other hand \(H_t S_t^{- 1} = \mathbb{1} + S_t DF_t = \mathbb{1} - S_t D^2 V_t\). The last claim is the Brascamp–Lieb inequality.\(\Box\)

So the forecast coordinates are the first two conditional moments of the stochastic localisation \(\mu_t^{\Phi_t}\) of Proposition 7.2: \((\psi_t, \text{Cov}_t) = (\mathbb{E}_t \Phi_T, \text{Cov}_t \Phi_T)\) (Figure 8.1). They are tied by Itô's formula: since \(\mathbb{E}_t [\Phi_T \otimes \Phi_T] = \psi_t \otimes \psi_t + \text{Cov}_t\) is a martingale,

\(\displaystyle \mathrm{d} \langle \psi, \psi \rangle_t =\mathcal{C}_t \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} t = \text{Cov}_t \dot{\mathscr{R}}_t \text{Cov}_t \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} t, \qquad \text{dCov}_t = - \text{Cov}_t \dot{\mathscr{R}}_t \text{Cov}_t \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} t + \mathrm{d} (\text{martingale}) .\) (8.9)

Conditional variance is resolved at the rate \(\text{Cov}_t \dot{\mathscr{R}}_t \text{Cov}_t\), which for the Gaussian reference is \(S_t \dot{\mathscr{R}}_t S_t = \dot{Q}_t\).

Brascamp–Lieb and Cramér–Rao bounds.
The conditional law \(\mu^{\phi}_t\) has density proportional to \(\mathrm{e}^{- W}\), with \(W (y) = V_T (y) + \frac{1}{2} \langle y - \phi, S_t^{- 1} (y - \phi) \rangle\) and \(D^2 W = S_t^{- 1} + D^2 V_T\). Two classical inequalities bound its covariance by the Hessian of \(W\). If \(D^2 W > 0\), the Brascamp–Lieb inequality [BL76], \(\operatorname{Var}_{\mu} (f) \leq \mathbb{E}_{\mu} \langle Df, (D^2 W)^{- 1} Df \rangle\), applied to linear functions \(f = \langle h, \cdot \rangle\), and without any convexity the Cramér–Rao inequality give

\(\displaystyle (\mathbb{E}_t [S_t^{- 1} + D^2 V_T (\Phi_T)])^{- 1} \leq \operatorname{Cov}_t \leq \mathbb{E}_t [(S_t^{- 1} + D^2 V_T (\Phi_T))^{- 1}] .\)

The lower bound follows from \(\mathbb{E}_{\mu} [(y - m) \otimes DW (y)] = 1\) (integration by parts) and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, with the Fisher information \(\mathbb{E}_{\mu} [DW \otimes DW] =\mathbb{E}_{\mu} D^2 W\) (Exercise 8.10). The two bounds coincide when \(V_T\) is quadratic, and their gap is a Jensen gap for the inverse. Through Proposition 8.3 they are two-sided bounds on the Hessian of the effective potential, i.e. on the response \(H_t\), which is the propagator of the Wetterich equation below. Some consequences:

  • If \(V_T\) is convex, then \(\operatorname{Cov}_t \leq S_t\): the interaction can only reduce the conditional variance. Equivalently \(D^2 V_t \geq 0\): convexity is preserved along the Polchinski flow (as also follows from the Prékopa–Leindler inequality), and \(D^2 \Gamma_t \geq 0\) in (8.10).

  • If \(D^2 V_T \geq \kappa\) with \(\kappa > 0\), then \(\operatorname{Cov}_t \leq (S_t^{- 1} + \kappa)^{- 1}\) uniformly in the conditioning: a uniform mass in the interaction is a uniform mass in every conditional law.

  • For \(\Phi^4_3\) the counterterm \(- \gamma_T \int : \varphi^2 :\) makes \(V_T\) non-convex, with a negative curvature that diverges as \(T \to \infty\). The upper bound is lost, and at low resolution the conditional law may spread over several wells (Figure 8.1). This is the large-field problem. In Lecture 5 it is handled by a clipped approximate action, whose global coercivity (Lemma 14.1 there) is a bound of Brascamp–Lieb type on the response.

8.5The gradient sector: Wetterich's equation

Phase space and Lagrangian graphs.
Pairs \((x, p)\) of a field and a force form the phase space \(\mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R}^n\), with the symplectic form \(\omega = \sum_i \mathrm{d} x_i \wedge \mathrm{d} p_i\). A submanifold \(L\) of dimension \(n\) is Lagrangian if \(\omega\) vanishes on it. The graph \(L = \{(x, F (x))\}\) of a force field is Lagrangian if and only if \(\sum_{i, j} \partial_j F_i \mathrm{d} x_i \wedge \mathrm{d} x_j = 0\), i.e. \(DF\) is symmetric, i.e. \(F\) is (locally) a gradient: the gradient sector is the sector of Lagrangian graphs. A linear map preserves \(\omega\) (it is symplectic) if and only if it maps Lagrangian submanifolds to Lagrangian submanifolds. The shear (8.1) is symplectic, since \(\Sigma_t^{\ast} \omega = \omega + \sum_{i, j} (S_t)_{ij} \mathrm{d} p_j \wedge \mathrm{d} p_i = \omega\) for \(S_t\) symmetric. So it maps the graph of a gradient to a Lagrangian submanifold; if the image is again a graph over the new base variable, it is the graph of a gradient. This is what we use now.

Now let \(F_t = - DV_t\). The graph \(\{(x, - DV_t (x))\}\) is a Lagrangian submanifold of the (field, force) phase space, and the shear (8.1) is symplectic because \(S_t\) is symmetric. Its image is again Lagrangian. It is a graph over \(\psi\): the map \(x \mapsto x + S_t F_t (x)\) is \(S_t\) times the gradient of \(\frac{1}{2} \langle x, S_t^{- 1} x \rangle - V_t (x)\), which is strictly convex since its Hessian is \(S_t^{- 1} \text{Cov}_t S_t^{- 1} > 0\) (Proposition 8.3), so the map is injective. Hence the image is the graph of a gradient:

\(\displaystyle \mathscr{F}_t = D \Gamma_t, \qquad H_t = (S_t^{- 1} + D^2 \Gamma_t)^{- 1}, \qquad \Gamma_T = V_T .\) (8.10)

The generating function of this canonical transformation is the Moreau (infimal convolution) transform

\(\displaystyle V_t (x) = \inf_{\psi} \left\{ \Gamma_t (\psi) + \tfrac{1}{2} \langle x - \psi, S_t^{- 1} (x - \psi) \rangle \right\},\) (8.11)

whose optimality conditions \(x = \psi + S_t D \Gamma_t (\psi)\), \(DV_t (x) = D \Gamma_t (\psi)\) are (8.5). It is well defined by the same convexity: up to the change of variable \(j = S_t^{- 1} x\), it is a Legendre transform. We use (8.11) to fix the additive constant in \(\Gamma_t\). Theorem 8.4. (Wetterich equation) (formal) \(\Gamma_t\) solves

\(\displaystyle \partial_t \Gamma_t = \tfrac{1}{2} \text{Tr} [\dot{\mathscr{R}}_t ((S_t^{- 1} + D^2 \Gamma_t)^{- 1} - S_t)], \qquad \Gamma_T = V_T .\) (8.12)

Equivalently, the effective average action \(\Gamma^{\text{EAA}}_t (\psi) := \Gamma_t (\psi) + \frac{1}{2} \langle \psi, Q_T^{- 1} \psi \rangle\) solves, up to a field-independent term,

\(\displaystyle \partial_t \Gamma^{\text{EAA}}_t = \tfrac{1}{2} \text{Tr} [\dot{\mathscr{R}}_t (D^2 \Gamma^{\text{EAA}}_t + \mathscr{R}_t)^{- 1}] .\) (8.13)

It interpolates between the bare action \(\Gamma^{\text{EAA}}_T = \frac{1}{2} \langle \psi, Q_T^{- 1} \psi \rangle + V_T (\psi)\) (\(\mathscr{R}_T = \infty\)) and the 1PI effective action \(\Gamma^{\text{EAA}}_0\) of \(\nu_T\) (\(\mathscr{R}_0 = 0\)).

Proof. Integrate (8.8) in field space. With \(\mathscr{F} = D \Gamma\), the derivative of \(H = (S^{- 1} + D^2 \Gamma)^{- 1}\) in a direction \(v\) is \(- H \hspace{0.17em} D^3 \Gamma [v] \hspace{0.17em} H\), so by the total symmetry of \(D^3 \Gamma\),

\(\displaystyle D_v \text{Tr} (\dot{\mathscr{R}}_t H_t) = - \text{Tr} \left( H_t \dot{\mathscr{R}}_t H_t \hspace{0.17em} D^3 \Gamma_t [v] \right) = - \text{Tr} \left( \mathcal{C}_t \hspace{0.17em} D^2 \mathscr{F}_t \right) [v] .\)

Hence (8.8) reads \(D \left( \partial_t \Gamma_t - \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} (\dot{\mathscr{R}}_t H_t) \right) = 0\), and (8.12) holds up to a function of \(t\) alone. That this function is \(- \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} (\dot{\mathscr{R}}_t S_t)\) with the normalisation (8.11) follows from the Polchinski equation by the envelope theorem (Exercise 8.5). For (8.13), note that \(D^2 \Gamma^{\text{EAA}}_t + \mathscr{R}_t = D^2 \Gamma_t + S_t^{- 1}\). For the endpoints: \(S_T = 0\); and at \(t = 0\), \(S_0 = Q_T\), so (8.11) identifies \(\frac{1}{2} \langle x, Q_T^{- 1} x \rangle - V_0 (x)\), up to a constant, with the cumulant generating function of \(\nu_T\) at \(Q_T^{- 1} x\), whose Legendre transform is \(\Gamma^{\text{EAA}}_0\) (Exercise 8.7).\(\Box\)

Thus the Wetterich equation is the gradient-integrated form of the force equation (8.8), i.e. of the backward half of the FBSDE in forecast coordinates [Mor94, BCG26]. Every object in it is an observable of the Polchinski process:

Wetterich FBSDE
field argument \(\psi\) forecast \(\psi_t =\mathbb{E}_t [\Phi_T]\), the state transported by \(\Sigma_t\)
\(D \Gamma_t (\psi_t)\) minus the costate, \(- F_t =\mathbb{E}_t [DV_T (\Phi_T)]\)
propagator \((D^2 \Gamma^{\text{EAA}}_t + \mathscr{R}_t)^{- 1}\) conditional covariance \(\text{Cov}_t\)
regulator \(\mathscr{R}_t = S_t^{- 1} - Q_T^{- 1}\) precision of the unresolved shell, minus that of the prior
\(\partial_t \Gamma_t\) \(\frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} [\dot{\mathscr{R}}_t (\text{Cov}_t - S_t)]\)

The flow of \(\Gamma\) is driven by the difference between the interacting and the free conditional covariances, paired with the shell \(\dot{\mathscr{R}}_t\). It is one-loop exact, with the full propagator inside the trace. In the Gaussian case \(D^2 \Gamma_t\) is constant and only the constant term of \(\Gamma\) flows. For the heat profile with \(T = \infty\), \(S_t = C (1 - \mathrm{e}^{- A / t^2})\) and

\(\displaystyle \mathscr{R}_t = \frac{A}{\mathrm{e}^{A / t^2} - 1},\) (8.14)

which behaves like a mass term \(t^2\) on the modes \(|k| \ll t\) (for \(m \ll t\)) and is exponentially small on the modes \(|k| \gg t\): the exponential regulator of the functional renormalisation group (Figure 8.3).

8.6Variational meaning and the tree level

Constrained free energies.
(formal) By the Gibbs variational principle applied to the conditional law (7.5), and the identity \(\text{Ent} (\nu | \mu^S (\cdot - x)) = \text{Ent} (\nu | \mu^S (\cdot - \psi)) + \frac{1}{2} \langle x - \psi, S^{- 1} (x - \psi) \rangle\) for \(\nu\) of mean \(\psi\),

\(\displaystyle \Gamma_t (\psi) = \inf \left\{ \int V_T \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} \nu + \text{Ent} \left( \nu \hspace{0.17em} | \hspace{0.17em} \mu^{S_t} (\cdot - \psi) \right) : \left. \int \varphi \hspace{0.17em} \nu (\mathrm{d} \varphi) = \psi \right\} . \right.\) (8.15)

So \(\Gamma_t\), the effective average action up to the quadratic term \(\frac{1}{2} \langle \psi, Q_T^{- 1} \psi \rangle\), is the free energy of the unresolved shell constrained to the forecast \(\psi\), and (8.11) is the minimisation over the forecast. In the language of Theorem 9.1: \(V_t\) is the value function of the control problem, and \(\Gamma_t\) is its Moreau transform, i.e. the value function expressed in the forecast coordinate, the interaction picture of the classical part of the dynamics.

The tree level.
Dropping the Itô term in (8.8) gives \(\Gamma_t = V_T\), and then (8.11) becomes the Hopf–Lax formula

\(\displaystyle V^{\text{tree}}_t (x) = \inf_y \left\{ V_T (y) + \tfrac{1}{2} \langle y - x, S_t^{- 1} (y - x) \rangle \right\},\) (8.16)

the solution of the noiseless Polchinski equation \(\partial_t V = \frac{1}{2} \langle DV, \dot{Q} DV \rangle\). It is the saddle point of the Gaussian convolution (7.3). Its minimiser solves the classical field equation \(S_t^{- 1} (y - x) + DV_T (y) = 0\) in the shell \([t, T]\) with background \(x\), and expanding it in \(V_T\) produces exactly the tree diagrams.

8.7Log-Sobolev inequalities along the localisation

Bauerschmidt and Bodineau [BB19, BBD24] proved log-Sobolev inequalities by running the Polchinski flow. In their language the renormalised measure at a given scale is the law of \(\Phi_t\), and the fluctuation measure is the conditional law \(\mu_t^{\Phi_t}\) of Proposition 7.2, i.e. the stochastic localisation of \(\nu_T\) [Eld23]. (Their scale parameter runs from the full measure to the Gaussian one, the opposite of our resolution: their integrated covariance is our \(S_t\).) In this dictionary their argument is an entropy computation along our filtration.

Entropy as quadratic variation.
Let \(F \geq 0\) with \(\mathbb{E}_{\nu_T} F = 1\), and let \(M_t =\mathbb{E}_t [F (\Phi_T)] = G_t (\Phi_t)\), with \(G_t (\phi) = \int F \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} \mu_t^{\phi}\). The martingale \(M\) runs from \(M_0 = 1\) to \(M_T = F (\Phi_T)\), and its noise is that of the field: \(\mathrm{d} M_t = \langle DG_t (\Phi_t), \dot{Q}_t^{1 / 2} \mathrm{d} W_t \rangle\). Itô's formula for \(M \log M\) gives

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{Ent}_{\nu_T} (F) = \frac{1}{2} \mathbb{E} \int_0^T \frac{\mathrm{d} \langle M \rangle_t}{M_t} = 2\mathbb{E} \int_0^T \left\| \dot{Q}_t^{1 / 2} D \sqrt{G_t} (\Phi_t) \right\|^2 \mathrm{d} t,\)

and, stopping at resolution \(t\), the chain rule

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{Ent}_{\nu_T} (F) =\operatorname{Ent} (G_t (\Phi_t)) +\mathbb{E} \left[ \operatorname{Ent}_{\mu_t^{\Phi_t}} (F) \right] :\)

the entropy already revealed at resolution \(t\) plus the expected entropy still hidden. The increments are controlled by the conditional law: differentiating the density of \(\mu_t^{\phi}\) in \(\phi\), \(DG_t (\phi) = S_t^{- 1} \operatorname{Cov}_{\mu_t^{\phi}} (F, y)\).

Theorem 8.5. (Multiscale Bakry–Émery [BBD24]) (formal in infinite dimensions) Suppose that there are real numbers \(\kappa_t\) such that, for all \(t \in [0, T]\) and all \(\phi\), as quadratic forms,

\(\displaystyle \dot{Q}_t D^2 V_t (\phi) \dot{Q}_t + \frac{1}{2} \ddot{Q}_t \geq \kappa_t \dot{Q}_t .\)

Then \(\nu_T\) satisfies the log-Sobolev inequality

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{Ent}_{\nu_T} (F) \leq \frac{2}{\gamma} \mathbb{E}_{\nu_T} \left[ \left\| \dot{Q}_T^{1 / 2} D \sqrt{F} \right\|^2 \right], \qquad \frac{1}{\gamma} = \int_0^T \exp \left( - 2 \int_t^T \kappa_r \mathrm{d} r \right) \mathrm{d} t.\)

The hypothesis gives, at each resolution, the gradient bound \(\| \dot{Q}_t^{1 / 2} D \sqrt{G_t} \|^2 \leq \mathrm{e}^{- 2 \int_t^T \kappa} \int \| \dot{Q}_T^{1 / 2} D \sqrt{F} \|^2 \mathrm{d} \mu_t^{\phi}\); inserted in the entropy formula it gives the theorem [BBD24]. For \(V_T = 0\) and a linear profile \(Q_t = tQ_T / T\) one may take \(\kappa = 0\), so \(1 / \gamma = T\) and the theorem is the Gaussian log-Sobolev inequality \(\operatorname{Ent} (F) \leq 2\mathbb{E} \langle D \sqrt{F}, Q_T D \sqrt{F} \rangle\). The hypothesis is a convexity condition on the effective potentials at every scale, not on \(V_T\), corrected by the scale dependence \(\ddot{Q}_t\) of the decomposition.

In terms of the conditional covariance.
By Proposition 8.3, \(D^2 V_t = S_t^{- 1} - S_t^{- 1} \operatorname{Cov}_t S_t^{- 1}\), and \(S_t^{- 1} \dot{Q}_t S_t^{- 1} = \dot{R}_t\) is the derivative of the regulator (8.6). So the hypothesis is an upper bound on the conditional covariance, measured in the metric \(\dot{R}_t\): it bounds the same quantity \(\operatorname{Cov}_t \dot{R}_t \operatorname{Cov}_t\) which, by (8.9), is the rate at which the conditional variance is resolved. In this form [BBD24], if \(\dot{R}_r \operatorname{Cov}_r \dot{R}_r \leq \alpha_r \dot{R}_r\) for \(r \leq t\), then

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{Ent}_{\nu_T} (F) \leq \exp \left( \int_0^t \alpha_r \mathrm{d} r \right) \mathbb{E} \left[ \operatorname{Ent}_{\mu_t^{\Phi_t}} (F) \right] :\)

the total entropy is controlled by the entropy that is still hidden at resolution \(t\). This is the entropic stability of the stochastic localisation literature: the low-resolution part of the localisation is where the measure may be multimodal, and the condition says that it does not hide too much. With the Brascamp–Lieb bounds of Section 8.4 the conditional covariance is controlled by the Hessian of \(V_T\) when this is convex; for \(\Phi^4_3\) it is not, and the condition is a statement about the effective potentials, i.e. about the renormalisation group flow itself.

9Variational structure

9.1Finite volume: the Boué–Dupuis formula

Let \(\mathbb{H}_a\) be the set of progressively measurable drifts \(u : [0, T] \to L^2 (\mathcal{M})\) with \(\mathbb{E} \int_0^T \|u_s \|^2 \mathrm{d} s < \infty\), and set

\(\displaystyle I_t (u) := \int_0^t \dot{Q}_s^{1 / 2} u_s \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s, \qquad X^u := X + I (u),\)

the field driven by the control \(u\) under the Gaussian reference \(\mathbb{P}_0\).

Theorem 9.1. (Boué–Dupuis [BD98]; Polchinski as HJB) (formal)

\(\displaystyle - \log \mathbb{E}_0 [\mathrm{e}^{- V_T (X_T)}] = V_0 (0) = \inf_{u \in \mathbb{H}_a} \mathbb{E}_0 \left[ V_T (X^u_T) + \frac{1}{2} \int_0^T \|u_s \|^2 \mathrm{d} s \right] .\) (9.1)

The infimum is attained at the feedback control \(u^{\ast}_s = - \dot{Q}_s^{1 / 2} DV_s (X^{u^{\ast}}_s)\), and \(\text{Law} (X^{u^{\ast}}_T) = \nu_T\). The effective potential is the value function, and (7.6) is the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation of this control problem.

Proof. (Verification.) For any \(u\), by Itô's formula and (7.6),

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{rl} \mathrm{d} V_t (X^u_t) & = \left[ \tfrac{1}{2} \| \dot{Q}_t^{1 / 2} DV_t \|^2 + \langle \dot{Q}_t^{1 / 2} DV_t, u_t \rangle \right] \mathrm{d} t + \mathrm{d} M_t\\ & = \left[ \tfrac{1}{2} \|u_t + \dot{Q}_t^{1 / 2} DV_t \|^2 - \tfrac{1}{2} \|u_t \|^2 \right] \mathrm{d} t + \mathrm{d} M_t, \end{array}\)

with \(DV_t = DV_t (X^u_t)\) and a martingale \(M\). Integrating and taking expectations,

\(\displaystyle \mathbb{E}_0 \left[ V_T (X^u_T) + \frac{1}{2} \int_0^T \|u_t \|^2 \mathrm{d} t \right] = V_0 (0) + \frac{1}{2} \mathbb{E}_0 \int_0^T \|u_t + \dot{Q}_t^{1 / 2} DV_t (X^u_t)\|^2 \mathrm{d} t.\) (9.2)

So the cost is \(\geq V_0 (0)\), with equality iff \(u = u^{\ast}\). With this control \(X^{u^{\ast}}\) solves the same SDE as \(\Phi\) in (7.7).\(\Box\)

Euler–Lagrange equation \(=\) FBSDE.
Perturbing \(u \to u + \varepsilon v\) in (9.1), the first-order condition is \(\mathbb{E} \int_0^T \langle u_s + \dot{Q}_s^{1 / 2} \mathbb{E}_s [DV_T (X^u_T)], v_s \rangle \mathrm{d} s = 0\) for all adapted \(v\), i.e.

\(\displaystyle u_s = - \dot{Q}_s^{1 / 2} \hspace{0.17em} \mathbb{E}_s [DV_T (X^u_T)] = \dot{Q}_s^{1 / 2} F_s .\) (9.3)

This is (7.8). The FBSDE is the Euler–Lagrange equation of the variational problem, and the Polchinski equation is its Hamilton–Jacobi equation. These are the three faces of the same object: the PDE (Polchinski), the stochastic dynamics (FBSDE), and the variational principle (Boué–Dupuis).

Entropy.
By Girsanov, \(\frac{1}{2} \mathbb{E}_0 \int \|u\|^2 \geq \text{Ent} (\text{Law} (X^u) \hspace{0.17em} | \hspace{0.17em} \text{Law} (X))\), which is at least the entropy of the terminal marginal. So (9.1) is a pathwise lift of the Gibbs variational principle \(- \log Z_T = \inf_{\nu} \{ \int V_T \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} \nu + \text{Ent} (\nu | \mu^{Q_T})\}\). The lift is useful because adapted drifts can be constructed scale by scale: one looks for \(u\) as a functional of the Gaussian process \(X\), not as an unknown measure.

Generating functionals.
Adding a test functional \(f\),

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{W}_T (f) := - \log \int \mathrm{e}^{- f} \mathrm{d} \nu_T = \inf_u \mathbb{E}_0 \left[ f (X^u_T) + V_T (X^u_T) + \frac{1}{2} \int_0^T \|u_s \|^2 \mathrm{d} s \right] - V_0 (0) .\) (9.4)

Bounds on \(\mathcal{W}_T (f)\) uniform in \(T\), for \(f\) in a rich class, give tightness, moment bounds and the identification of limits. For \(\Phi^4_3\) the naive problem does not have uniform bounds: the leading-order optimal drift \(u_s \approx - 4 \lambda \dot{Q}_s^{1 / 2} : X_s^3\): has a divergent cost. One renormalises by the change of variables \(u = - 4 \lambda \dot{Q}^{1 / 2} : X^3 : + r\). After it, and after further paracontrolled changes of variables, the singular terms are explicit polynomials in the Wick powers of \(X\), cancelled by the counterterms, and the residual functional is coercive. Its control in Besov and paracontrolled norms, uniformly in \(T\), is the construction of \(\Phi^4_3\) in [BG20]; see also [BG21b].

9.2Infinite volume

On \(\mathbb{T}^d_L\) the free energy \(V_0 (0)\) and the effective potential \(V_t\) are extensive: they grow like \(L^d\), and (9.1) has no limit as \(L \to \infty\). Three objects are intensive and survive formally:

  1. the force. For a local interaction \(V_T (\varphi) = \int v_T (\varphi (x)) \mathrm{d} x\), \(DV_T (\varphi) (x) = v_T' (\varphi (x))\), and (7.8) reads, on \(\mathbb{R}^d\),

    \(\displaystyle \Phi_t = \int_0^t \dot{Q}_s F_s \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s + \int_0^t \dot{Q}_s^{1 / 2} \mathrm{d} W_s, \qquad F_t (x) = -\mathbb{E}_t [v_T' (\Phi_T (x))] .\) (9.5)

    Here every quantity is local, and the equation makes sense without a volume cutoff. Infinite-volume limits of \(\nu_T^{(L)}\) are expected to be laws of solutions of (9.5). Uniqueness of such solutions (e.g. for small coupling, or by contraction in weighted spaces) is a uniqueness statement for the infinite-volume theory. Non-uniqueness is expected in the presence of phase transitions, e.g. for \(\Phi^4_2\) with a deep double-well potential [GJS75];

  2. the Burgers flow (7.12) of the force, for the same reason;

  3. differences of free energies. For a local \(f\), \(\mathcal{W}_T^{(L)} (f)\) in (9.4) is a difference of two extensive infima, and it stays bounded as \(L \to \infty\). Its variational characterisation, through the infinite-volume Euler–Lagrange equation (9.5), is the basis of the infinite-volume analysis of \(\Phi^4_2\) and \(\exp (\Phi)_2\) in [BG24].

Differences of free energies.
Fix the volume, and let \(u^{\ast}\) be the optimal drift of (9.1), so that \(X^{u^{\ast}} = \Phi\) is the Polchinski process (Theorem 9.1). In (9.4) the infimum may be taken over perturbations \(u = u^{\ast} + w\) of the optimal drift. The drift enters linearly, so \(X^{u^{\ast} + w} = \Phi + I (w)\) with \(I_t (w) = \int_0^t \dot{Q}_s^{1 / 2} w_s \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} s\), and by the Euler–Lagrange equation (9.3) the cross term of the energy is \(\mathbb{E} \int_0^T \langle u^{\ast}_s, w_s \rangle \mathrm{d} s = -\mathbb{E} \langle DV_T (\Phi_T), I_T (w) \rangle\). The two extensive infima then cancel exactly, and what is left is a variational problem for the perturbation alone.

Proposition 9.2. (Variational principle relative to the FBSDE) (formal) Let \(\Phi\) be the Polchinski process, i.e. the solution of the FBSDE (7.8) driven by the Brownian motion \(W\), and \(\nu_T\) the law of \(\Phi_T\). For every \(f\) bounded from below,

\(\displaystyle - \log \int \mathrm{e}^{- f} \mathrm{d} \nu_T = \inf_w \mathbb{E} \left[ f (\Phi_T + I_T (w)) + B_{V_T} (\Phi_T, I_T (w)) + \frac{1}{2} \int_0^T \|w_s \|^2 \mathrm{d} s \right],\) (9.6)

where the infimum runs over adapted \(w\) with \(\mathbb{E} \int_0^T \|w_s \|^2 \mathrm{d} s < \infty\), and

\(\displaystyle B_V (\phi, h) := V (\phi + h) - V (\phi) - \langle DV (\phi), h \rangle\)

is the Bregman divergence of \(V\). The minimiser satisfies \(w_s = - \dot{Q}_s^{1 / 2} \mathbb{E}_s [Df (\Phi^f_T) + DV_T (\Phi^f_T) - DV_T (\Phi_T)]\), with \(\Phi^f = \Phi + I (w)\): the perturbed field solves the FBSDE with terminal potential \(V_T + f\), and its force is that of \(\Phi\) corrected by \(F^f_s - F_s = -\mathbb{E}_s [Df (\Phi^f_T) + DV_T (\Phi^f_T) - DV_T (\Phi_T)]\).

Proof. By (9.4), the left-hand side is \(\inf_u J_f (u) - J_0 (u^{\ast})\), with \(J_f (u) =\mathbb{E} [f (X^u_T) + V_T (X^u_T) + \frac{1}{2} \int \|u\|^2]\). Writing \(u = u^{\ast} + w\) and expanding \(\frac{1}{2} \|u^{\ast} + w\|^2\), the terms \(V_T (\Phi_T)\) and \(\frac{1}{2} \int \|u^{\ast} \|^2\) cancel against \(J_0 (u^{\ast})\), and the cross term is computed above. The first-order condition in \(w\) is obtained as in (9.3).\(\Box\)

For a local interaction \(V_T (\varphi) = \int v_T (\varphi (x)) \mathrm{d} x\),

\(\displaystyle B_{V_T} (\phi, h) = \int b_T (\phi (x), h (x)) \mathrm{d} x, \qquad b_T (a, h) = v_T (a + h) - v_T (a) - v_T' (a) h,\)

and \(b_T (a, 0) = 0\). So every term of (9.6) is local: there is no free energy and no extensive quantity, only the field \(\Phi\) and the perturbation that \(f\) induces on it. For \(f\) supported in a bounded region one expects the optimal \(w\), and with it \(I (w)\), to decay away from the support, at the rate of the decay of correlations of the measure. Both sides of (9.6) then make sense in infinite volume, with \(\Phi\) a solution of the infinite-volume FBSDE (9.5). This suggests the following formal description of the infinite-volume measures (at a fixed ultraviolet cutoff \(T\)):

an infinite-volume measure is the law of \(\Phi_T\) for a solution \(\Phi\) of (9.5) which satisfies (9.6), with \(B = \int b_T \mathrm{d} x\), for all local \(f\).

The Laplace functionals in (9.6) determine the measure, and the condition involves only intensive quantities. When the FBSDE has several solutions, as is expected in the presence of phase transitions, each solution gives a measure with its own variational principle; the perturbations \(w\) of finite energy only probe a given solution locally. For \(\Phi^4_3\) the Bregman term contains the divergent counterterms, and, as for (9.4), the change of variables of [BG20] is needed before (9.6) has bounds uniform in \(T\); in two dimensions, compare the infinite-volume analysis of [BG24].

The coercive estimates must then be local: one uses weighted norms and needs bounds whose constants do not depend on the volume. This infrared problem is the analogue, for the scale dynamics, of the “coming down from infinity” estimates for the parabolic dynamics.

10Algebraic structure of the Polchinski flow

10.1A semigroup of renormalisation maps

For a positive covariance \(S\) define the renormalisation map

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{R}_S (V) := - \log (\mu^S \ast \mathrm{e}^{- V}), \qquad (\mu^S \ast u) (\phi) = \int u (\phi + \zeta) \mu^S (\mathrm{d} \zeta) .\) (10.1)

Since \(\mu^S \ast \mu^{S'} = \mu^{S + S'}\),

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{R}_S \circ \mathcal{R}_{S'} =\mathcal{R}_{S + S'} =\mathcal{R}_{S'} \circ \mathcal{R}_S, \qquad V_t =\mathcal{R}_{Q_{T, t}} (V_T) .\) (10.2)

So the effective potentials are an orbit of an abelian semigroup, indexed by the cone of positive covariances, acting nonlinearly on interactions and linearly on Boltzmann factors \(\mathrm{e}^{- V}\). Its infinitesimal generators are the vector fields

\(\displaystyle \mathcal{P}_B (V) := \tfrac{1}{2} \text{Tr} (BD^2 V) - \tfrac{1}{2} \langle DV, BDV \rangle, \qquad \left. \tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} s} \mathcal{R}_{sB} (V) \right|_{s = 0} =\mathcal{P}_B (V),\) (10.3)

and (7.6) reads \(\partial_t V_t = -\mathcal{P}_{\dot{Q}_t} (V_t)\).

Proposition 10.1. (Flatness) (formal) (a) \([\mathcal{P}_B, \mathcal{P}_{B'}] = 0\) for all positive \(B, B'\). (b) \(V_t\) depends on the profile only through \(Q_{T, t}\). In particular the free energy \(V_0 (0)\) and the law \(\nu_T\) depend only on \(Q_T\), not on the path \(t \mapsto Q_t\).

Proof. (a) Differentiate \(\mathcal{R}_{sB} \mathcal{R}_{rB'} =\mathcal{R}_{rB'} \mathcal{R}_{sB}\) at \(s = r = 0\), or compute directly (Exercise 10.1). (b) is (10.2).\(\Box\)

Different profiles with the same endpoints are different coordinate systems on the same renormalisation group trajectory. They change the process \(\Phi\) and the observables at intermediate resolution, but not the terminal theory. The flow has no holonomy [BCG26].

10.2Perturbation theory and counterterms

Expanding \(V_t =\mathcal{R}_{Q_{T, t}} (V_T)\) in cumulants of the Gaussian increment \(\zeta \sim \mu^{Q_{T, t}}\) gives

\(\displaystyle V_t (\phi) =\mathbb{E}_{\zeta} [V_T (\phi + \zeta)] - \tfrac{1}{2} \text{Var}_{\zeta} (V_T (\phi + \zeta)) + \ldots\) (10.4)

For (7.1), Exercise 6.1 of Lecture 3 shows that Gaussian convolution simply lowers the Wick ordering: since \(Q_{T, t} (x, x) = c_T - c_t\),

\(\displaystyle V_t (\phi) = \int [\lambda H_4 (\phi (x) ; c_t) + \gamma_T H_2 (\phi (x) ; c_t)] \mathrm{d} x + O (\lambda^2) .\) (10.5)

Here \(\gamma_T = O (\lambda^2)\) is kept for later use. At first order the effective potential is the Wick-ordered potential at the current resolution. This is the dual statement of Proposition 6.1 of Lecture 3: \(\int H_4 (X_t ; c_t)\) is an observable.

Proposition 10.2. (Second order) (formal) Write \(S = Q_{T, t}\) and \(: \phi^p :_t = H_p (\phi ; c_t)\). Then

\(\displaystyle V_t (\phi) = \int [\lambda : \phi^4 :_t + \gamma_T : \phi^2 :_t] - \frac{\lambda^2}{2} \sum_{k = 1}^4 \binom{4}{k}^2 k! \iint S (x, y)^k \hspace{0.17em} : \phi^{4 - k} :_t (x) \hspace{0.17em} : \phi^{4 - k} :_t (y) \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} x \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} y + O (\lambda^3) .\) (10.6)

Proof. By the binomial formula for Hermite polynomials,

\(\displaystyle H_4 (\phi + \zeta ; c_t + s) = \sum_{k = 0}^4 \binom{4}{k} H_k (\zeta ; s) H_{4 - k} (\phi ; c_t), \qquad s = S (x, x) .\)

Then compute the variance in (10.4) with Lemma 3.8 of Lecture 2.\(\Box\)

Now let \(T \to \infty\) at fixed \(t\), so that \(S \to C - Q_t\), which near the diagonal is as singular as \(G\). In \(d = 2\) every \(S^k\) is locally integrable, so with \(\gamma_T = 0\) every term has a limit: Wick ordering suffices. In \(d = 3\), \(G (z) \sim (4 \pi |z|)^{- 1}\):

  • \(k = 1, 2\): \(S\) and \(S^2\) are locally integrable. These terms are finite and nonlocal.

  • \(k = 3\) (the sunset): \(S^3 \sim |z|^{- 3}\) is not integrable. Writing \(\phi (x) \phi (y) = \frac{1}{2} (\phi (x)^2 + \phi (y)^2) - \frac{1}{2} (\phi (x) - \phi (y))^2\), up to a field-independent constant the divergent part is local: \(- 48 \lambda^2 \left( \int S^3 \right) \int : \phi^2 :_t +\)(finite), with \(\int S^3 \sim \frac{1}{16 \pi^2} \log T\). It is cancelled by

    \(\displaystyle \gamma_T := 48 \lambda^2 \int_{\mathbb{T}^3_L} Q_T (z)^3 \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} z = \frac{3 \lambda^2}{\pi^2} \log T + O (1),\) (10.7)

    since \(\gamma_T - 48 \lambda^2 \int S^3\) converges (Exercise 10.2).

  • \(k = 4\): a divergent constant \(- 12 \lambda^2 L^3 \int S^4 \sim T\), the vacuum energy. It cancels in \(\nu_T\) but not in the free energy.

Power counting (Exercise 10.3) shows that no further field-dependent counterterms are needed in \(d = 3\): \(\Phi^4_3\) is superrenormalisable. Thus renormalisation appears as a property of the flow: one chooses the terminal data \(V_T\) so that the effective potentials \(V_t\) at fixed resolution have limits. The divergences at second order are also related to the singularity of \(\Phi^4_3\) with respect to the GFF [BG21b]: the measures \(\nu_t\) at fixed \(t\) are expected to stay absolutely continuous with respect to \(\mu^{Q_t}\), while the terminal one is not.

11Dyson–Schwinger equations at every scale

Assume now that all the operators involved are functions of \(A\), hence commute, and let

\(\displaystyle K := Q_T^{- 1}, \qquad J_t := KQ_t, \qquad J_0 = 0, \quad J_T = \mathbb{1} .\)

For the heat profile, \(K = A \mathrm{e}^{A / T^2}\) and \(J_t = \mathrm{e}^{- A (t^{- 2} - T^{- 2})}\) is a heat semigroup, which tends to \(\mathrm{e}^{- A / t^2}\) as \(T \to \infty\). \(F_t\) is the force observable of Theorem 7.4.

Theorem 11.1. (DS equations at resolution \(t\)) (formal) The noise observable

\(\displaystyle \eta_t := K \Phi_t - J_t F_t\) (11.1)

is a centred \(\mathbb{P}\)-martingale, with \(\mathrm{d} \eta_t = K \dot{Q}_t^{1 / 2} \mathrm{d} W^{\mathbb{P}}_t - J_t \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} F_t\). For every \(t \leq T\), every \(h\) and every functional \(G\),

\(\displaystyle \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}} [D_{J_t h} G (\Phi_t)] =\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}} \left[ G (\Phi_t) \hspace{0.17em} \langle h, \eta_t \rangle \right] =\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}} \left[ G (\Phi_t) \hspace{0.17em} \langle h, K \Phi_t - J_t F_t \rangle \right] .\) (11.2)

At \(t = T\) this is the Dyson–Schwinger equation of \(\nu_T\),

\(\displaystyle \mathbb{E}^{\nu_T} [D_h G] =\mathbb{E}^{\nu_T} \left[ G \hspace{0.17em} \langle h, K \varphi + DV_T (\varphi) \rangle \right] .\) (11.3)

Proof. From (7.7) and \(\dot{J}_t = K \dot{Q}_t\), \(\mathrm{d} \eta_t = K (\dot{Q}_t F_t \mathrm{d} t + \dot{Q}_t^{1 / 2} \mathrm{d} W^{\mathbb{P}}_t) - K \dot{Q}_t F_t \mathrm{d} t - J_t \mathrm{d} F_t\). The drift cancels, \(F\) is a martingale and \(\eta_0 = 0\). For (11.2) use Proposition 7.2: \(\Phi_t \sim \nu_t \propto \mathrm{e}^{- V_t} \mu^{Q_t}\). Gaussian integration by parts for \(\mu^{Q_t}\) in the direction \(Q_t k\) gives \(\mathbb{E}^{\nu_t} [D_{Q_t k} G] =\mathbb{E}^{\nu_t} [G (\langle k, \varphi \rangle + \langle Q_t k, DV_t (\varphi) \rangle)]\). Take \(k = Kh\), so that \(Q_t k = J_t h\), and recall \(F_t = - DV_t (\Phi_t)\).\(\Box\)

Formally letting \(T \to \infty\), \(K \to A\), and (11.1) becomes an exact identity between observables at every resolution:

\(\displaystyle A \Phi_t = AQ_t \hspace{0.17em} F_t + \eta_t,\) (11.4)

with \(AQ_t = \mathrm{e}^{- A / t^2}\) for the heat profile. This is the Euclidean field equation \(A \varphi = - V' (\varphi) +\)noise of Lectures 1–2, seen at resolution \(t\). It needs no products of distributions: the nonlinearity enters only through the force observable \(F_t\), smeared at scale \(1 / t\). For the GFF (\(F = 0\)), (11.2) is Gaussian integration by parts for \(\mu^{Q_t}\). For \(\Phi^4_3\), where \(F_t\) plays the role of the renormalised cube, the limit \(t \to \infty\) of (11.4) corresponds to the renormalised DS equation of [GH21].

Remark 11.2. (Comparison with stochastic quantisation) In parabolic or elliptic stochastic quantisation (Lecture 3, §4) the field equation is imposed in an auxiliary time, and the field at fixed time has the right law. Here the field equation (11.4) holds at each resolution, and the auxiliary parameter is the resolution itself. Taking conditional expectations of an SPDE solution with respect to a scale filtration gives identities of the same shape, but with a different noise covariance: the martingale \(J_t \hspace{0.17em} \mathrm{d} F_t\) in \(\mathrm{d} \eta_t\) is essential [BCG26].
